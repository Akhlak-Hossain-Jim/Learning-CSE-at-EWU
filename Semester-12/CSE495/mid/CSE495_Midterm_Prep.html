Topic 01

Agile, Scrum & XP

This is the Q1 territory: a team "trying to be Agile" badly, and you have to name what they're doing, say what's wrong, and fix it.

Imagine you're building a house but the client keeps changing their mind — move the kitchen, add a room, swap the roof — all while the walls are going up. A rigid "draw the full blueprint once, then build for two years and hand over" plan (the classic Waterfall approach) collapses under that kind of change, because by the time you finish, the plan is wrong. Agile is the response: instead of one huge delivery at the end, you build the software in small pieces, show each piece to the customer quickly, and let their feedback steer the next piece. Change stops being a disaster and becomes the normal input.

Key idea

Agile = rapid, incremental delivery + welcoming change + the customer working alongside the team. You trade "big plan up front" for "short cycles and constant course-correction."

Agile is a philosophy, not a single recipe. The two recipes your faculty taught are Scrum and Extreme Programming (XP). A rough way to hold them apart: Scrum manages the process (who does what, in what rhythm), while XP manages the code (engineering habits that keep quality high). Real teams often use both at once.

Scrum — the management rhythm

Scrum runs the project in fixed-length cycles called Sprints (1–4 weeks). Each Sprint takes a slice of work and turns it into something usably finished. It has exactly three roles, three artifacts, and five events — learn these as a set, because the exam scenario is basically a list of these pieces done wrong.

The three roles

  • Product Owner (PO): owns the what. Keeps the Product Backlog, prioritises it for business value, and speaks for the customer. Exactly one per product.
  • Scrum Master (SM): a servant-leader / coach. Makes sure Scrum is followed, removes impediments, and shields the team from outside pressure. Explicitly not a traditional boss handing out tasks.
  • Development Team: 5–9 cross-functional, self-organising people who actually build the increment. No fixed job titles inside the team.

The three artifacts

  • Product Backlog: the master, prioritised list of everything that might be built. Owned by the PO, continuously refined.
  • Sprint Backlog: the items pulled from the Product Backlog for this Sprint, plus the tasks to finish them. Owned and updated daily by the team.
  • Increment / Definition of Done (DoD): the tested, documented, potentially shippable result. "Done" means done — nothing left to test or fix later.

The five events

  • Sprint — the container: a fixed 1–4 week iteration producing a usable increment.
  • Sprint Planning — decide what to deliver and how.
  • Daily Scrum — a 15-minute stand-up to sync and plan the next 24 hours.
  • Sprint Review — show the increment to stakeholders and gather feedback.
  • Sprint Retrospective — inspect the process and decide what to improve.

▶ What is Scrum? Roles, Artifacts & Events — Invensis Learning. Why it helps: it walks the roles → artifacts → events loop as one moving system, which is exactly how the exam expects you to reason about a broken Scrum team.

XP — the engineering habits

XP focuses on keeping the code clean and changeable through disciplined practices. The ones the faculty listed, in plain terms:

  • Pair Programming: two developers, one keyboard — a "driver" types, a "navigator" reviews every line live.
  • Test-Driven Development (TDD): write the automated test before the code it checks.
  • Continuous Integration (CI): merge everyone's code into a shared repo many times a day; each merge auto-builds and auto-tests.
  • Refactoring: restructure existing code without changing what it does, to keep it readable.
  • Collective Code Ownership: nobody "owns" a file; the whole team is responsible for all of it.
  • Incremental Development, Planning Game, On-site Customer: build in small value-adding steps; customers and developers plan/prioritise together; a customer rep sits with the team daily.
  • User Story / Metaphor / Spike: a User Story is a plain-language feature description from the user; a Metaphor is a shared vision of how the system works; a Spike is a tiny throwaway program built to test whether a proposed solution is feasible (like a prototype).
Watch out — the exam's favourite trap

Symptoms map to specific missing pieces. "Tasks never finish" → no timeboxed Sprint + no Definition of Done. "Modules don't integrate" → no Continuous Integration / collective ownership. "Testing left to the end" → no TDD / testing inside the Sprint. "Meetings go nowhere" → ceremonies run without a Scrum Master or a Sprint Goal. Don't answer "they should be more Agile" — name the exact practice each symptom violates.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from This checks whether you can separate process management from engineering discipline — the Scrum vs XP table in the Agile lecture. The examiner wants contrasts, not two separate definitions.

Three differences: (1) Focus — Scrum organises who does what and when through roles and ceremonies, whereas XP prescribes how to write the code through practices like TDD and pair programming. In practice teams layer XP practices inside a Scrum rhythm.

Topic 02

Work Breakdown Structure (WBS)

The tool that turns a vague project into a list of things you can actually assign, cost, and track.

A big project ("build a university portal") is too large to manage as one lump — you can't estimate it, assign it, or tell when it's done. A Work Breakdown Structure chops the whole project into a tree of smaller and smaller pieces until each leaf is a work package: a chunk small enough that one person or team can own it, estimate it, and finish it. Think of it as taking one impossible question and splitting it into a hundred answerable ones.

Key idea

A WBS is a hierarchical, deliverable-oriented decomposition: Project → Task → Sub-task → Work Package. Each leaf should be a measurable deliverable, not a vague activity.

The 100% Rule (the thing they test)

The single rule that governs a valid WBS: it must capture 100% of the work — no more, no less. Concretely that means: (1) every deliverable needed to finish the project appears somewhere in the WBS (nothing missing); (2) no work outside the WBS counts as part of the project (nothing extra); and (3) no overlap — each piece belongs to exactly one parent, so no two boxes secretly cover the same work.

Watch out

Two failure modes the faculty's CampusConnect example drilled: missing deliverable (the client asked for "user training" but it's nowhere in the draft → violates completeness) and overlap ("Frontend Development" and "Mobile App Development" both doing UI; "API Development" and "Backend Development" both doing the logic layer → double-counted work). If asked to critique a WBS, hunt for exactly these two.

Levels and types

The levels run 1 = whole project, 2 = major deliverables/phases, 3 = sub-deliverables/tasks, 4 = work packages. There are three ways to organise the tree, and you pick based on what you care about:

  • Deliverable-based — organised by the things you produce ("what we produce"). Use when the outputs are concrete and clear.
  • Phase-based — organised by life-cycle stages (Initiation → Design → Development → Implementation → Closure). Use when the process matters more than the product.
  • Function-based — organised by department (Design, Engineering, Marketing, Finance). Use in organisations with distinct teams.
Practice 2.1 · short answer

A draft WBS for a food-delivery app lists: 1.1 UI Design, 1.2 Frontend, 1.3 Mobile App, 1.4 Backend, 1.5 API, 1.6 Payment, 1.7 Testing, 1.8 Deployment. The client also asked for staff onboarding and a user manual. Identify the 100%-Rule violations.

Show model answer

Fix by adding the two missing branches and merging or clearly separating the overlapping ones.

Topic 03

Critical Path Method (CPM)

The single most exam-heavy calculation in this course. Q2 is a CPM problem — master the forward/backward pass and you own it.

Some tasks in a project can wait; others can't. If you delay picking a paint colour, no harm done — but if you delay pouring the foundation, everything after it slips. CPM is the method that finds, out of all the chains of dependent tasks, the longest one — because that longest chain is what actually sets the project's finish date. That chain is the critical path: every task on it is a task where a one-day slip is a one-day slip for the whole project.

Key idea

The critical path is the longest path through the network, and its length is the shortest possible project duration. Tasks on it have zero float — no slack at all.

The four numbers on every task

For each activity you compute four times. Learn them as two pairs. The forward pass (left → right) gives the "earliest" pair; the backward pass (right → left) gives the "latest" pair.

Forward pass — earliest times
\[ \text{ES} = \max(\text{EF of all predecessors}) \qquad \text{EF} = \text{ES} + \text{duration} \]
Backward pass — latest times
\[ \text{LF} = \min(\text{LS of all successors}) \qquad \text{LS} = \text{LF} - \text{duration} \]
Float (slack) and the critical test
\[ \text{Float} = \text{LS} - \text{ES} = \text{LF} - \text{EF}, \qquad \text{Float}=0 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{critical} \]
  • ES (Early Start): earliest a task can begin. No predecessors → ES = 0. Otherwise ES = the largest EF among its predecessors (you must wait for the slowest one).
  • EF (Early Finish): EF = ES + duration.
  • LF (Late Finish): latest a task can finish without delaying the project. Last task → LF = project EF. Otherwise LF = the smallest LS among its successors.
  • LS (Late Start): LS = LF − duration.
Watch out — max vs min

Forward pass at a merge point takes the maximum predecessor EF (a task waits for all its inputs). Backward pass at a branch point takes the minimum successor LS (a task must not delay any of the paths that depend on it). Flip these and every float comes out wrong.

Worked example (the faculty's own)

Tasks: A(2), B(4, after A), C(3, after A), D(2, after B & C), E(1, after D).

TaskPredDurESEFLSLFFloatCritical?
A—202020Yes

CA325361No

Practice 3.1 · medium — this is the faculty's own practice table

Find the critical path and duration. A(3, —), B(2, —), C(4, A), D(3, A), E(2, B), F(3, C&E), G(2, D&F), H(1, G).

Show worked solution

Do the forward pass first, then the backward pass, then float.

  1. \( \text{ES}(A)=0,\ \text{EF}(A)=3;\quad \text{ES}(B)=0,\ \text{EF}(B)=2 \)

    A and B have no predecessors, so ES = 0; \( \text{EF}=\text{ES}+\text{dur} \).

  2. \( \text{ES}(C)=3,\ \text{EF}(C)=7;\quad \text{ES}(D)=3,\ \text{EF}(D)=6 \)

    C, D follow A, so their ES = EF(A) = 3.

  3. \( \text{ES}(E)=2,\ \text{EF}(E)=4 \)

    E follows B, so ES = EF(B) = 2.

  4. \( \text{ES}(F)=\max(7,4)=7,\ \text{EF}(F)=10 \)

    Merge: F waits for C(7) and E(4); take the max.

  5. \( \text{ES}(G)=\max(6,10)=10,\ \text{EF}(G)=12 \)

    Merge: G waits for D(6) and F(10); take the max.

  6. \( \text{ES}(H)=12,\ \text{EF}(H)=13 \)

    H follows G. Project duration = 13 (the last EF).

  7. \( \text{LF}(H)=13,\ \text{LS}(H)=12;\ \text{LF}(G)=12,\ \text{LS}(G)=10;\ \text{LF}(F)=10,\ \text{LS}(F)=7;\ \text{LF}(C)=7,\ \text{LS}(C)=3 \)

    Backward pass: last task LF = 13; each earlier LF = min LS of successors, LS = LF − dur.

  8. \( \text{Float}: D=6,\ E=3,\ B=3;\quad A,C,F,G,H=0 \)

    \( \text{Float}=\text{LS}-\text{ES} \). Zero-float tasks are critical.

Answer

Critical path: A → C → F → G → H = 3+4+3+2+1 = 13 days. Sanity check: the other route A-D-G-H = 3+3+2+1 = 9 < 13, and B-E-F-G-H = 2+2+3+2+1 = 10 < 13, so 13 is genuinely the longest. ✓

Topic 04

PERT — scheduling under uncertainty

CPM assumes you know each duration exactly. PERT is for when you don't — and it lets you answer "what's the chance we finish by the deadline?"

For a brand-new task (R&D, novel software) you can't name one honest duration. So instead of pretending, PERT asks for three guesses per task: a best case, a worst case, and a most-likely case. It then blends them into a single expected time and, crucially, a measure of how uncertain that time is. That uncertainty is what lets PERT do something CPM can't: attach a probability to hitting a deadline.

Key idea

PERT = CPM + probability. Same critical-path logic, but each duration is a weighted blend of three estimates, and each task carries a variance so you can compute the odds of finishing on time.

The faculty's symbols: optimistic a, most likely m, pessimistic b.

Expected time (weighted toward the most likely)
\[ T_E = \frac{a + 4m + b}{6} \]
Variance of one task (how spread-out its duration is)
\[ \sigma^2 = \left(\frac{b - a}{6}\right)^2 \]
Finishing on time — the Z-score
\[ Z = \frac{T - T_{E,\text{project}}}{\sigma_{\text{project}}}, \qquad \sigma_{\text{project}} = \sqrt{\textstyle\sum \sigma^2 \text{ on the critical path}} \]

Then look Z up in the standard normal (Z) table to get the probability of finishing by deadline \(T\).

Watch out

Two classic slips: (1) the 4 multiplies m only, and you divide by 6, not 3 — it's a weighted average, not a plain one. (2) Project variance sums the variances of the tasks on the critical path only, then \( \sigma=\sqrt{\text{that sum}} \). Don't add standard deviations directly, and don't include off-path tasks.

Worked example (the faculty's Example 1)

Taskamb\(T_E\)VarPred
A1232.000.11—
B2383.671.00A
C1111.000.00A
D3454.000.11B,C

  1. \( T_E(B)=\frac{2+4(3)+8}{6}=\frac{22}{6}=3.67 \)

    Apply \( (a+4m+b)/6 \) to each task (B shown).

  2. \( \text{Var}(B)=\left(\frac{8-2}{6}\right)^2 = 1^2 = 1.00 \)

    Apply \( ((b-a)/6)^2 \) to each task (B shown).

  3. \( \text{A-B-D}=2+3.67+4=9.67;\quad \text{A-C-D}=2+1+4=7.0 \)

    Sum \(T_E\) along each path; the larger is critical.

  4. \( \sigma=\sqrt{0.11+1.00+0.11}=\sqrt{1.22}\approx 1.10 \)

    Sum variances on the critical path A-B-D, then square-root.

  5. \( Z=\frac{11-9.67}{1.10}=1.21 \Rightarrow P\approx 0.887 \)

    Z-score for deadline \(T=11\); look up 1.21 in the Z-table.

Answer

Critical path A-B-D = 9.67 weeks; ~88.7% chance of finishing by week 11. Sanity check: the deadline (11) is above the expected finish (9.67), so a probability above 50% is expected. ✓

Topic 05

Critical Chain Project Management (CCPM)

CPM's successor: it fixes two things CPM ignores — shared resources, and the padding hidden inside every estimate.

CPM has a blind spot: it assumes that if two tasks can run in parallel, they will — even if both need the same one senior developer. CCPM starts by respecting resources. Then it notices a human habit: people pad their estimates for safety, and when padded tasks run back-to-back the padding piles up and gets wasted (work expands to fill the time). CCPM strips the padding out of each task and pools it into one shared buffer at the end. Result: a shorter planned schedule that is still protected.

Key idea

CCPM = the resource-feasible longest chain (the critical chain), with per-task safety removed and reinserted as a single shared project buffer (typically 50% of the chain length).

The workflow, and why the order of resource-sharing tasks matters

First find the CPM schedule. Then resolve resource conflicts: if two tasks share a person, one must go first — and the two orderings usually give different total durations, so you pick the shorter one. Then cut each estimate (the lectures assume ~50% is padding) and add the buffer.

In the faculty's example the unconstrained CPM schedule was 38 days; forcing the shared Senior Developer to run sequentially pushed it to 42 or 48 depending on order; cutting the padding and adding an 11-day project buffer brought the protected finish down to 32 days.

The three buffer types (a favourite short-answer)

  • Project Buffer (PB): sits at the end of the critical chain; absorbs delays on the chain so the promised finish date holds. ≈ 50% of the chain.
  • Feeding Buffer (FB): sits where a side path merges into the critical chain; absorbs delays on the feeding path so the chain itself isn't hit. ≈ 50% of the feeding path.
  • Resource Buffer (RB): not time — it's an advance alert that a needed resource (e.g. an external DevOps engineer) must be ready exactly when a chain task begins. Adds zero days.
Watch out

The Resource Buffer adds no days to the schedule — it's a notification, not a time allowance. Marking it as "X days" is a classic error. Also: the critical chain can differ from the critical path, because the chain accounts for the shared resource, not just dependencies.

Practice 5.1 · medium

A critical chain is Design(4) → Build(6) → Deploy(4). A feeding chain Content(4) → Review(2) merges in before Deploy. Size the project buffer and feeding buffer, and give the protected finish.

Show worked solution

  1. \( \text{chain}=4+6+4=14 \)

    Sum the critical-chain task durations.

  2. \( \text{PB}=0.5\times14=7 \)

    Project buffer ≈ 50% of the critical chain.

  3. \( \text{feeding path}=4+2=6,\quad \text{FB}=0.5\times6=3 \)

    Feeding buffer ≈ 50% of the feeding path length.

  4. \( \text{protected finish}=14+7=21 \)

    Chain length plus project buffer. The feeding chain (6+3=9) finishes before Deploy, so it doesn't push the finish.

Answer

PB = 7 days, FB = 3 days, protected finish = 21 days. If a DevOps engineer were needed at Deploy, you'd also flag a Resource Buffer — an alert, adding 0 days. ✓

Topic 06

Cost Estimation — Analogous Estimating

This is Q3. It wasn't in the uploaded lecture notes, so treat this section as the supplementary background you need to solve that question.

Note on scope

The uploaded lectures don't cover estimating, but the Fall 2025 paper's Q3 tests it — so it's clearly on the syllabus. This section gives the minimum you need; check it against your own class notes in case your faculty used slightly different wording.

Suppose you've built three houses and someone asks the cost of a fourth. Rather than pricing every brick from scratch, you find the most similar past house and adjust its known cost for the differences. That's analogous estimating (also called top-down): use a comparable finished project as the anchor, then nudge its actual cost up or down for whatever differs — size, quality grade, design tier.

Key idea

Pick the most similar past project (fewest differences), then apply each difference as a multiplier. Fewer adjustments = less error, which is exactly why you choose the closest analogue.

Handle each stated difference as a factor on the known cost:

General form
\[ \text{Cost}_{\text{new}} = \text{Cost}_{\text{ref}} \times \frac{\text{Area}_{\text{new}}}{\text{Area}_{\text{ref}}} \times (\text{design factor}) \times (\text{material factor}) \]

A "+20%" difference is a ×1.20 factor when adding it, or ÷1.20 when removing it from the reference; "−10%" is ×0.90 or ÷0.90. Area scales directly (proportional). The whole game is deciding which factors you need — and picking a reference that needs as few as possible.

Watch out

Estimating the same new project from different references gives slightly different numbers, because percentage adjustments don't compose perfectly. That's not a mistake — it's the reason the method insists on the closest analogue. If your chosen reference already matches on design and material, you only scale for area, and the estimate is at its most trustworthy.

Topic 07

The Business Model Canvas (9 blocks)

The entrepreneurship half of the course: how a business creates, delivers, and captures value — on one page.

A business model is simply how a company creates, delivers, and captures value. Alexander Osterwalder's Business Model Canvas lays that logic out as nine linked blocks on a single sheet, so you can design or pivot a whole business at a glance. A useful mental split: the right side is the "show" (what the customer sees), the left side is the "factory" (what happens backstage), and the bottom is the "wallet" (money in vs money out).

Key idea

Nine blocks: Customer Segments, Value Propositions, Channels, Customer Relationships, Revenue Streams (the right/"show" + wallet), and Key Resources, Key Activities, Key Partners, Cost Structure (the left/"factory" + wallet).

  1. Customer Segments — who you serve. B2C segmentation: demographic (age, income), psychographic (values, lifestyle — the "why"), behavioural (usage, loyalty). Remember user ≠ customer: the child uses the toy, the parent pays; you (searcher) use Google, the advertiser pays.
  2. Value Propositions — why they pick you. Quantitative value (price, performance, cost/risk reduction) vs qualitative (newness, design, convenience, brand, "getting the job done"). Tool: the Value Proposition Canvas pairs the Customer Profile (jobs, pains, gains) with the Value Map (products, pain relievers, gain creators).
  3. Channels — how you reach them, across five phases: Awareness → Evaluation → Purchase → Delivery → After-sales.
  4. Customer Relationships — the connection type, driven by three goals: acquisition, retention, and boosting sales (up/cross-sell).
  5. Revenue Streams — how you capture value: transactional (one-off sales) vs recurring (subscriptions, high lifetime value). Pricing can be fixed (list, feature/segment/volume-based) or dynamic (negotiation, yield management, real-time market).
  6. Key Resources — the assets you need: physical, intellectual (brands, patents), human, financial.
  7. Key Activities — the actions that use those resources: production, problem-solving, platform/network. (Resources are the things; activities are the doing.)
  8. Key Partners — the network you rely on, motivated by: optimisation/economies of scale, reducing risk/uncertainty, acquiring resources you lack, or co-opetition (partnering with a competitor).
  9. Cost Structure — what it all costs: fixed vs variable costs, and a strategic tilt that's either cost-driven (Ryanair, Walmart) or value-driven (Rolex).
Watch out

Don't confuse Key Resources (the assets — a patent, a factory) with Key Activities (the actions — manufacturing, R&D). And keep the user-vs-customer distinction sharp; it's a favourite one-mark trap.

Topic 08

Business Model Types

The short taxonomy: who is transacting with whom. Easy marks if you can tell them apart with an example each.

These label who sells to whom. Learn each by its one-line example — the exam usually asks you to name the type from a scenario or vice versa.

  • B2B (Business-to-Business): firms trade with firms. Types: supplier-, buyer-, and intermediary-oriented. Example: a carmaker buying tyres, glass, and hoses.
  • B2C (Business-to-Consumer): a company sells directly to end-users. Example: that same carmaker selling the finished car to you.
  • C2C (Consumer-to-Consumer): consumers transact via a third-party platform. Example: an online auction where people bid on each other's items.
  • B2G (Business-to-Government): a firm serves a government agency. Example: an IT consultancy keeping a ministry's systems current without it hiring full-time staff.
  • C2B (Consumer-to-Business): individuals create value that businesses consume. Example: a consumer writing a review or suggesting a product idea the company adopts.
Watch out

One real-world flow can contain several types. The carmaker's supply chain is a stack of B2B transactions; only the final sale to the driver is B2C. Read the scenario for who pays whom at each step.

What this tests & where it comes from Whole-scenario diagnosis against the Agile / Scrum lecture, plus XP practices for the engineering symptoms. The examiner wants each symptom mapped to a specific missing Scrum/XP element, then a concrete fix — not "be more Agile."

(a) The methodology [1 mark]. They are attempting Agile, specifically Scrum — "flexible and collaborative," cross-functional teams, iterative "development cycles" (Sprints), and a "product lead" (a Product Owner). But it's Scrum in name only: the framework's discipline is absent.

(b) The key mistakes [4 marks] — each symptom is a specific violation:

  • Tasks never finish on time → no timeboxed Sprint with a committed Sprint Goal and no Definition of Done, so work has no closing line.
  • Isolation → integration problems → no Continuous Integration and no collective code ownership; the daily sync isn't functioning.
  • Requirements change with no structure → no prioritised Product Backlog and no backlog refinement, so change hits mid-flow instead of being queued and re-prioritised.
  • Meetings lack focus/outcomes → ceremonies run without a Scrum Master facilitating and without timeboxes or goals (a Daily Scrum that isn't 15 minutes and forward-looking).
  • Testing left to the end → no TDD / no testing inside the Sprint, so bugs accumulate and the increment is never truly "done."
  • Burnout + strained PO↔team communication → no Scrum Master to remove impediments and shield the team, no sustainable pace, and a broken feedback loop between the Product Owner and developers.

(c) What I'd do as PM [5 marks] — install real Scrum, reinforced with XP engineering practices:

  • Define the roles: a single Product Owner (owns/prioritises the backlog), a Scrum Master (removes impediments, shields the team, facilitates), and a self-organising Dev Team.
  • Stand up a prioritised Product Backlog and route all changing requirements through backlog refinement and re-prioritisation, instead of absorbing them mid-Sprint.
  • Run fixed-length Sprints with a clear Sprint Goal and a strict Definition of Done that includes testing, so work actually closes.
  • Fix the ceremonies: focused Sprint Planning, a 15-minute forward-looking Daily Scrum, a Sprint Review with stakeholders, and a Retrospective to improve the process.
  • Add XP practices to kill the technical symptoms: Continuous Integration and collective ownership (integration), TDD / test-within-Sprint (bugs), and pair programming (isolation, knowledge sharing).
  • Protect the people: the Scrum Master enforces a sustainable pace and repairs the PO↔team communication channel to end the burnout.

Q2 — Activity-on-Node scheduling · 10 marks (2+2+3+3), CO1/CO2

A(3, —), B(6, A), C(8, A), D(4, B), E(5, C), F(3, B&C), G(4, D&E&F). (a) Compute ES/EF for all activities. (b) Does ES of G depend only on the latest predecessor? Justify. (c) New policy: "G can begin once 80% of its input branches (D,E,F) have finished, provided the unfinished branch has ≤1 day of work remaining." Show the effect on total duration. (d) Merge-point delay risk: D (p=0.1, +1 day), E (p=0.3, +2 days), F (p=0.2, +1 day). (I) Estimate the expected total delay at G. (II) Compare with the time saved in (c): credible net benefit, or trading reliability for nominal speed?

Show worked solution

(a) Forward pass [2 marks].

  1. \( \text{ES}(A)=0,\ \text{EF}(A)=3 \)

    No predecessor → ES = 0; EF = 0+3.

  2. \( \text{ES}(B)=3,\ \text{EF}(B)=9;\quad \text{ES}(C)=3,\ \text{EF}(C)=11 \)

    B, C follow A, so ES = EF(A) = 3.

  3. \( \text{ES}(D)=9,\ \text{EF}(D)=13 \)

    D follows B: ES = EF(B) = 9.

  4. \( \text{ES}(E)=11,\ \text{EF}(E)=16 \)

    E follows C: ES = EF(C) = 11.

  5. \( \text{ES}(F)=\max(9,11)=11,\ \text{EF}(F)=14 \)

    Merge: F waits for B(9) and C(11) → max = 11.

  6. \( \text{ES}(G)=\max(13,16,14)=16,\ \text{EF}(G)=20 \)

    Merge: G waits for D(13), E(16), F(14) → max = 16.

Project duration = 20 days. Critical path (from a quick backward pass): A → C → E → G = 3+8+5+4 = 20. ✓

(b) Does ES(G) depend only on the latest predecessor? [2 marks].

Yes — here it does. ES(G) = max(EF(D), EF(E), EF(F)) = max(13, 16, 14) = 16 = EF(E). A merge node can't start until all its inputs are done, so the binding constraint is whichever finishes last — E, at day 16. D (13) and F (14) finish earlier, so they have slack and don't affect G's start. Caveat to state for full marks: "latest" means latest finish (largest EF), not longest duration — you must still compute all three EFs to know which one is latest; you can't eyeball it from durations alone.

(c) The 80% merge relaxation [3 marks].

Read the operative clause: G may start when the branches are essentially done — the straggler needs only ≤1 day of work remaining. The latest branch is E, finishing at 16. So instead of waiting for 16, G can start when E has 1 day left, i.e. at day 15 (by then D finished at 13 and F at 14, so two of three branches are complete and E is within one day).

  1. \( \text{new ES}(G)=16-1=15 \)

    Start one day before the last branch (E) finishes, per the ≤1-day clause.

  2. \( \text{new duration}=15+4=19 \)

    EF(G) = ES + 4. Time saved = 20 − 19 = 1 day.

State your interpretation

"80% of 3 branches" is loose (2 of 3 = 67%). Say so explicitly and lean on the precise, testable clause ("unfinished branch ≤1 day remaining"), which fixes the early start at day 15. Examiners reward flagging the ambiguity rather than hiding it.

(d-I) Expected total delay at G [part of 3 marks]. Expected delay per branch = probability × impact; sum them.

  1. \( D:\ 0.1\times1=0.1 \)

    p(delay) × schedule impact.

  2. \( E:\ 0.3\times2=0.6 \)

    Same rule; E is the big contributor.

  3. \( F:\ 0.2\times1=0.2 \)

    Same rule.

  4. \( \text{Expected total delay}=0.1+0.6+0.2=0.9 \text{ days} \)

    Sum of expected contributions across the three inputs.

(d-II) Credible benefit, or trading reliability for speed? The rule saves a nominal 1 day but the merge point carries an expected 0.9 days of delay — a net expected gain of only ~0.1 day, and that's before accounting for variance. Worse, the whole relaxation bets on E finishing on schedule, yet E is precisely the riskiest input (highest probability 0.3 and largest impact 2 days, ≈0.6 of the total risk) and it sits on the critical path. So the rule pays out a near-zero net time saving in exchange for real schedule reliability.

Verdict

Not a credible net benefit. ~0.1 day expected gain vs a fragile bet on the single most delay-prone, critical-path branch (E). It trades away reliability for nominal speed.

Q3 — Analogous estimating · 10 marks (5+5), CO2

Past projects: A (1,200 sq.m, Standard, Medium, 15M BDT); B (1,500, Luxury, High, 22M); C (1,000, Standard, Low, 11M). Rules: Luxury +20% over Standard; High materials +15% over Medium, Low −10% under Medium; cost ∝ floor area. New Project D: 1,300 sq.m, Standard, Medium. (a) Which past project is most similar to D — justify. (b) Estimate D's cost.

Show worked solution

(a) Most similar project [5 marks]. D is Standard + Medium. Compare: A is Standard + Medium (matches both), C is Standard + Low (material differs), B is Luxury + High (both differ). So Project A is the closest analogue — identical design tier and material grade, with only floor area differing (1,200 vs 1,300). Choosing A means no design or material adjustment is needed, only a simple area scaling, which minimises adjustment error — the whole point of picking the nearest match.

(b) Estimate D's cost [5 marks]. Since A and D share design and material, only floor area changes, and cost is directly proportional to area.

  1. \( \text{Cost}_D = 15 \times \dfrac{1300}{1200} \)

    Scale A's known 15M by the area ratio; no design/material factor because they're identical.

  2. \( = 15 \times 1.0833\ldots \)

    \( 1300/1200 = 13/12 \approx 1.0833 \).

  3. \( = 16.25 \text{ million BDT} \)

    \( 15 \times 13/12 = 195/12 = 16.25 \).

Answer

≈ 16.25 million BDT.

Cross-check for confidence (optional, earns credit): estimate from C instead (Standard, one material step away): \( 11 \times \tfrac{1}{0.90} \times \tfrac{1300}{1000} = 11 \times 1.111 \times 1.3 \approx 15.9\text{M} \) — close to 16.25M, so the estimate is robust around ~16M. Estimating from B (Luxury, High — two adjustments) would give ≈13.8M, an outlier: proof that the least-similar analogue is the least reliable, which is exactly why A was chosen.
