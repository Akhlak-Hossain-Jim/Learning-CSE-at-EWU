Before you start

Exam-day playbook

How to use this book with two or three days left.

Book 1 taught the topics. This book is for practice under conditions, and the single most valuable thing you can do between now and the exam is sit at least one of these mocks with a timer running and your notes closed. Reading a model answer feels like learning and mostly is not. Producing an answer, failing, and then reading the model is where the improvement happens.

How to sit a mock properly

Ninety minutes, phone in another room, notes closed, one sheet of paper. Read the case for ten minutes with a pen in hand, annotating as Topic 11 of Book 1 describes. Then write all six answers before you open a single solution. When you do open them, mark yourself honestly against the four moves: did you name a lecture concept, anchor it in a case fact, explain the mechanism, and state a consequence?

The timing budget

Thirty marks in ninety minutes. Use this split and check the clock at each boundary:

ClockTaskOutput
0–10 minRead and annotate the caseMargin labels: traits, before/after, churn, metrics pile, crisis, options
10–15 minSkim all questions, jot one keyword eachSix keywords, so nothing surprises you at minute 70
15–80 minAnswer, roughly 11 min eachDo the numerical question first if there is one
80–90 minFill gaps, add a missing consequence sentenceEvery question has at least a labelled skeleton
Do the calculation question first

If the paper contains an earned value or EMV question, answer it before the analytical ones. Numerical marks are binary and fast: you either get them in eight minutes or you do not. Analytical answers expand or contract to fill whatever time is left, so protect the certain marks first.

What to do with each mock

  • Mock 1 (TiffinTrail) is closest to the Fall 2025 pattern: a service business scaling badly, with all five classic question slots plus one earned value question. Sit this one under exam conditions.
  • Mock 2 (LedgerLite) is a software product case, weighted toward lean architecture, KPI unit economics and EMV. Use it to cover the topics Fall 2025 skipped.
  • Mock 3 (RepairKori) is a mixed paper: a shorter case with two standalone calculation questions bolted on, which is the format to expect if your faculty decides to examine cost analysis separately.
Mock Paper 01

TiffinTrail — a home-meal subscription service

30 marks · 90 minutes · six questions of five marks. Closest to the Fall 2025 pattern in structure, with one extra question so the calculation topics get covered too.

Case study

Founder and background. Nusrat Jahan, 29, Dhaka. Six years as a supply chain analyst at an FMCG distributor before founding TiffinTrail in 2022, after two years of eating what she described as "identical, reheated, joyless" office lunches. Capitalised with personal savings plus a BDT 3,000,000 angel investment in exchange for 25% equity.

Leadership profile (Big Five assessment). Openness 71 · Conscientiousness 90 · Extraversion 58 · Agreeableness 62 · Neuroticism 79.

Business today (Year 3). Two segments. B2C: 610 household subscribers, on a Solo plan at BDT 4,500 per month (410 subscribers) or a Duo plan at BDT 7,900 (200 subscribers). B2B: six corporate lunch contracts covering 340 employees, billed at BDT 180 per meal across roughly 20 working days. Monthly revenue BDT 4,649,000, monthly operating cost BDT 4,180,000, net profit BDT 469,000.

Current crisis. The head chef has given notice, citing exhaustion from producing six different bespoke corporate menus alongside the standard rotation. Each corporate client negotiated its own menu at signing, and the B2B division consumes a disproportionate share of kitchen labour. The angel investor has asked for a committed growth path within five months.

Strategic options on the table.

  1. Government school-meal tender. 40,000 meals per day at BDT 95 per meal for 24 months. Requires nine regional kitchens, nutrition compliance certification, and daily reporting to a ministry.
  2. Cloud-kitchen franchise. Twelve franchisees at BDT 800,000 each plus a 6% royalty. TiffinTrail supplies brand, recipes and the app; food preparation and quality control pass to the franchisees.
  3. Venture funding. A term sheet offering BDT 20,000,000 for 30% equity, to build a demand-forecasting and route-optimisation platform over 15 months.

Platform project status (relevant to Question 6). TiffinTrail began the route-optimisation platform ahead of the term sheet, self-funded, with an approved budget at completion of BDT 4,000,000. At the month-nine review, 50% of the work was scheduled to be complete, 40% is actually complete, and BDT 2,200,000 has been spent.

Question 1 · 5 marks

Identify the core business model failure that explains the falling retention and the deterioration in service quality.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from The diagnosis slot, drawing on the prototype-versus-product distinction and the MVP misconception (Prototypes & MVP lecture, Sections 1 and 6) together with the value-delivery reasoning behind Q1 of the Fall 2025 paper. The examiner wants a named failure plus the mechanism, and the before-and-after table is where they planted the evidence.

The core failure: value proposition delivery collapsed under industrialisation. TiffinTrail scaled its meal volume while deleting the two attributes customers were actually paying a premium for, personalisation and reliability. At 120 subscribers the product was 14 home chefs cooking a menu chosen by that household, with the founder personally calling every new subscriber and a 96% on-time record. At 610 subscribers plus corporate contracts it is a single central kitchen producing one fixed 14-day rotation for everyone, app-only, with on-time delivery down to 78%. The company kept the noun, a tiffin subscription, and discarded the adjectives that justified its price.

The mechanism, read off the churn data. Every top exit reason maps onto a specific operational change. 41% cite inconsistent quality, which is what happens when 14 chefs cooking small batches are replaced by one kitchen producing at scale without the process maturity to hold quality there. 24% cite unreliable delivery windows, matching the 96% to 78% fall. 21% cite menu repetition, which is the direct consequence of replacing per-household personalisation with a 42-recipe fixed rotation, and the customer quotation makes it explicit: "I can tell you what Thursday's box contains without opening it."

The commoditisation consequence. Once the food is standardised and delivery is unreliable, TiffinTrail is no longer differentiated from any local caterer, so it competes on price in a market where it is not the cheapest. The 14% who left for a cheaper local option are the visible edge of that, and the thin margin, BDT 469,000 net on BDT 4,649,000 of revenue, roughly 10%, means it has very little room to defend itself on price.

The compounding factor. The support data shows the largest ticket driver is "change or pause my delivery slot", 210 tickets, against a policy requiring 24 hours' notice and no phone line. The flexibility that existed by default when orders came over WhatsApp was engineered out, so the operational change created both the churn reason and the support load that now obscures it.

Question 2 · 5 marks

From the data collected by the operations team, identify four KPIs. Justify why each qualifies as a KPI, and explain why you excluded the others.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from The KPI selection slot, built on the KPI-versus-metric table and the coffee shop case (KPI lecture, Sections 2 and 6). Three components are marked: selection, justification tied to a strategic question, and reasoned exclusion. Skipping the exclusions typically costs a third of the marks.

Selected KPIs

1. Monthly subscriber churn (6.1%). The retention KPI, and for a subscription business the number that determines whether it has a business at all. At 6.1% a month the entire base turns over in roughly sixteen months, so every marketing taka is buying a replacement rather than growth. It measures a strategic outcome, is fully quantifiable, and is actionable through the specific fixes the exit survey points to.

2. On-time delivery rate (78%). The operational KPI, equivalent to the average wait time the lecture selects in the coffee shop case as a measure of service efficiency. For a meal delivered at a fixed mealtime, punctuality is not a convenience but part of the product itself: a lunch that arrives at 3 PM has failed entirely. It is also causally upstream of churn, since 24% of leavers name it.

3. Gross margin per subscriber-month (BDT 770) read against customer acquisition cost (BDT 3,900). The unit economics KPI. At BDT 770 of margin per month it takes just over five months to recover the BDT 3,900 acquisition cost, while the average subscriber lasts roughly sixteen months at current churn. The business is therefore viable but with a thin cushion, and if churn worsens to about 20% a month the model inverts. This is the number that decides whether spending more on growth helps or accelerates the loss.

4. Corporate contract renewal rate (67%). The segment-health KPI. B2B is the segment consuming disproportionate kitchen labour and driving the head chef to resign, so knowing whether that cost buys durable revenue is a strategic question, not an operational one. A third of contracts failing to renew means the most expensive segment is also the least sticky, which is decisive information for the growth decision the investor is demanding.

Excluded, with reasons

  • App downloads (26,000) and registered accounts (9,400): volume without quality. Both count people who have never subscribed, and with 610 active subscribers the gap shows how little they predict. They can rise indefinitely while revenue is flat, which is the masking effect the Fall 2025 answer warns about with raw signups.
  • Facebook page followers (88,000): no proven link to a business outcome. A top-of-funnel marketing figure with no demonstrated conversion to subscriptions or cash flow, the same reason the lecture rejects Instagram followers in the coffee shop case.
  • Distinct recipes in rotation (42): an input, not an outcome. It describes what the kitchen produces rather than whether customers value it, and the case shows it is currently too low, so as a KPI it would perversely reward adding recipes without asking whether anyone wants them.
  • Meals dispatched (61,400): a volume metric. It rises with subscriber count and tells you nothing about quality, punctuality or profitability, and it would look healthy in exactly the quarter the business is deteriorating.
  • Kitchen food waste (11% of raw input): a genuine efficiency metric and a defensible fifth choice, since waste directly erodes the thin margin. Excluded here because it is an internal operational measure rather than an outcome measure, and because the four above already cover retention, service, economics and segment health without redundancy.
  • Active subscribers (610): useful context and a component of other measures, but as a raw count it is a snapshot rather than an indicator of direction. Churn tells you where it is heading, which is what a KPI is for.
Question 3 · 5 marks

Analyse TiffinTrail's scaling decision in MVP terms, and reconstruct a value proposition that can grow without repeating the mistake.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from The MVP slot, drawing on the MVP definition, the Build-Measure-Learn loop and the feature-reduction misconception (Prototypes & MVP, Sections 5 to 7). This mirrors Q3 of the Fall 2025 paper. Roughly half the marks sit in the reconstruction, so budget your time to reach it.

What was actually validated at 120 subscribers. The early business proved something quite specific, and naming it precisely is what makes the rest of the answer work. It was not that people will buy delivered lunch, which was never in doubt. It was that households will pay a premium for food that feels individually cooked for them, arriving reliably, from a business that knows who they are. The 96% on-time rate and the customer line "it feels like someone's mother is cooking for me" are the evidence, and 14 home chefs plus a founder phone call were the mechanism producing it.

The misinterpretation. Scaling was treated as a capacity problem rather than a value-delivery problem. A central kitchen genuinely solves capacity: more meals, lower unit cost, easier logistics. But it silently removed personalisation, and going app-only with a 24-hour change window removed responsiveness, so the two validated attributes were traded away for throughput without anyone deciding to trade them. This is the lecture's core MVP misconception in its operational form: the company confused producing more with delivering more value. The Build-Measure-Learn loop was effectively broken as well, since the scale-up was built and shipped without measuring whether the qualities customers were paying for survived it. The 41% quality complaints and the fall to 78% on-time are the measurement arriving eighteen months late.

Reconstructed value proposition. Rebuild around personalisation and reliability delivered at scale, rather than around kitchen throughput:

  • A modular menu instead of a fixed rotation. Keep the central kitchen for base preparation, which is where the cost advantage genuinely lives, but let each subscriber set a small profile: spice level, two excluded items, one preferred protein per week. Personalisation is restored at the assembly stage, where it costs little, rather than at the cooking stage, where it costs a great deal.
  • A satellite-chef tier for the premium segment. Re-engage a small panel of home chefs for a higher-priced plan, which preserves the original proposition for the customers who valued it most and gives the brand a quality anchor the central kitchen can be measured against.
  • Same-day changes up to a published cut-off. The 210 slot-change tickets and the 9 to 11 PM ordering peak are telling the company exactly what the product needs. Move the cut-off to that window and turn the biggest support cost into a feature.
  • A published on-time guarantee with automatic credit. Converts reliability from an internal metric into a customer-visible promise, and forces the operational fix by making failure cost money immediately.
  • Standardised corporate menu tiers. Replace six bespoke negotiated menus with three fixed corporate tiers. This removes the labour bloat that is driving the head chef out while keeping the B2B revenue, and it is the change that makes everything above affordable.

Why this scales where the central kitchen alone did not. Each element restores a validated attribute at a cost that grows far more slowly than subscriber count: profile-based assembly is a data problem rather than a labour problem, the chef panel serves only the premium tier, and the corporate standardisation actually releases kitchen capacity. The company keeps the efficiency it gained and buys back the differentiation it lost, which is the difference between scaling value and scaling volume.

Question 4 · 5 marks

Using the Big Five assessment, explain how Nusrat Jahan's personality traits have contributed to both the early success and the current difficulties of TiffinTrail.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from The founder trait slot, drawing on the Big Five section of the Entrepreneurship lecture (Section 4). Use the three-link chain each time: trait and score, the behaviour it produces, the named outcome in the case. Give one upside and one downside per pairing, as the Fall 2025 model answer does.

Very high conscientiousness (90) with high neuroticism (79): meticulous execution that has hardened into control. The lecture treats conscientiousness as a two-sided trait, an early-stage asset that becomes a late-stage brake, and both sides are visible here. The upside is real: discipline and attention to detail are what produced a 96% on-time record with 14 independent home chefs, which is operationally difficult, and they are why an angel was willing to put BDT 3,000,000 into a first-time founder, since the lecture notes high discipline builds investor trust. The downside is that the same instinct, under scaling pressure, favours the option that is easiest to control. A single central kitchen with one fixed menu is the maximally controllable configuration and the least personalised one, so the trait that protected quality early is what destroyed the differentiation later. Neuroticism at 79 amplifies this, since the lecture identifies it as the most reliable negative predictor across all phases, producing excessive risk aversion: standardising is the low-variance choice, and variance is what a highly neurotic founder minimises.

Neuroticism (79) again, on the strategic decision. The behaviour the lecture predicts is indecisiveness under high-stakes pressure, and the case shows it directly. Three growth options are on the table and the investor has had to impose a five-month deadline to force a choice, which is the same pattern as the co-founder's 4.5-month ultimatum in the Fall 2025 case. The consequence is that the head chef is resigning now, while the structural decision that would relieve him is still pending.

Moderate agreeableness (62) with low-moderate extraversion (58): why the B2B bloat is not the usual story. This is worth a line because it distinguishes a real analysis from a memorised one. In the Fall 2025 case, very high agreeableness (92) explained an inability to refuse customised requests. Here agreeableness is only moderate, so the six bespoke corporate menus are better explained by conscientiousness and revenue anxiety, a determination to deliver exactly what was promised at signing rather than a reluctance to say no. The lecture also finds extraversion has no significant predictive power, so 58 explains nothing about performance, though it is consistent with a founder who removed the phone line and moved everything to an app.

Openness (71): moderate, and neither the problem nor the solution. The lecture describes openness as an initial catalyst that attracts early funding but shows zero correlation with scaling or exit success. At 71 it plausibly contributed to the original home-chef model, which was an unconventional idea, but it is not what the current situation turns on.

Answer in one line

The trait that built the early product and the trait that is now dismantling it are the same one: conscientiousness of 90, which delivered 96% reliability with 14 chefs and then chose the most controllable rather than the most valuable configuration at scale, with neuroticism of 79 turning that preference into paralysis over what to do next.

Question 5 · 5 marks

Recommend a structural or governance change for TiffinTrail, and explain what specific problems in the case it would address. Briefly state which of the three strategic options you would pair it with, and why.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from The structural slot, following the reasoning of Q5 in the Fall 2025 paper and combining it with the organic-versus-strategic growth argument (Business Growth lecture, Section 2). A recommendation should remove the founder from the bottleneck, install a decision-forcing mechanism, and let the founder keep doing what she is good at.

Recommendation 1: appoint a Head of Culinary Operations with genuine authority over the menu and the kitchen. This is the operational bottleneck the case has demonstrated. The head chef is resigning because six bespoke corporate menus sit on top of the standard rotation, and no one but the founder can refuse a client's menu request. A named executive owning menu architecture, with authority to standardise the corporate offering into three fixed tiers, removes the labour bloat at source and removes the founder from every kitchen decision. It also directly addresses the 41% quality complaint, since consistency at scale is a process discipline that needs a full-time owner rather than a founder's spare attention.

Recommendation 2: establish a formal Investment and Strategy Committee with the angel investor. The case shows a founder with neuroticism at 79 unable to commit to one of three growth paths, to the point where the investor has issued a five-month deadline. A standing committee converts that from a personal decision, which this founder finds hardest, into a scheduled institutional one with defined criteria and a date. That is a decision-forcing mechanism rather than a criticism of the founder, and it is the same instrument the Fall 2025 answer recommends for the same reason.

What this frees the founder to do. Her supply chain background and her conscientiousness are genuine assets, and the redesign should point them at logistics and reliability, where 78% on-time is the most fixable of the three churn drivers, rather than at menu negotiation and strategic agonising, where the same traits are doing damage. The principle is to redesign the role around the person's strengths, not to ask the person to become someone else.

Which strategic option to pair with it: the cloud-kitchen franchise, with a caveat, or venture funding if reliability is fixed first. Take the options in turn. The government school-meal tender should be rejected: 40,000 meals a day against a current base of roughly 61,400 meals a quarter is an order-of-magnitude leap requiring nine regional kitchens, and the company cannot hold quality in one kitchen today. The lecture's warning that risk rises with operational and market unfamiliarity applies at maximum force, and a ministry contract with daily compliance reporting is unforgiving of the exact failure mode TiffinTrail already has.

The franchise option is tempting on cash, BDT 9,600,000 in fees, which is organic-style funding requiring no equity, but it hands quality control to twelve third parties at the precise moment quality is the company's biggest problem. It is only defensible after the culinary standardisation above is complete, because a franchise can only replicate a process that already exists and works.

Venture funding is the option most aligned with the diagnosis, since demand forecasting and route optimisation attack the 78% on-time rate and the 11% food waste directly, but Question 6 shows that platform is already running at a CPI of about 0.73 with a forecast overrun of roughly BDT 1,500,000. Taking BDT 20,000,000 for 30% to accelerate a project that is failing on execution would be funding the problem rather than solving it. The sequenced answer is therefore: standardise the corporate menu and appoint the culinary head first, fix platform execution second, and only then choose between the franchise and the term sheet, which is the lecture's argument that organic capability should precede strategic growth commitments.

Question 6 · 5 marks · numerical

For the route-optimisation platform (BAC = BDT 4,000,000; 50% planned complete; 40% actually complete; BDT 2,200,000 spent), calculate PV, EV, CV, SV, CPI, SPI, EAC, ETC and VAC. State what the results mean for the investor conversation.

Show worked solution

  1. \( PV = BAC \times \%\text{planned} = 4{,}000{,}000 \times 0.50 = 2{,}000{,}000 \text{ BDT} \)

    Definition of planned value: the budgeted cost of work scheduled by this point.

  2. \( EV = BAC \times \%\text{actual} = 4{,}000{,}000 \times 0.40 = 1{,}600{,}000 \text{ BDT} \)

    Definition of earned value: completed work valued at the budgeted rate, not at what it actually cost.

  3. \( CV = EV - AC = 1{,}600{,}000 - 2{,}200{,}000 = -600{,}000 \text{ BDT} \)

    Cost variance. Negative, so over budget: BDT 1.6m of value was bought for BDT 2.2m of spending.

  4. \( SV = EV - PV = 1{,}600{,}000 - 2{,}000{,}000 = -400{,}000 \text{ BDT} \)

    Schedule variance. Negative, so behind schedule by BDT 400,000 worth of work.

  5. \( CPI = \dfrac{EV}{AC} = \dfrac{1{,}600{,}000}{2{,}200{,}000} = \dfrac{8}{11} \approx 0.727 \)

    Cost performance index. Divide numerator and denominator by 200,000 to get 8/11; keeping the fraction avoids rounding error in the EAC step.

  6. \( SPI = \dfrac{EV}{PV} = \dfrac{1{,}600{,}000}{2{,}000{,}000} = 0.80 \)

    Schedule performance index. Progressing at 80% of the planned rate, agreeing in sign with the negative SV.

  7. \( EAC = \dfrac{BAC}{CPI} = 4{,}000{,}000 \times \dfrac{11}{8} \)

    Estimate at completion. Dividing by 8/11 is multiplying by 11/8, which keeps the arithmetic exact. This assumes current cost efficiency continues.

  8. \( EAC = 5{,}500{,}000 \text{ BDT} \)

    \(4{,}000{,}000 \div 8 = 500{,}000\), then \(\times 11 = 5{,}500{,}000\). Above BAC, as required when CPI is below 1.

  9. \( ETC = EAC - AC = 5{,}500{,}000 - 2{,}200{,}000 = 3{,}300{,}000 \text{ BDT} \)

    Estimate to complete: what remains to be spent from today.

  10. \( VAC = BAC - EAC = 4{,}000{,}000 - 5{,}500{,}000 = -1{,}500{,}000 \text{ BDT} \)

    Variance at completion. Negative, so a forecast overrun of BDT 1.5 million, which is 37.5% above the approved budget.

Answer

PV 2,000,000 · EV 1,600,000 · CV −600,000 · SV −400,000 · CPI ≈ 0.73 · SPI 0.80 · EAC 5,500,000 · ETC 3,300,000 · VAC −1,500,000 (all BDT).

Sanity check: both variances negative, both indices below 1, EAC above BAC, VAC negative. All four agree, which is the internal consistency test.

What this means for the investor conversation. Three things need saying, and saying them first is better than being asked.

First, the platform is failing on execution, not on ambition: a CPI of 0.73 means only 73 paisa of budgeted value is being earned per taka spent, and an SPI of 0.80 means it is also running four-fifths of planned pace. The two together indicate a systematic estimation or capability problem rather than a one-off setback.

Second, the exposure is larger than it looks. BDT 2.2 million is already spent for 40% of the work, and the forecast says another BDT 3.3 million is required, which is more than the entire original budget minus what has been spent. The overrun is not behind the company, it is ahead of it.

Third, and most important for the negotiation, this changes what the BDT 20,000,000 term sheet is actually buying. If the platform completes at BDT 5.5 million rather than BDT 4 million on current performance, then part of the raise is silently funding an overrun rather than the 15-month build being pitched. The honest position is to present the EAC and VAC to the investor before signing, revise the platform baseline through formal change control, and either fix the delivery capability or reduce the platform's scope first. Raising equity to cover an unacknowledged 37.5% overrun is how a growth round turns into a bridge round twelve months later.

30 marks · 90 minutes. Weighted toward lean architecture, unit economics and EMV, the topics Fall 2025 left alone.

Question 1 · 5 marks

Identify the core failure in LedgerLite's product development approach and explain how it connects to the company's financial position.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from The diagnosis slot, but pointed at a software product rather than a service. It draws on the lean principle of eliminating waste and the Agile-versus-Lean comparison (Lean Architecture, Sections 2 and 4) together with the MVP misconception about features versus value (Prototypes & MVP, Section 6).

The core failure: output has been mistaken for outcome, so most engineering capacity has gone into waste. The company measures itself by features shipped, 47 in 18 months, and the founder cites the figure in every pitch. The analytics say six features carry 89% of usage and 19 are touched by fewer than 2% of paying accounts. By the lecture's first lean principle, anything that does not deliver measurable value to the end user is waste, so roughly 40% of the feature set is waste that was expensive to build and is now permanently expensive to maintain.

The mechanism, and why the cost keeps recurring. This is the traditional agile pattern the lecture describes in the food delivery case: batched feature delivery including low-value components, with high velocity and erratic value. The comparison table predicts exactly what follows, elevated bugs and technical debt from rapid uncoupled additions, and the case shows it, three production incidents a month at 4.2 hours each, six-hour manual deployments, and no automated test suite.     Multi-factor ratio model from the lecture. Write the formula first; it earns its own mark.

  2. \( F_{\text{size}} = \dfrac{27{,}000}{18{,}000} = 1.5 \)

    Size factor is the ratio of new to historical size, both in lines of code, so the units cancel. Above 1.0, correctly reflecting a larger system.

  3. \( F_{\text{complexity}} = 1.25 \)

    Read from the problem. High complexity exceeds the medium baseline of 1.0, so it raises cost.

  4. \( F_{\text{team}} = 0.85 \)

    The sign trap. A high-experience team scores below 1.0, exactly as in the lecture's own worked example, because experienced developers work faster and rework less. Writing 1.15 here would invert the whole answer.

  5. \( C_{\text{new}} = 1{,}200{,}000 \times 1.5 \times 1.25 \times 0.85 \)

    Substitute all four values into the model.

  6. \( 1{,}200{,}000 \times 1.5 = 1{,}800{,}000 \)

    Apply the size factor first: the cost of the larger system at the old complexity and team profile.

  7. \( 1{,}800{,}000 \times 1.25 = 2{,}250{,}000 \)

    Apply complexity to the running total. The model is multiplicative because each factor scales the whole estimate rather than adding to it.

  8. \( 2{,}250{,}000 \times 0.85 = 1{,}912{,}500 \)

    Apply the team factor last, reducing the estimate by 15% to reflect the stronger team.

Answer

\( C_{\text{new}} = \text{BDT } 1{,}912{,}500 \)

Sanity check: the combined multiplier is \(1.5 \times 1.25 \times 0.85 = 1.59375\), and \(1{,}200{,}000 \times 1.59375 = 1{,}912{,}500\). The direction is right: a system 50% larger and harder, built by a better team, should cost somewhat more than 1.5 times the original.

Comparison with the WBS estimate: compare like with like. This is where most students go wrong, so do it deliberately. The multi-factor ratio model estimates the work cost, because the historical BDT 1,200,000 it scales from was the cost of building the original platform, not that cost plus a reserve. The comparable figure from Question 3 is therefore the aggregated work cost of BDT 1,700,000, not the cost baseline of BDT 1,904,000. Setting the ratio estimate against the baseline would be comparing an estimate that contains no contingency with one that does, and it would produce a flattering agreement that is not real.

The actual gap. \( 1{,}912{,}500 - 1{,}700{,}000 = 212{,}500 \), which is about 12.5% above the bottom-up work estimate. That is a meaningful divergence and it is the useful output of running two methods: the top-down view says the team's package-by-package figures may be optimistic by roughly an eighth.

Which to believe. Neither automatically. The ratio model inherits whatever was true of the original booking platform, including any underestimation baked into it, and it assumes cost scales linearly with size, which understates integration and testing effort on a larger system. The WBS is decomposed and time-phased, so it can actually be used for control, which the ratio estimate cannot. The two are also not fully independent, since the people who built the WBS had the earlier project in mind.

Practical recommendation. Adopt the WBS-based baseline of BDT 1,904,000, because only a decomposed, time-phased estimate can serve as a control baseline, but treat the 12.5% divergence as a warning rather than noise. Note that the contingency of BDT 204,000 happens to cover most of the BDT 212,500 gap, so the baseline as a whole is defensible even if the underlying work estimate is light. The specific package to interrogate is integration and testing at BDT 210,000 out of BDT 1,700,000, roughly 12% of the work cost, which is where a linear size assumption most often breaks down.

Question 6 · 5 marks

The manufacturer's authorised service partnership would classify as which growth strategy? Recommend whether RepairKori should pursue it, and state one structural change the company needs regardless of that decision.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from The growth strategy framework and the diversification quadrants (Business Growth lecture, Sections 4 and 5), combined with the structural recommendation pattern. The classification here is genuinely arguable, so the marks are in the justification rather than the label.

Classification. The partnership is best read as market expansion, or market development: RepairKori's existing service, vetted repair delivered through its platform, is taken to a new customer segment, the manufacturer's in-warranty customers, whom it does not currently reach. A defensible alternative reading is vertical diversification, forward integration, on the grounds that becoming an authorised service arm moves the company along the manufacturer's value chain toward the end customer under the manufacturer's brand. Either classification earns credit if the reasoning names the change in product and market, which is what the framework actually turns on: the service is essentially unchanged, and the market is new, which is what makes market expansion the cleaner fit.

Recommendation: pursue it, but sequence it after the retention fix, and negotiate on volume rather than on rate.

Why pursue it. It attacks the exact weakness identified in Question 1. In-warranty work comes with a manufacturer relationship, standardised parts, defined procedures and a payer who is not the householder, so it is far harder to take off-platform: a technician cannot privately fulfil an authorised warranty repair. It converts a slice of revenue from the leaky repeat-business model into contracted volume. It also raises the switching cost against the ride-hailing entrants in factor 3, since an authorised partnership is a credential a well-funded newcomer cannot simply buy with subsidy.

Why sequence it carefully. Two constraints in the case argue against rushing. First, dispatch is still manual at 4,800 jobs a month, so absorbing a manufacturer's volume with no automation risks failing on service levels in front of the one partner whose standards are contractual. Second, technician churn at 31% means the supply side is unstable, and an authorised partnership commits RepairKori to coverage it may not be able to staff. The lecture's argument that organic capability should precede strategic growth applies directly: build the dispatch automation and stabilise technician retention, then take the partnership at a volume the company can actually serve.

Why negotiate on volume, not rate. Authorised service work typically pays less per job than open-market repair, so accepting it at a low rate to win the relationship would compress an already thin 9.5% net margin. The value here is retention and defensibility, not price, and the negotiation should be framed that way.

The structural change needed regardless: formalise the technician relationship and re-price repeat work. Introduce written agreements with a tiered commission that falls on repeat jobs, so a technician's economics improve on exactly the bookings they currently take off-platform, and appoint a Head of Supply or Technician Operations to own recruitment, retention and the contract framework. This addresses the founder's demonstrated blind spot, since the case shows agreeableness at 88 has produced a network held together entirely by personal goodwill, and it moves the company from relationships owned by a person to relationships owned by the company. Automating dispatch belongs in the same package, because it removes the founder from the daily operational path and lifts the volume ceiling that manual assignment imposes.

Drill set 01

Numerical drills: earned value

Four problems, rising in difficulty. Do them with a calculator and nothing else.

These are the fastest marks on any version of this paper. Aim to complete a full nine-measure calculation in under eight minutes. Before you start, write the formula block at the top of your page from memory: PV, EV, CV, SV, CPI, SPI, EAC, ETC, VAC. If you cannot, go back to Book 1 Topic 09 first.

Drill 1 · standard, over budget

BAC = BDT 2,000,000. Planned complete 75%. Actually complete 60%. Actual cost BDT 1,400,000. Calculate all nine measures and interpret.

Show worked solution

  1. \( PV = 2{,}000{,}000 \times 0.75 = 1{,}500{,}000 \)

    Planned value: BAC times planned percentage complete.

  2. \( EV = 2{,}000{,}000 \times 0.60 = 1{,}200{,}000 \)

    Earned value: BAC times actual percentage, valued at the budgeted rate.

  3. \( CV = 1{,}200{,}000 - 1{,}400{,}000 = -200{,}000 \)

    CV = EV − AC. Negative: over budget by BDT 200,000.

  4. \( SV = 1{,}200{,}000 - 1{,}500{,}000 = -300{,}000 \)

    SV = EV − PV. Negative: behind schedule by BDT 300,000 of work.

  5. \( CPI = \dfrac{1{,}200{,}000}{1{,}400{,}000} = \dfrac{6}{7} \approx 0.857 \)

    CPI = EV / AC. Cancel 200,000 from both to get 6/7 and keep the fraction for the EAC step.

  6. \( SPI = \dfrac{1{,}200{,}000}{1{,}500{,}000} = 0.80 \)

    SPI = EV / PV. Progressing at 80% of planned rate, agreeing in sign with the negative SV.

  7. \( EAC = 2{,}000{,}000 \times \dfrac{7}{6} \approx 2{,}333{,}333 \)

    EAC = BAC / CPI. Dividing by 6/7 is multiplying by 7/6, which keeps it exact.

  8. \( ETC = 2{,}333{,}333 - 1{,}400{,}000 \approx 933{,}333 \)

    ETC = EAC − AC: remaining forecast spend.

  9. \( VAC = 2{,}000{,}000 - 2{,}333{,}333 \approx -333{,}333 \)

    VAC = BAC − EAC. Forecast overrun of about BDT 333,000.

Answer

PV 1,500,000 · EV 1,200,000 · CV −200,000 · SV −300,000 · CPI ≈ 0.86 · SPI 0.80 · EAC ≈ 2,333,333 · ETC ≈ 933,333 · VAC ≈ −333,333.
Interpretation: over budget and behind schedule; roughly 0.86 BDT of value earned per taka spent, at 80% of planned pace, forecast to finish about BDT 333,000 above a BDT 2,000,000 budget. Note the schedule problem is proportionally worse than the cost problem here, so investigate delivery pace first.

Drill 2 · standard, under budget

BAC = BDT 800,000. Planned complete 30%. Actually complete 35%. Actual cost BDT 260,000. Calculate all nine measures.

Show worked solution

  1. \( PV = 800{,}000 \times 0.30 = 240{,}000 \)

    BAC times planned percentage.

  2. \( EV = 800{,}000 \times 0.35 = 280{,}000 \)

    BAC times actual percentage. EV exceeding PV is the first sign the project is ahead.

  3. \( CV = 280{,}000 - 260{,}000 = +20{,}000 \)

    CV = EV − AC. Positive: under budget.

  4. \( SV = 280{,}000 - 240{,}000 = +40{,}000 \)

    SV = EV − PV. Positive: ahead of schedule.

  5. \( CPI = \dfrac{280{,}000}{260{,}000} = \dfrac{14}{13} \approx 1.077 \)

    CPI = EV / AC. Above 1.0, cost efficient.

  6. \( SPI = \dfrac{280{,}000}{240{,}000} = \dfrac{7}{6} \approx 1.167 \)

    SPI = EV / PV. Running at about 117% of planned pace.

  7. \( EAC = 800{,}000 \times \dfrac{13}{14} \approx 742{,}857 \)

    EAC = BAC / CPI. Dividing by a CPI above 1 reduces the forecast, which is the check that the formula is the right way up.

  8. \( ETC = 742{,}857 - 260{,}000 \approx 482{,}857 \)

    ETC = EAC − AC.

  9. \( VAC = 800{,}000 - 742{,}857 \approx +57{,}143 \)

    VAC = BAC − EAC. Positive, forecasting an underrun.

PV 240,000 · EV 280,000 · CV +20,000 · SV +40,000 · CPI ≈ 1.08 · SPI ≈ 1.17 · EAC ≈ 742,857 · ETC ≈ 482,857 · VAC ≈ +57,143.
Both variances positive, both indices above 1, EAC below BAC, VAC positive. All four agree, which is what a healthy project looks like. Caution worth writing: only 35% is complete, so this favourable picture rests on early performance that may not hold.

Drill 3 · hard · reverse

A project reports SPI = 0.75 with planned value of BDT 600,000 at the review point. BAC is BDT 1,500,000 and BDT 520,000 has been spent. Find EV, the planned and actual completion percentages, CPI, EAC and VAC.

Show worked solution

  1. \( SPI = \dfrac{EV}{PV} \implies EV = SPI \times PV \)

    Rearrange the SPI definition by multiplying both sides by PV. SPI and PV are the two given values, so this is the only door into the problem.

  2. \( EV = 0.75 \times 600{,}000 = 450{,}000 \text{ BDT} \)

    Substitute. EV below PV is consistent with an SPI under 1.

  3. \( \%\text{planned} = \dfrac{PV}{BAC} = \dfrac{600{,}000}{1{,}500{,}000} = 40\% \)

    Rearrange the PV definition to isolate the planned percentage.

  4. \( \%\text{actual} = \dfrac{EV}{BAC} = \dfrac{450{,}000}{1{,}500{,}000} = 30\% \)

    Rearrange the EV definition. The project should be 40% done and is 30% done, which is what SPI = 0.75 means in plain terms.

  5. \( CPI = \dfrac{EV}{AC} = \dfrac{450{,}000}{520{,}000} = \dfrac{45}{52} \approx 0.865 \)

    CPI = EV / AC, now computable because EV was recovered in step 2.

  6. \( EAC = 1{,}500{,}000 \times \dfrac{52}{45} \approx 1{,}733{,}333 \text{ BDT} \)

    EAC = BAC / CPI, using the fraction to stay exact: \(1{,}500{,}000 \div 45 = 33{,}333.33\), then \(\times 52\).

  7. \( VAC = 1{,}500{,}000 - 1{,}733{,}333 \approx -233{,}333 \text{ BDT} \)

    VAC = BAC − EAC. Forecast overrun of roughly BDT 233,000.

Answer

EV 450,000 · planned 40% · actual 30% · CPI ≈ 0.87 · EAC ≈ 1,733,333 · VAC ≈ −233,333 (all BDT).
Worth adding: at only 30% complete, ETC is about BDT 1,213,333, more than twice what has been spent so far, so almost the entire exposure lies ahead. This is the point in Book 1 Topic 09, Question 9.3 about early-project CPI carrying more remaining risk than the same CPI late on.

Drill 4 · interpretation only

A project reports CPI = 1.05 and SPI = 0.85. Without any further figures, describe what is happening and what a project manager should investigate. Would you expect EAC to be above or below BAC?

Show model answer
What this tests Whether you can read the indices as a pattern rather than compute them. Mixed signals like this one are the most common real-world case and a favourite short-answer question, because the two indices point in opposite directions and the naive reading of each cancels out.

What is happening. The project is cost efficient but slow. A CPI of 1.05 means 1.05 BDT of budgeted value is being earned for every 1.00 BDT spent, so the work completed cost less than it was budgeted to cost. An SPI of 0.85 means work is progressing at only 85% of the planned rate, so less has been completed than should have been by now.

The most likely explanation, and it is not good news. The commonest cause of this exact pattern is under-resourcing. If the project is running with fewer people than planned, or people are shared with other work, spending falls below plan, which flatters CPI, while output falls behind plan, which depresses SPI. The apparent cost efficiency is then an artefact of not doing the work rather than of doing it cheaply, and it will disappear the moment the team is brought up to strength to recover the schedule.

What to investigate, in order. First, staffing against plan: are the budgeted people actually assigned and working full time on this project? Second, whether the delay sits in one work package or is spread across all of them, since a single blocked dependency has a different remedy from a general pace problem. Third, whether the reported completion percentage is honest, because an understated percentage would depress SPI and CPI together, and here CPI is high, so that is unlikely but worth confirming. Fourth, whether quality has been traded for cost, since skipped testing lowers AC now and raises it later.

EAC relative to BAC. Using the lecture's formula \(EAC = BAC / CPI\), with CPI at 1.05 the EAC comes out below BAC, forecasting an underrun of roughly 4.8%. That is the answer the formula gives, and you should say so. But it is worth adding the caveat, because it earns the mark that separates a mechanical answer from an understanding one: the formula uses cost performance only and ignores schedule entirely, so it does not capture the cost of recovering the delay. If the team is expanded or overtime is authorised to bring SPI back toward 1.0, CPI will fall and the forecast underrun will evaporate. A schedule problem usually becomes a cost problem the moment someone tries to fix it.

Drill set 02

Numerical drills: EMV and cost estimation

Six shorter problems. These take three to five minutes each.

Drill 5 · EMV, standard

Calculate the total EMV. Vendor hardware delay: threat, P 0.40, impact BDT 250,000. Client scope change: threat, P 0.55, impact BDT 600,000. Early beta adoption bonus: opportunity, P 0.30, impact BDT 900,000. Key staff illness: threat, P 0.15, impact BDT 200,000.

Show worked solution

  1. \( \text{EMV} = \sum (P_i \times I_i) \)

    State the formula before touching the numbers.

  2. \( I: -250{,}000,\; -600{,}000,\; +900{,}000,\; -200{,}000 \)

    Apply signs first. Threats negative, opportunities positive, per the lecture's definition. Only the beta bonus is positive.

  3. \( 0.40 \times (-250{,}000) = -100{,}000 \)

    Hardware delay risk score.

  4. \( 0.55 \times (-600{,}000) = -330{,}000 \)

    Scope change risk score, the largest threat contribution on both probability and impact.

  5. \( 0.30 \times (+900{,}000) = +270{,}000 \)

    Opportunity risk score.

  6. \( 0.15 \times (-200{,}000) = -30{,}000 \)

    Staff illness risk score, smallest on both factors.

  7. \( \text{EMV} = -100{,}000 - 330{,}000 + 270{,}000 - 30{,}000 = -190{,}000 \)

    Sum the column. Threats total −460,000, offset by +270,000.

Answer

EMV = −BDT 190,000. Net expected loss, so a contingency reserve of at least that order should be built into the baseline. The scope change risk alone accounts for BDT 330,000 of exposure, so mitigation effort belongs there first, most practically through a written change control procedure agreed with the client at contract stage.

Drill 6 · EMV, decision

A company must choose between building a component in-house or buying it. Build: 60% chance of success saving BDT 1,200,000; 40% chance of failure costing BDT 800,000. Buy: 85% chance of success saving BDT 700,000; 15% chance of failure costing BDT 300,000. Calculate both EMVs and recommend.

Show worked solution

  1. \( \text{EMV}_{\text{build}} = (0.60 \times 1{,}200{,}000) + (0.40 \times -800{,}000) \)

    Apply the formula to the two branches. The probabilities sum to 1.0, confirming the outcomes are exhaustive.

  2. \( = 720{,}000 - 320{,}000 = +400{,}000 \text{ BDT} \)

    Multiply each branch and sum.

  3. \( \text{EMV}_{\text{buy}} = (0.85 \times 700{,}000) + (0.15 \times -300{,}000) \)

    Same method for the second option.

  4. \( = 595{,}000 - 45{,}000 = +550{,}000 \text{ BDT} \)

    Multiply and sum.

  5. \( 550{,}000 - 400{,}000 = 150{,}000 \text{ BDT} \)

    Quantify the gap so the recommendation has a magnitude attached to it.

Answer

Build = +BDT 400,000, Buy = +BDT 550,000. Recommend buying, better by BDT 150,000 on expected value and also lower variance, since it fails only 15% of the time against 40%. Here EMV and risk appetite point the same way, which is unusual and worth noting; where they conflict, a single unsurvivable downside outweighs a higher average.

Drill 7 · EMV into cost baseline

A project has aggregated work package costs of BDT 5,000,000. Its risk register lists: data centre migration failure (P 0.20, impact BDT 1,500,000), third-party API deprecation (P 0.35, impact BDT 400,000), regulatory fine (P 0.10, impact BDT 2,000,000). All are threats. Calculate the EMV and use it to set the contingency reserve and cost baseline.

Show worked solution

  1. \( 0.20 \times (-1{,}500{,}000) = -300{,}000 \)

    Migration failure risk score. All three impacts are negative because all three are threats.

  2. \( 0.35 \times (-400{,}000) = -140{,}000 \)

    API deprecation risk score: most likely event, but modest impact.

  3. \( 0.10 \times (-2{,}000{,}000) = -200{,}000 \)

    Regulatory fine risk score: largest impact, lowest probability.

  4. \( \text{EMV} = -300{,}000 - 140{,}000 - 200{,}000 = -640{,}000 \text{ BDT} \)

    Sum. With threats only, the total must be negative.

  5. \( \text{Contingency reserve} = 640{,}000 \text{ BDT} \)

    Sizing the reserve from the expected exposure. Contingency covers known unknowns, and these three risks are exactly that: identified and quantified, so the total EMV is the natural size for the reserve. Note the honest caveat: neither lecture states this link explicitly, the cost lecture is silent on how the reserve is sized and the risk lecture only says to allocate contingency funds to protect ROI, so this is standard practice joining the two topics rather than a formula the faculty gave.

  6. \( \text{Cost Baseline} = 5{,}000{,}000 + 640{,}000 = 5{,}640{,}000 \text{ BDT} \)

    Step 4 of the baseline procedure: aggregated work cost plus contingency reserves, still excluding management reserves.

Answer

EMV = −BDT 640,000. Contingency reserve = BDT 640,000. Cost baseline = BDT 5,640,000 (before time-phasing).

The point of this drill is that the two lectures connect: EMV need not be an isolated calculation, it is the usual way a contingency reserve in the cost baseline gets its number. A management reserve would then be added on top to give the project budget, covering the unknown unknowns that no register can list.

One caveat worth a mark. A reserve set at exactly the EMV covers the average outcome. If the regulatory fine actually occurs, it alone costs BDT 2,000,000, more than three times the whole reserve, so an organisation that cannot absorb that should either buy insurance against it, treat it separately outside the reserve, or fund at a higher confidence level than the mean.

Drill 8 · single-factor ratio

A similar past project cost BDT 750,000. The new project's scope is assessed as 20% smaller. Estimate the new cost, and state one condition under which this estimate would be unsafe.

Show worked solution

  1. \( E_{\text{new}} = E_{\text{old}} \times F \)

    Single-factor ratio model from the lecture.

  2. \( F = 1 - 0.20 = 0.80 \)

    Convert the percentage to a factor. 20% smaller means 80% of the original, so F is below 1.0 and will reduce the estimate. Compare the lecture's example, where 25% larger gave F = 1.25.

  3. \( E_{\text{new}} = 750{,}000 \times 0.80 = 600{,}000 \text{ BDT} \)

    Substitute and multiply.

Answer

BDT 600,000. Sanity check: it is smaller than the historical cost, which is the right direction for a reduced scope.

When this would be unsafe. The model assumes cost scales proportionally with scope, which breaks down when a large share of the cost is fixed rather than variable. If BDT 300,000 of the original project went on environment setup, architecture and a licence that the new project also needs in full, then cutting scope by 20% cuts only the variable half, and the true estimate is closer to \(300{,}000 + (450{,}000 \times 0.8) = 660{,}000\). A second unsafe condition is that the two projects are not genuinely comparable, since the whole method rests on the historical project being a valid analogue.

Drill 9 · multi-factor ratio

  1. \( C_{\text{new}} = C_{\text{old}} \times F_{\text{size}} \times F_{\text{complexity}} \times F_{\text{team}} \)

    State the model first.

  2. \( F_{\text{size}} = \dfrac{15{,}000}{12{,}000} = 1.25 \)

    Size factor: ratio of new to old size. Above 1.0, raising cost.

  3. \( F_{\text{complexity}} = 0.90 \)

    Given. Below 1.0, because low complexity is easier than the medium baseline and therefore cheaper. This is the reverse of the usual case, so read the wording carefully.

  4. \( F_{\text{team}} = 1.15 \)

    Given. Above 1.0, because a low-experience team is slower and produces more rework. Note this is the opposite direction from the lecture's example, where a high-experience team scored 0.85.

  5. \( 420{,}000 \times 1.25 = 525{,}000 \)

  6. \( 525{,}000 \times 0.90 = 472{,}500 \)

BDT 543,375. Sanity check: combined multiplier \(1.25 \times 0.90 \times 1.15 = 1.29375\), and \(420{,}000 \times 1.29375 = 543{,}375\). The three factors partly cancel, so a project 25% larger ends up only about 29% more expensive, because the easier work offsets the weaker team.

Twenty ways to lose marks

Read this the night before. Most of these are worth one or two marks each.

Method errors

  1. Restating the case instead of interpreting it. "In Year 1 they had 120 subscribers and by Year 3 they had 610" earns nothing. Every case fact must be attached to a claim.
  2. Naming a concept without a mechanism. "This is a value proposition failure" is a label. The marks are in how the failure produced the observed numbers.
  3. Skipping the exclusions in a KPI question. The faculty's own model answers always say what was rejected and why. Four selections with no exclusions is an incomplete answer.
  4. Only criticising the founder in a Big Five question. Give one upside and one downside per trait pairing. The traits built the business before they broke it.
  5. Writing an essay when four labelled sentences would do. Five marks is roughly 150 to 200 words. Length is not the currency; links are.
  6. Leaving a question blank because time ran out. A three-sentence skeleton scores two or three; a blank scores zero. Budget the clock and stop when the time is up.

Content errors

  1. Writing "entrepreneurs are risk takers". The lecture argues the opposite: founders are motivated by risk mitigation, and overconfident risk-tolerant founders perform worse.
  2. Praising a charismatic founder's extraversion. The lecture states extraversion has no statistical predictive power. Saying so is the mark.
  3. Calling high conscientiousness simply good. It is two-sided: an early asset, a scaling brake.
  4. Describing an MVP as a cut-down or half-finished product. It is the simplest complete solution delivering real utility at every stage.
  5. Proposing "build a simple version of the app" as a prototype. The lecture's exemplary MVP wrote no software at all.
  6. Filing an internal weakness under threats in SWOT. A small budget or a missing skill is internal, therefore a weakness.
  7. Confusing concentric with horizontal diversification. Concentric shares the technology; horizontal shares the customer but not the technology.
  8. Treating "defer commitment" and "deliver fast" as contradictory. One governs irreversible decisions, the other reversible increments.
  9. Forgetting that opportunity cost is the next best alternative, not the sum of everything foregone.

Calculation errors

  1. Losing the sign on an opportunity in EMV. The single most common numerical error in the course. Write the signed impact column before multiplying anything.
  2. Computing EV from actual cost rather than BAC. EV always uses the budgeted rate: \(EV = BAC \times \%\text{actual}\).
  3. Inverting the team experience factor. A strong team is below 1.0. Writing 1.15 for high experience inverts the answer.
  4. Putting management reserves inside the cost baseline. Baseline holds contingency only; the budget holds both.
  5. Giving a number with no interpretation. Every calculated answer needs a sentence saying what it means for the project. In a five-mark question the interpretation is usually worth one or two of them on its own.
The two checks that catch most slips

Earned value: CV and CPI must agree in direction, and so must SV and SPI. If CV is negative, CPI must be below 1. If they disagree, you have swapped a formula.
EMV: count the threats and opportunities before you sum. Mostly threats should give a negative total; a dominant opportunity should give a positive one. If the sign surprises you, find the lost minus.

Last-minute cheat sheet

Earned value, all nine

\( PV = BAC \times \%p \), \( EV = BAC \times \%a \)
\( CV = EV-AC \), \( SV = EV-PV \)
\( CPI = EV/AC \), \( SPI = EV/PV \)
\( EAC = BAC/CPI \), \( ETC = EAC-AC \), \( VAC = BAC-EAC \)

Reading the indices

Below 1 is bad. CPI 0.82 = 82 paisa of value per taka. Negative VAC = forecast overrun. CV must agree in sign with CPI.

EMV

\( \text{EMV} = \sum (P_i \times I_i) \). Signs before multiplying. Sets the contingency reserve. It is an average, never a prediction.

Cost estimation

\( E_{new}=E_{old} \times F \)
\( C_{new}=C_{old} \times F_{size} \times F_{cx} \times F_{team} \)
Strong team = 0.85, weak team > 1.0

Budget structure

Work packages + contingency = cost baseline. Baseline + management reserve = project budget. Judge performance against the baseline.

Reserves

Contingency = known unknowns, inside the baseline. Management = unknown unknowns, outside it.

Big Five verdicts

Neuroticism: always harmful, most reliable negative predictor. Conscientiousness: helps early, hinders at scale. Openness: early cheques only. Agreeableness: early rapport only. Extraversion: nothing.

Big Five answer chain

Trait + score → behaviour it produces → named consequence in the case. One upside and one downside per pairing.

MVP

Leanest functional version giving maximal validated learning at minimal engineering effort. Skateboard, not a wheel. More content is not more value.

Lean seven

Eliminate waste · build quality in · create knowledge · defer commitment · deliver fast · respect people · optimise the whole.

Lean four steps

Value stream mapping → just-in-time design → automated quality and feedback → evolutionary design.

KPI selection recipe

Pick four. Justify each by the strategic question it answers. Exclude the rest with reasons: volume without quality, internal input, no link to outcome, input not output.

KPI test

Can this number rise while the business gets worse? If yes, it is a metric, not a KPI.

Growth strategies

Existing product + existing market = penetration. Existing + new market = expansion. New product + existing market = product development. New + new = diversification.

Diversification quadrants

Concentric = related tech. Horizontal = unrelated tech, same customers. Vertical = up or down the chain. Conglomerate = unrelated everything.

SWOT

Strengths and weaknesses internal. Opportunities and threats external. Small budget = weakness. Rival entering = threat.

Cost categories

Direct, indirect, opportunity (next best alternative only), risk-related (reserves and insurance).

The four moves

Name the concept → anchor it in a case fact → explain the mechanism → state the consequence. About 150 words.
