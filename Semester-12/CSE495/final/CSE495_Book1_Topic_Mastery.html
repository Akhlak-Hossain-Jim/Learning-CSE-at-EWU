Orientation

How this exam actually works

Read this first. It changes how you study everything else.

Before you learn a single definition, understand what your faculty does with them. I read the Fall 2025 final paper carefully, and it is not a paper that rewards memorising. It gives you one long business scenario, roughly a page and a half of it, and then asks five questions worth five marks each. The header on the Fall 2025 paper reads "30 (5 questions × 5 marks + case study)", so five marks sit with the case itself rather than with a sixth question; the mock papers in Book 2 use six five-mark items instead, purely so they can cover more of the syllabus in one sitting. Every question hands you the scenario and expects you to walk into it carrying a framework from lecture.

That distinction matters enormously for a three-day revision window. If you spend those days memorising the seven principles of lean architecture as a list, you will be able to recite them and still score badly, because nobody will ask you to recite them. What gets asked is: here is a company falling apart in a specific way, which lecture idea explains the collapse, and what would you change?

The pattern, decoded

SkillBridge Academy (Fall 2025) was a case about an online course business that grew from 200 students to 18,000 and lost its soul in the process. The five questions that followed were, in order:

Q1. Diagnose the core business model failure behind falling retention.
Q2. Pick the real KPIs out of a pile of numbers and justify each choice.
Q3. Analyse the MVP evolution and rebuild the value proposition.
Q4. Connect the founder's Big Five scores to concrete business outcomes.
Q5. Recommend a structural or governance change.

Look at what that sequence is doing. It is a diagnosis, then a measurement question, then a product question, then a people question, then an organisation question. It walks down the same ladder a real consultant would walk down. And each rung maps to a different lecture:

Question slotLecture it draws onWhat you must produce
Q1 DiagnosisPrototypes & MVP + Business GrowthName the failure, then explain the mechanism
Q2 KPIsKey Performance Indicators4 chosen with reasons, plus what you rejected
Q3 ProductPrototypes & MVP + LeanA rebuilt value proposition that scales
Q4 FounderEntrepreneurship (Big Five)Trait → behaviour → business consequence
Q5 StructureGrowth + governanceA named role or body, and what it fixes
Do not assume the pattern repeats exactly

Three of your lecture files got no direct question in Fall 2025: Cost Analysis (earned value, estimation models), Risk Management (SWOT, EMV), and Lean Architecture. Those are the three most calculation-heavy and most definable topics in the course, which makes them the most likely candidates for a separate question this time, or for a numerical part bolted onto the case. Prepare them properly. A five-mark EVA calculation is the cheapest five marks on any paper if you know the formulas, and the most expensive if you do not.

What a five-mark answer looks like

Five marks in ninety minutes across five questions plus reading the case means roughly fourteen minutes per question once you have spent ten minutes reading the case, and a couple of those go on thinking. You are writing maybe 150 to 200 words. That is short. It is not an essay. Look at the model answers in the Fall 2025 paper: most of them are two labelled points, each stating a concept and then attaching it to a specific fact from the case. The exception is Q2, the KPI question, which lists four selections plus an exclusions line, and that shape is specific to KPI questions.

Take Q4's model answer. It says "High Agreeableness (92) and Openness (88)" (that is the concept, with the number quoted), then "helped build early customer trust and community warmth" (that is the upside), then "but created an inability to say no to customized B2B requests, causing operational bloat and instructor burnout" (that is the downside, and it points at named facts from the case: the B2B customisation, the instructor resigning). Concept, evidence, consequence. Three moves, twice over, and you are done.

Key idea

Every mark on this paper comes from a link, not from a fact. Stating "high neuroticism is harmful" earns nothing. Stating "her neuroticism of 68 shows up as the 4.5-month paralysis over choosing between the three growth options" earns the mark, because you connected a lecture concept to a case fact.

Topic 11 of this guide turns that into a repeatable sentence template you can run on any question. If you only have one hour tonight, read Topic 11 (the answer engine) and Topic 06 (KPIs), because those two carry the most predictable marks.

Topic 01

Entrepreneurship: definitions and myths

The foundation lecture. Its definitions turn up inside answers to other questions.

Start with a question that sounds naive but is not: what actually separates an entrepreneur from someone who runs a shop? Both take money in, both take risks, both can fail. Your faculty's definition draws the line at a particular place, and the line is worth understanding because the exam sometimes turns on it.

The faculty's definition

Entrepreneurship is the creation or extraction of economic value in ways that generally entail beyond the minimal amount of risk assumed by a traditional business, and potentially involving values beyond purely economic returns.

Unpack that phrase by phrase, because every clause is doing work. "Creation or extraction of economic value" means you either make something new that has worth, or you find worth that was sitting there unnoticed and pull it out. Both count. "Beyond the minimal amount of risk assumed by a traditional business" is the dividing line: a grocery shop that copies the shop next door takes the ordinary risk of trading, while a venture doing something not yet proven takes more than that. And "values beyond purely economic returns" leaves the door open for social ventures, where the return is partly measured in impact rather than taka.

The entrepreneur, in the same lecture, is the individual who creates or invests in one or more businesses, bearing the majority of the risks and reaping the majority of the rewards. Note the symmetry there, because it is examinable: risk and reward sit on the same person. That is precisely what distinguishes the entrepreneur from the intrapreneur, who innovates inside a company using the company's money and therefore carries far less personal downside.

Quote worth remembering

"Rather than taking huge risks, what characterizes the entrepreneurial mindset is a willingness to bet on yourself." — Neil Kane, Burgess Institute for Entrepreneurship & Innovation, Michigan State University. Your faculty put this on the slide deliberately, because it sets up the myth-busting section that follows.

The myths, and what the research actually says

There is a popular story about founders: they quit a good job on a Tuesday, burn the boats, and win because they were brave enough to risk everything. Your lecture spends real time dismantling this, which tells you the faculty cares about it.

The myth of risking it all. Founders rarely walk away from secure, thriving jobs on a whim. Sorenson's work, cited in your lecture, finds that many ventures start from unemployment, from dissatisfaction with a current job, or from genuine conviction in a workable idea. The motivation the research keeps finding is risk mitigation: people start ventures partly to protect themselves from losing income or standing, not to gamble those things away. That is close to the opposite of the popular story.

Two extra findings from the same section that make good one-line additions to an answer. Female entrepreneurs are notably driven by social impact alongside financial viability. And a focus purely on financial gain, with no genuine user pain point being solved, is one of the leading indicators of startup failure. Hold on to that second one, because it is the seed of the whole MVP lecture later.

Confidence versus overconfidence. Here the finding is blunt: overconfident and overly risk-tolerant founders produce less profitable businesses on average and fail more often. Being sure of yourself is not the asset people assume it is. Meanwhile more than 80% of small business owners deal with imposter syndrome, and the ones who succeed handle it by delegating and by bringing in people whose skills fill their gaps rather than by pretending the doubt is not there.

Watch out

Students routinely write "entrepreneurs are risk takers" in an exam answer and lose marks, because the lecture argues the more precise position: entrepreneurs accept above-minimal risk while actively working to mitigate it. Reckless risk tolerance correlates with worse outcomes. If a case gives you a founder making bold uncalculated bets, that is a weakness to flag, not a strength to praise.

The six types of entrepreneurship

Your faculty lists six. In a case question you will usually be asked to classify a venture or to argue that it is drifting from one type toward another, so learn them by their core focus rather than by their examples.

The six types are not a ranking. Imitator entrepreneurship is not a lesser form of scalable startup; it trades innovation risk for execution risk deliberately. In a case answer, the interesting move is usually to point out that a venture is positioned as one type but behaving like another. SkillBridge, for instance, raised money and talked like a scalable startup while its B2B arm behaved like a small consulting business, and that mismatch is exactly what caused the burnout.

Question 1.1 · easy · 3 marks

Define entrepreneurship using the definition given in lecture, and explain what the phrase "beyond the minimal amount of risk" is meant to exclude.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from A straight recall-plus-interpretation question on the opening definition from the Entrepreneurship lecture, Section 1. The examiner is checking that you can not only reproduce the definition but say why the risk clause is in there. The connection wanted is between the definition and the boundary it draws against ordinary business.

Definition. Entrepreneurship is the creation or extraction of economic value in ways that generally entail beyond the minimal amount of risk assumed by a traditional business, and potentially involving values beyond purely economic returns.

What the risk clause excludes. Every business carries some baseline risk simply from trading: customers may not come, costs may rise, a supplier may fail. That is the minimal risk a traditional business accepts, and by itself it does not make an activity entrepreneurial. The clause draws the boundary at ventures that take on additional, uncertain risk, typically because they are doing something unproven, entering an unserved market, or building a model nobody has validated yet. So a shop opening on a street where four similar shops already trade profitably sits below that line, while a venture testing whether a market exists at all sits above it.

The final clause. The phrase about values beyond purely economic returns exists so the definition covers social entrepreneurship, where part of the return is measured as impact rather than profit (this connects to the six types listed later in the same lecture).

Question 1.2 · medium · 5 marks

A founder tells an investor: "I quit a stable job at a bank, put my entire savings into this venture, and I have never once doubted the idea. That is what real entrepreneurship takes." Using evidence from the entrepreneurship lecture, evaluate this claim.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from This tests the Myths of Entrepreneurship and Empirical Realities section (Entrepreneurship lecture, Section 2). The examiner has planted three separate myths in one quotation and wants to see whether you can identify each and correct it with the research the lecture cited. Notice the exam trick: the statement sounds admirable, so a student who is not paying attention will agree with it.

The claim is wrong on all three of its components, and the lecture gives specific evidence against each.

1. On "risking it all". The lecture cites Sorenson's finding that founders rarely abandon secure, thriving jobs on a whim. Ventures frequently begin from unemployment, from dissatisfaction with existing employment, or from conviction in a viable idea. More importantly, the studies find founders are predominantly motivated by risk mitigation, protecting themselves against loss of income or prestige, rather than by an appetite for reckless risk. So dramatic self-sacrifice is not the defining feature the founder claims it is.

2. On "never once doubted". This is the overconfidence problem the lecture treats directly: empirical research shows overconfident and overly risk-tolerant founders produce less profitable businesses on average and experience higher failure rates. Total absence of doubt is a warning sign to an investor, not a credential. The lecture adds that over 80% of small business owners experience imposter syndrome, and that successful founders manage it by delegating and partnering with complementary talent, which means self-doubt handled well is compatible with success.

3. On what actually predicts success. Neil Kane's line from the lecture reframes it: the entrepreneurial mindset is "a willingness to bet on yourself", not a willingness to take huge risks. And the lecture warns separately that pursuing financial gain without solving a genuine user pain point is a leading indicator of failure. The founder's pitch says nothing at all about a customer problem, which is the actual gap in it.

Answer in one line

The statement mistakes three known myths for virtues; the research in lecture associates all three with worse outcomes, and the pitch omits the one thing that does predict survival, a real customer pain point.

Question 1.3 · medium · 5 marks

Classify each of the following into one of the six types of entrepreneurship, and justify each classification in one sentence.

(a) A team inside Robi Axiata builds a new digital wallet feature using company engineers and budget.
(b) Two graduates launch a Bangla-language version of a proven Indonesian food-delivery model.
(c) A rural NGO builds a solar-panel leasing business that breaks even while electrifying 400 villages.
(d) A software engineer builds a cloud infrastructure product, raises a seed round, and targets 40% month-on-month growth.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from Direct application of the Types of Entrepreneurship table (Entrepreneurship lecture, Section 3). The examiner wants classification plus the distinguishing feature, not just the label. A one-word answer scores about half.

(a) Intrapreneurship. The innovation happens inside an established corporation, using existing corporate resources and budget, so the individuals carry little personal financial risk. This is the structure the lecture illustrates with Google's 20% time producing Gmail and 3M producing Post-it notes.

(b) Imitator entrepreneurship. The model is already proven elsewhere, so the founders are deliberately trading away innovation risk in exchange for execution and localisation risk, exactly the pattern the lecture describes for regional copycat applications and early Alibaba.

(c) Social entrepreneurship. The venture is mission driven and produces a blended return, social and environmental impact alongside enough financial sustainability to break even, matching the lecture's description and its Grameen Bank example.

(d) Scalable startup. It is technology driven, dependent on external venture funding, and pursuing rapid growth with large-scale returns in view, the lecture's Uber and Stripe pattern. The growth-rate target is the giveaway: small businesses do not set month-on-month growth goals.

Trap in this question

(c) tempts you toward "small business" because it is local and modest in scale. Scale is not the criterion. The criterion is whether the mission is the point, and here it is.

Question 1.4 · hard · 5 marks

A venture launched as a scalable startup now earns 60% of its revenue from bespoke consulting projects delivered by its two most senior engineers. Using the types of entrepreneurship from lecture, explain what has happened to this business and why it threatens the original funding thesis.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from This is the exam's favourite move: it takes the Types of Entrepreneurship table and asks you to detect a drift between types rather than to classify a static case. It is exactly the diagnosis behind SkillBridge's B2B problem in the Fall 2025 paper, so treat this as direct rehearsal for Q1 of the real thing.

What has happened. The venture has drifted from scalable startup behaviour toward small business behaviour without anyone deciding to. The lecture defines a scalable startup by rapid growth, technology-driven delivery, and large-scale returns; it defines a small business by dependence on personal skill and limited scalability. Bespoke consulting delivered by two named senior engineers is definitionally the second: revenue is bound to specific people's hours, so doubling revenue requires roughly doubling their hours, which cannot be done.

Why it threatens the funding thesis. Venture funding is priced on the assumption of non-linear growth, which is only available when the product, not the person, does the delivering. Once 60% of revenue is labour-bound, the growth ceiling is the size of the senior engineering team, and margin falls as headcount rises. The investor bought exposure to a scalable startup and now owns a consultancy with a startup's cost base.

The second-order risk. The lecture's remark that entrepreneurs bear the majority of risk and reward becomes uncomfortable here: the two engineers now hold the revenue, so their resignation is an existential event rather than a staffing problem. This is the same mechanism that put SkillBridge's lead instructor at the centre of its crisis.

Topic 02

The Big Five and founder behaviour

This was Q4 on the last paper. Expect it again in some form.

Personality psychology has, after decades of argument, mostly settled on five dimensions that describe how people differ. They are not types you belong to, they are sliders you sit somewhere along. Your lecture takes those five and asks a sharper question than a psychology course would: which of them actually predict whether a startup raises money, grows, and exits?

The answer is counterintuitive, and that is exactly why it is examinable.

The five traits, defined

Learn these as behaviours, not adjectives, because in a case you will be shown behaviour and asked to name the trait.

  1. Openness to experience. Intellectual curiosity, creativity, seeking novelty, thinking unconventionally. In a founder this looks like generating new product ideas constantly and being drawn to unproven approaches.
  2. Conscientiousness. Organisation, discipline, reliability, structured planning, attention to detail. Looks like careful processes, clean books, thorough documentation, and things being done on time.
  3. Extraversion. Sociability, assertiveness, outgoing nature, drawing energy from being around people. Looks like heavy networking, comfort on stage, front-of-room presence.
  4. Agreeableness. Empathy, trustworthiness, cooperativeness, warmth. Looks like strong relationships, a pleasant culture, and a marked reluctance to refuse requests.
  5. Neuroticism. Emotional volatility, susceptibility to stress, anxiety, self-doubt. Looks like panic under pressure, indecision at high stakes, and avoidance of irreversible choices.
The one that matters most

Neuroticism is the single most reliable negative predictor across every startup phase. Not one of several. The most reliable. High neuroticism produces panic under high-stakes pressure, unconvincing pitches, excessive risk aversion, and indecisiveness. If a case gives you a founder with a high neuroticism score, that is where the examiner has hidden the mark.

What the evidence says about each trait

Your lecture evaluates the traits against four concrete startup milestones: raising initial funding, the size of that initial funding, the number of investors in the syndicate, and ultimate exit through IPO or acquisition. That framing matters, because a trait can help at one milestone and do nothing at another.

Neuroticism, consistently harmful. Covered above. Harmful at every stage, no redeeming phase.

Conscientiousness, two-sided. This is the trait that reverses. Early on, high discipline is a real asset: it builds investor trust, produces structured execution, and keeps resource allocation lean when there is very little to allocate. Later, in the scaling phase, extreme structure becomes a brake. It resists rapid pivoting, resists "breaking things", and slows adaptability in chaotic hypergrowth. Same trait, same person, opposite sign, depending on stage.

Openness, an initial catalyst only. It attracts early angel cheques, because visionary narratives are persuasive when there is no data yet to argue with. But the lecture is explicit that it shows zero correlation with long-term scaling or exit success. Vision gets you the first cheque and does nothing for the last one.

Agreeableness, early rapport only. Helps with early stakeholder rapport and initial fundraising. No statistical advantage for scaling or exits. And in a case, high agreeableness usually shows up as a specific failure mode: an inability to say no.

Extraversion, no predictive power at all. The lecture flags this as directly contradicting common intuition. The charismatic, outgoing founder archetype has no significant impact on performance, funding scale, or exit viability. If a question asks you to explain a surprising research finding in this lecture, this is almost certainly the one.

Watch out

Do not write "high openness is good" or "high agreeableness is good". The lecture's whole point is that most traits are stage-dependent or inert, and only neuroticism is unambiguous. An answer that praises four traits and criticises one has missed the argument.

How to turn a score into a business consequence

This is the actual skill Q4 tests, so here is the mechanism. A score on its own is not an answer. You need a three-link chain:

The answer chain for any Big Five question

Trait + score → the specific behaviour it produces → the named consequence visible in the case

Watch it run on the Fall 2025 paper. Fatima Rahman scored Agreeableness 92 and Openness 88. Trait and score: very high agreeableness with high openness. Behaviour: warmth and receptiveness to every request, so she said yes to customised corporate training over and over. Consequence, named in the case: operational bloat in the B2B division and a lead instructor resigning from burnout. Three links, and the last one quotes a fact from the scenario.

Run it again on the other pairing. Conscientiousness 76 and Neuroticism 68. Behaviour: meticulous early execution, which was an asset, but under scaling stress the same discipline turned into micromanagement and reluctance to delegate, while the neuroticism produced anxiety about committing to a path. Consequence: delayed strategic delegation and paralysis over the three growth options, with a co-founder now demanding a decision inside 4.5 months.

Scoring tip

Always give one upside and one downside for each trait pairing. The Fall 2025 model answer does this for both pairs. A purely negative answer reads as incomplete, because the same traits genuinely built the early business.

Question 2.1 · easy · 3 marks

Which Big Five trait is the strongest negative predictor of startup success, and which has essentially no predictive power? Explain why the second finding is described in lecture as counterintuitive.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from Recall from Empirical Predictive Power on Startup Milestones (Entrepreneurship lecture, Section 4). The examiner is checking that you remember the two endpoints of the finding, and the "why counterintuitive" clause is there to stop you answering with two words.

Strongest negative predictor: neuroticism. It is the single most reliable negative predictor across all startup phases. High neuroticism produces panic under high-stakes pressure, pitches that fail to convince, excessive risk aversion, and indecisiveness, and unlike the other traits it has no phase in which it helps.

No predictive power: extraversion. The research finds no significant impact on startup performance, funding scale, or exit viability.

Why that is counterintuitive. The popular image of a founder is a charismatic, outgoing person who wins rooms and closes investors by force of personality, so intuition says sociability should drive fundraising outcomes in particular. The data says otherwise. A plausible reading is that what persuades investors is the substance of the venture and the founder's credibility rather than social energy, so the trait that looks most founder-like is inert.

Question 2.2 · medium · 5 marks

Explain why conscientiousness is described in lecture as a "two-sided trait", and give one concrete example of it helping and one of it harming a venture.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from Tests whether you understand that trait effects in this lecture are stage-dependent, not fixed. Drawn from Entrepreneurship lecture Section 4. The examiner wants the reversal explained by what changes about the business between the two stages, which is the part most students omit.

Why it is two-sided. Conscientiousness is discipline, structure, planning and attention to detail. Whether that helps depends entirely on what the venture needs at that moment, and what the venture needs reverses as it grows.

Early stage, positive. A young venture has almost no resources and no track record, so its scarcest assets are credibility and runway. High discipline produces structured execution and lean resource allocation, which stretches the runway, and it builds investor trust, because a founder with clean plans and reliable delivery is a lower-risk bet than one without. Structure is doing useful work precisely because there is nothing else holding the venture together.

Later scaling stage, negative. In hypergrowth the environment changes faster than any plan survives, so the binding constraint becomes adaptability rather than reliability. Extreme structure now hinders rapid pivoting, creates resistance to breaking existing systems, and slows response in a chaotic environment. The same instinct that once protected the venture now defends processes that have stopped fitting the situation.

The underlying principle. The trait did not change; the fitness landscape did. Early on the business is fragile and needs discipline to survive. Later it is fast and needs looseness to keep up. This is why a Big Five answer should always ask which stage the case is in before deciding whether a score is an asset.

Question 2.3 · hard · 5 marks · case-pattern

A founder is assessed as: Openness 46, Conscientiousness 88, Extraversion 62, Agreeableness 51, Neuroticism 81. Her logistics startup has excellent operational metrics and clean processes, but has shipped no new product line in two years, lost two senior hires who described her as "impossible to get a decision from", and has turned down two acquisition conversations without taking a meeting. Analyse how her personality profile connects to these business outcomes.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from This is a direct rehearsal of Q4 of the Fall 2025 final. It draws on the Big Five section of the Entrepreneurship lecture, and the examiner wants the three-link chain each time: trait and score, the behaviour it produces, then the specific outcome named in the scenario. Note that this profile is deliberately the mirror image of Fatima Rahman's, so a memorised SkillBridge answer will score nothing here.

Very high neuroticism (81) with high conscientiousness (88): decision paralysis. The lecture identifies neuroticism as the most reliable negative predictor across all startup phases, producing indecisiveness and excessive risk aversion under high-stakes pressure. Combined with extreme conscientiousness, which demands that every choice be fully worked out before it is made, this yields a founder who cannot commit until the evidence is complete, and the evidence is never complete. The visible outcomes are precisely those the scenario names: two senior hires resigning over an inability to get a decision, and two acquisition conversations refused without a meeting, since an acquisition is the most irreversible decision available.

High conscientiousness (88) at scale: operational strength, strategic rigidity. The lecture treats conscientiousness as two-sided, an early-stage asset that becomes a late-stage brake. Here both sides are visible in the same company. The upside is real and should be credited: excellent operational metrics and clean processes are exactly what disciplined execution produces. The downside is that extreme structure resists pivoting and slows adaptability, which explains two years without a new product line.

Low openness (46): no pipeline of new directions. The lecture describes openness as the early catalyst behind visionary narratives, with no correlation to later scaling. A low score does not damage operations, but it means the business generates few unconventional options in the first place, which compounds the rigidity above. The company is not choosing badly among new ideas; it is not producing them.

Moderate agreeableness (51) and extraversion (62): mostly not the story. Worth one line to show you know what to exclude. The lecture finds extraversion has no significant predictive power at all, so the moderate score explains nothing, and agreeableness confers only early rapport advantages. Neither is the driver of the outcomes described.

Answer in one line

Her operational excellence and her strategic paralysis are the same trait combination seen from two sides: conscientiousness of 88 delivers the processes, and conscientiousness of 88 fused with neuroticism of 81 blocks every irreversible decision the company now needs to make.

Topic 03

Creativity vs innovation

A short topic with two diagrams. Both diagrams are examinable as one-liners.

These two words get used interchangeably in ordinary speech, and your lecture separates them with a phrase worth memorising exactly, because it is short enough to quote and precise enough to earn a mark.

The distinction, in the faculty's words

Innovation is "doing different things" (new value, new methods, new products and paradigms).
Creativity is "doing things differently" (approaching existing processes from new angles).

The lecture draws these nested inside one another, with creativity as the wider container. The reading is that creative thinking is the general capacity, and innovation is what happens when that capacity produces something genuinely new rather than a new way of doing the old thing. A team that redesigns its deployment process to cut release time in half is being creative. A team that builds a product category that did not exist is innovating.

The three components of creativity

The other diagram in this lecture is a three-circle Venn: Expertise, Creative Thinking Skills, and Motivation. The claim is that entrepreneurial creativity lives only where all three overlap, and that is the sentence to write in an exam.

  • Expertise. The technical, procedural and intellectual knowledge base. You cannot recombine ideas in a field you do not know.
  • Creative thinking skills. Flexibility and imaginative approaches to problems. Cognitive agility, the ability to hold a problem from several angles.
  • Motivation. Drive and passion, and here the lecture is specific: intrinsic motivation is more effective than extrinsic. Wanting to solve the problem beats being paid to solve it.

The reason the intersection matters is that each pair without the third fails in a predictable way. Expertise plus creative thinking without motivation gives you a person who could build something and does not. Motivation plus creative thinking without expertise gives enthusiasm aimed at a problem the person does not understand well enough to solve. Expertise plus motivation without creative thinking gives competent execution of conventional answers. Only all three together, in the lecture's phrasing, convert innovative ideas into value-creating profit.

Question 3.1 · easy · 3 marks

Distinguish creativity from innovation as defined in lecture, and give one example of each from a software company.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from The Creativity vs Innovation diagram, Entrepreneurship lecture Section 5. Short recall question; the examples are where the marks separate good answers from adequate ones, so make them concrete rather than generic.

The distinction. Innovation is doing different things: producing new value, new methods, new products or new paradigms. Creativity is doing things differently: approaching existing processes and problems from novel angles. In the lecture's nesting, creativity is the broader capacity and innovation is the outcome when that capacity produces something categorically new.

Creativity example. A team that replaces a two-hour manual release checklist with an automated pipeline is doing the same job, shipping software, from a different angle. The output is unchanged; the method is reimagined.

Innovation example. The same company launching a usage-based pricing model that no competitor offers, creating a new way for customers to buy, is doing a different thing rather than the same thing differently.

Question 3.2 · medium · 5 marks

A well-funded R&D team of highly experienced engineers, generously bonused for every patent filed, produces plenty of patents but no commercially viable product in three years. Using the three components of creativity, diagnose the problem.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from Applies the Three Components of Creativity Venn diagram (Entrepreneurship lecture, Section 5) to a scenario. The examiner has deliberately supplied strong expertise and strong extrinsic motivation, so the mark is in noticing which component is missing or mis-specified rather than reciting all three.

Expertise is present. Highly experienced engineers give the team a deep technical and procedural knowledge base, which is the first component and is clearly not the constraint.

Motivation is present but of the wrong kind. The lecture states specifically that intrinsic motivation is more effective than extrinsic. Bonusing per patent filed is purely extrinsic, and it is worse than merely weak because it is aimed at the wrong target: it rewards filings, not solved customer problems. The team is optimising exactly what it is paid for, and patents filed is not commercial viability.

Creative thinking skills are likely under-used. The component the lecture describes as flexibility and imaginative approaches to problems is what turns knowledge into a solution shaped like a real need. A team measured on patent count has no incentive to sit with a messy customer problem and approach it from several angles, because that work does not produce filings quickly.

The synthesis. The lecture holds that entrepreneurial creativity emerges strictly at the intersection of all three, converting novel ideas into value-creating, profitable enterprises. Here two components are nominally present but the intersection is empty, because the extrinsic reward pulls the team away from the problem-facing work where the three would overlap. The fix implied by the lecture is to re-anchor motivation intrinsically, by attaching the team to customer problems they care about solving, and to measure outcomes rather than filings.

Topic 04

Prototypes, hypotheses and MVP

Q3 territory on the last paper, and the source of the single most misunderstood idea in the course.

Here is the problem this whole lecture exists to solve. Building software is expensive and slow, and most of what gets built turns out to be wrong. Not badly engineered, just wrong: aimed at a customer who does not exist, solving a problem nobody minds having, priced at a level nobody will pay. The traditional response is to plan harder before building. The response in this lecture is different: build something deliberately incomplete, put it in front of real people, and let reality tell you what is wrong while the bill is still small.

Prototype versus product

Everything starts here, and the difference is not about size. It is about what the thing is for. A prototype exists to produce information. A product exists to produce value and profit. Once you hold that distinction, the rest of the table follows from it.

DimensionPrototypeProduct
Primary goalDeliver validated information by testing risky business hypothesesDeliver sustained customer value and ongoing business profit
Scope & fidelityMinimal, basic, often non-functional; tests a single assumptionScalable, fully engineered, tested, secure, supported end to end
Cost & timeMinimal, hours to weeksHigh capital investment, extensive engineering, continuous upkeep
AudienceEarly adopters and selected test usersThe entire target market segment
Evaluation metricsLearning velocity, hypothesis validation, user desirabilityRevenue, gross margin, customer lifetime value, retention
Key idea

Judging a prototype by revenue is a category error, and so is judging a product by learning velocity. If a case shows a team measuring an early experiment on revenue, or measuring a mature product on how much it taught them, that mismatch is the answer to the question.

The three fidelity levels

Fidelity means how closely the prototype resembles the real thing. You climb the ladder as your questions get more specific, and the point of climbing slowly is that each rung is cheaper than the next.

  • Low fidelity. Paper sketches, whiteboard sessions, rough digital wireframes. Used for rapid concept testing, information architecture, user flow mapping and quick stakeholder feedback, with no code written at all.
  • Mid fidelity. Structured digital blueprints: wireframes and clickable mockups focused on layout, content hierarchy and navigation flow. Used to evaluate interaction workflows and usability without visual styling distracting the tester.
  • High fidelity. Realistic interactive simulations with the final product's visual design, components and animations. Used for detailed usability validation and executive sign-off just before engineering sprints begin.

Note why mid fidelity is deliberately grey and ugly. If you show a user a beautiful screen, they comment on the colours. Strip the styling out and they comment on whether they can find the button, which is the question you actually asked.

Hypotheses and the Business Model Canvas

A hypothesis, in this lecture's sense, is not any old assumption. It is an assumption critical to the business model, such that if it is disproven the venture fails. That severity test is what stops you wasting prototypes on things that do not matter.

MVP and the Build-Measure-Learn loop

An MVP is the leanest functional version of a product that enables maximal validated customer learning with minimal engineering effort. Read that definition slowly, because two words in it are doing all the work. Functional: it has to work. Learning: its output is knowledge, not revenue.

The loop it sits inside runs Build, then Test (measure real user metrics), then Learn (insights and decisions), then back to Build. The direction matters: you build in order to measure, and you measure in order to learn, and the learning determines what you build next. A team that builds, then builds, then builds, and only measures at the end has not run the loop once.

The misconception, stated precisely

An MVP is not a broken or half-built product. It is the simplest complete solution that delivers real customer utility at every stage.

The lecture's diagram makes this unmissable. The wrong approach builds a wheel, then an axle with wheels, then a chassis, then finally a car; nothing is usable until the very end. The right approach builds a skateboard (it transports you), then a scooter (it steers), then a bicycle (it pedals), then a motorcycle (it is powered), then a car (it shelters you). Every stage is a whole usable thing.

Watch out: the trap the last exam actually used

The other half of this misconception, and the one that decided Q3 in Fall 2025, is confusing more content or more features with more value. SkillBridge scaled by adding recorded videos, which felt like growth, while quietly deleting the thing that made the product work: live interaction, human accountability, and practical problem solving. Adding volume is not the same as scaling value. If a case shows a company that grew its catalogue while retention fell, this is your answer.

The three MVP scenarios from lecture

Your faculty worked three examples and gave a verdict on each. Learn the reason for each verdict, because a new scenario on the exam will be a variation of one of these.

  • Dhanmondi grocery delivery, exemplary MVP. No software at all. One WhatsApp Business number, informal partnerships with 8 local shops, deliveries by rickshaw, metrics tracked in Google Sheets for three weeks. It validated demand, order frequency, delivery times and willingness to pay at near-zero capital cost. Why it works: the founders tested the business hypothesis, not the technology.
  • University canteen ordering app, anti-pattern. Two months of building authentication, dashboards, queues, ratings and loyalty, without ever talking to a student. Elaborate product, zero validation of whether students wanted it or the canteen would cooperate.
  • AI Shopify dashboard, over-engineering. Real-time pipeline, custom AI models and alerting, all before a single paying customer. Heavy technical investment with no evidence anyone would pay for automated insight.

Notice that scenarios two and three fail for the same underlying reason in different clothes: engineering came before evidence.

Definition. An MVP is the leanest functional version of a product that enables maximal validated customer learning with minimal engineering effort.

Why a half-built product fails the definition. The word doing the work is "functional". A half-built product delivers no customer utility, so nobody uses it in earnest, so it generates no honest usage data, so it produces no validated learning. It fails the purpose clause of the definition even though it satisfies the minimal-effort clause.

The diagram's logic. The wrong path builds a wheel, then an axle with wheels, then a chassis, then a complete car. Each stage is a complete, usable means of transport, so a real user can use it, and the team learns something real at every stage. The MVP is therefore the simplest complete solution, not a fraction of the final one.

If shopkeepers stop sending entries after the first few days, or send them in weekly batches from the paper khata rather than as they happen, the hypothesis is disproven, because it shows the perceived benefit is not worth the daily effort. Question 4.3 · hard · 5 marks · case-pattern

An online tutoring startup began with 80 students receiving twice-weekly live small-group sessions and a 74% course completion rate. To scale, it recorded those sessions, expanded to 5,000 students on a video library, and added 60 new courses. Completion has fallen to 11% and refund requests have tripled. Explain what has gone wrong in MVP terms, and reconstruct a value proposition that can scale without repeating the mistake.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from This is Q3 of the Fall 2025 final in a new costume. It draws on the MVP misconception section (Prototypes & MVP, Section 6) and on the prototype-versus-product distinction (Section 1). The examiner wants two things in sequence: name the misinterpretation, then rebuild the proposition. Half the marks are in the rebuild, so do not spend all your time diagnosing.

What was actually validated at 80 students. The 74% completion rate was evidence for a specific value proposition, and it is worth being precise about which one. The students were not paying for video content, which was freely available elsewhere. They were paying for live human attention, a small group that noticed if you disappeared, and the accountability of a scheduled session twice a week. That was the validated proposition.

The misinterpretation. When the company scaled, it kept the artefact and discarded the mechanism. Recording the sessions preserved the information in them while deleting the live interaction, the small group, and the schedule, which were the parts doing the work. Adding 60 courses then compounded it. This is the classic MVP misreading the lecture warns about: confusing more features and more content with more delivered value. Completion collapsing from 74% to 11% is the measurement of exactly that deletion, and tripled refunds are customers saying the same thing in cash.

A second framing worth one line. By moving to a passive video library the company also stepped into direct competition with free and near-free alternatives on content volume, where it cannot win, instead of competing on the accountability it had already proven customers valued.

Reconstructed value proposition. Rebuild around accountability delivered at scale rather than instructor hours delivered at scale:

  • Cohort-based enrolment with fixed start and end dates, restoring the schedule that made completion a shared event rather than an individual intention.
  • Peer accountability groups of six to eight students, which recreates the small-group pressure without consuming instructor time, since the accountability comes from peers.
  • Community teaching assistants drawn from high-performing alumni, giving cheap human responsiveness that scales with the student body instead of against it.
  • Automated grading and feedback loops on practical assignments, so students still receive the "somebody looked at my work" signal at marginal cost near zero.
  • Weekly live office hours run by senior mentors, keeping a real human touchpoint at one-to-many cost rather than one-to-one.

Why this scales where the video library did not. Each element's cost grows far more slowly than the student count, because the labour is either peer-supplied, alumni-supplied, automated, or delivered once to many. The proven mechanism, accountability and human presence, is retained; only its delivery is redesigned. That is the difference between scaling value and scaling volume.

Topic 05

Lean architecture

Not examined in Fall 2025, which raises rather than lowers the odds this time.

Lean started in Toyota's factories as a way of thinking about waste, and the core observation was simple: in any process, most of the elapsed time and cost goes into activity that the customer would not pay for if they could see it. Lean architecture takes that lens and points it at software design and delivery. Its three stated objectives in your lecture are to eliminate waste, maximise customer value, and optimise system efficiency.

The seven principles

Learn these with the examples attached. In a case question you will be given a symptom and asked which principle is being violated, and the examples are what let you recognise the symptom.

1. Eliminate waste. Strip away any task, meeting, process or line of code that does not deliver measurable value to the end user. Organisationally, that looks like replacing multi-hour daily status meetings with short asynchronous dashboard check-ins. Technically, it looks like pruning bloated unused features and removing redundant code from the repository.

2. Build quality in. Prevent defects during development rather than fixing them after release. In practice that means automated unit and integration tests written while the code is written, through TDD and CI. The lecture's illustration is worth quoting: catching a payment checkout flaw early in the pipeline avoids an expensive production hotfix later.

3. Create knowledge. Cultivate institutional learning through structured reflection, most concretely through sprint retrospectives that dig for root causes rather than symptoms. The lecture's example: identifying that API latency was causing client-side timeouts, and establishing a centralised Redis caching layer in response.

4. Defer commitment. Avoid speculative over-engineering; make irreversible architectural choices at the last responsible moment, based on real data. The example is deploying a simple rule-based recommendation filter first, watching how customers actually behave, and only then architecting a machine-learning pipeline if the data justifies it.

5. Deliver fast. Ship small modular increments quickly so feedback loops close quickly. Launch search with basic text filtering, gather feedback, add cuisine and budget filters in later sprints.

6. Respect people. Empower development, QA and UX teams by decentralising technical and operational decisions. Let engineering choose its stack; give QA a genuine voice in feature prioritisation. The purpose is shared ownership.

7. Optimise the whole. Maximise end-to-end value stream performance rather than sub-optimising a component. The lecture's example is precise and worth reproducing: speeding up one microservice so much that it overwhelms database write throughput, making the total system slower. Local improvement, global regression.

The two most confusable principles

Defer commitment and deliver fast sound like opposites and are not. Defer commitment is about irreversible decisions: postpone those until data arrives. Deliver fast is about reversible increments: ship those immediately to generate the data. They work together. You ship the simple rule-based filter fast precisely so you can defer the architecture decision responsibly.

Agile versus lean architecture

Your lecture builds this comparison around a food delivery platform. In the traditional agile version, sprints deliver batches of features: authentication, food search, cart and checkout, ratings and reviews, push notifications. Velocity looks good. But reviews go barely used, old payment logic stays tightly coupled to checkout and makes it slow, technical debt climbs, and the value actually delivered per sprint is erratic.

The lean response is four steps: value stream mapping (map order to delivery and see that checkout matters more than reviews), just-in-time design (decouple checkout from payment so each can evolve), automated quality and feedback (continuous test pipelines on checkout, tracking and metrics), and evolutionary design (build the review features only when user volume validates them).

MetricTraditional agileLean architecture
Delivery speedFixed 2-week sprints, delayed by accumulated rework2-week sprints with minimal rework, prioritised by value
Feature valueBatched delivery including low-value componentsHigh-value, customer-validated features built first
Bugs & tech debtElevated from rapid uncoupled additionsMinimised by automated tests and quality gates
User satisfactionModerate; bloated workflows obscure core valueHigh; focused on frictionless core journeys
Key idea

Agile asks "are we shipping fast?". Lean asks "is what we ship worth shipping?". Both run two-week sprints, so the difference is never speed, it is selection. That single sentence answers most agile-versus-lean comparison questions.

Question 5.2 · medium · 5 marks

A development team reports rising sprint velocity for six consecutive sprints, yet customer satisfaction is flat and the support queue is growing. Using the agile-versus-lean comparison, explain how both facts can be true at once and what four-step lean intervention you would apply.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from Uses the food delivery case study and the Agile vs Lean comparison table (Lean Architecture, Sections 3 and 4). The examiner has constructed the exact paradox from the lecture, high velocity with erratic value, and wants you to explain the mechanism and then name the four lean steps in order.

Why both facts are true. Velocity measures output, not outcome. It counts how much work the team completes, and says nothing about whether that work was worth completing. The lecture's food delivery case shows this precisely: sprints delivered authentication, search, cart and checkout, ratings and reviews and push notifications on schedule, yet reviews went barely used and the value delivered per sprint was erratic. High velocity with flat satisfaction means the team is efficiently building things customers do not need.

Why the support queue grows at the same time. The comparison table attributes elevated bugs and technical debt in traditional agile to rapid, uncoupled feature additions. Each new feature layered onto tightly coupled code adds both surface area for defects and friction to existing journeys, so the very additions that raise velocity also raise support volume. In the lecture's example, legacy payment logic coupled to checkout made checkout slow, which is a support problem created by feature work.

The four-step lean intervention.

  1. Value stream mapping. Map the full customer journey end to end and identify which steps carry the value. In the lecture's case this revealed checkout as far more important than reviews, which redirects prioritisation away from volume and toward impact.
  2. Just-in-time design. Decouple the modules that are choking each other, as the lecture does by separating checkout from payment so each can evolve without dragging the other.
  3. Automated quality and feedback. Put continuous test pipelines on the high-value paths, checkout, tracking and metrics, so quality is built in rather than inspected afterward, which drains the support queue at source.
  4. Evolutionary design. Build low-usage features such as reviews only when real user volume validates them, which is the defer-commitment principle applied at the feature level.
Answer in one line

The team is optimising throughput while lean optimises selection; velocity will keep rising and satisfaction keep flattening until the prioritisation input changes, which is what value stream mapping fixes.

Question 5.3 · hard · 5 marks

An engineering lead argues: "Defer commitment and deliver fast contradict each other. You cannot both postpone decisions and ship quickly." Resolve the apparent contradiction, then identify which principle is being violated in each of the following: (a) a team builds a Kubernetes multi-region architecture for a product with 40 users; (b) a team speeds up its search service and the database begins timing out.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from Tests genuine understanding rather than recall of principles 4, 5 and 7 (Lean Architecture, Section 2). The examiner has picked the two principles students most often treat as opposites. The two mini-scenarios then check that you can apply the distinction rather than just state it.

Resolving the contradiction. The two principles govern different kinds of decision, so they never actually meet. Defer commitment applies to irreversible architectural choices: the ones that are expensive or impossible to undo, such as a data model, a platform commitment, or a distributed system topology. Deliver fast applies to small reversible increments: features and changes that can be shipped, measured and withdrawn cheaply.

Far from conflicting, each enables the other. Deferring an irreversible choice is only responsible if you have a way of learning what the right choice is, and shipping fast is what generates that learning. Equally, you can only ship fast if you have not yet locked yourself into a heavy architecture that makes every change slow. The lecture's own example runs both at once: the simple rule-based recommendation filter is delivered fast, and the machine-learning pipeline decision is deferred until that filter's usage data arrives. The phrase in the lecture is "the last responsible moment", and responsible is the operative word: not the latest possible moment, but the last one at which you still have the information and the time to choose well.

(a) Kubernetes multi-region for 40 users: defer commitment is violated. This is textbook speculative over-engineering. A multi-region topology is an expensive, deeply irreversible architectural commitment being made on a guess about future scale, with no empirical data to support it, at a point when 40 users would be served fine by something trivial. Eliminate waste is a defensible secondary answer, since the effort delivers no measurable end-user value, but defer commitment is the primary violation because the fault is in the timing of an irreversible decision.

(b) Faster search service, database timing out: optimise the whole is violated. This is almost exactly the lecture's illustration, where accelerating an isolated microservice overwhelms database write throughput. The team improved one component's local metric while the end-to-end system got worse, which is the definition of sub-optimisation. The lean response is to analyse total system workflow capacity, so that the search service's throughput is set by what the whole pipeline can absorb rather than by what the service alone can produce.

Topic 06

Key performance indicators

Q2 on the last paper, and the most predictable five marks available. Learn this one properly.

Your faculty opens with an analogy worth keeping: KPIs act like GPS. Used correctly, they tell you whether parts of your project are about to drive off a bridge into a lake, or whether you are heading toward your objective at top speed. The value is in early warning, not in record keeping.

Definition

A KPI is a quantifiable measurement used to evaluate how effectively an organisation, project team or individual achieves predefined strategic goals and critical milestones. Its purpose is to let project managers, Scrum Masters, product owners and executives judge whether current strategies are working or need immediate intervention.

KPI versus metric: the distinction the exam is built on

Every KPI is a metric. Most metrics are not KPIs. The difference is not size or importance in some vague sense, it is what the number is about.

DimensionMetricKPI
What it tracksTactical performance of specific tasks, processes or activitiesHigh-level progress toward defined strategic objectives
FocusProcess execution and operational statusBusiness outcome, effectiveness and strategic impact
ExamplesTotal hours logged, daily page views, raw budget expendedOn-time delivery rate, conversion rate, net customer retention

A useful test when you are staring at a list of numbers in an exam: if the number moved sharply tomorrow, would a manager have to change a decision? If yes, it is behaving like a KPI. Page views doubling changes nothing on its own. Conversion rate halving changes everything.

A second test that catches most of the traps: can this number rise while the business gets worse? Total registered users can rise while every one of them churns. Hours logged can rise while delivery slows. Those are metrics. A true KPI cannot comfortably move the right way while the business moves the wrong way.

What makes a KPI effective

Three requirements from the lecture, and they are short enough to quote verbatim:

  • Directly linked to clear organisational and team strategic targets.
  • Fully quantifiable and actionable, meaning it enables targeted corrective intervention.
  • Realistic and achievable within operational constraints.

These sit on top of the S.M.A.R.T. framework, which the lecture presents as the goal-setting structure KPIs are built against: Specific (clear, concise, defined goals), Measurable (quantify progress with precise data), Achievable (feasibly within team capability), Relevant (aligned to overarching business aims), Time-bound (a definitive deadline).

Easy marks

If a question asks you to "propose a KPI", always write it in S.M.A.R.T. form. Not "improve delivery times" but "reduce average delivery time from 52 to 40 minutes across all Dhaka zones by the end of Q3". The second one names the metric, the baseline, the target, the scope and the deadline, and it ticks all five letters in one sentence.

Pros and cons

The lecture gives five of each. You rarely need all ten, but a question asking whether a company should adopt heavy KPI tracking wants the balanced view.

Pros: objective data-backed insight; teams empowered to act decisively; clear visibility of what is working versus what needs remediation; transparent accountability across teams and leadership; clarity, alignment and motivation.

Cons: organisational tunnel vision that obscures the bigger picture; unrealistic targets causing demotivation and burnout; long-term strategic health sacrificed to short-term target hitting; overly rigid KPIs stifling creative problem solving; and perverse incentives, where people game the system to hit the number artificially.

Watch out

That last con is the one examiners like, because it turns up in cases as a plausible-looking success. A support team told to minimise average ticket resolution time will start closing tickets without solving them. If a case shows a KPI improving while the outcome it is supposed to represent gets worse, you are looking at gaming, and naming it earns the mark.

How the faculty grades a KPI selection question

Look carefully at the coffee shop example in your lecture and at Q2 of the Fall 2025 paper. Both do the same three things, and your answer must do all three:

  1. Select a small set, typically four. Not all of them, and not one.
  2. Justify each one by naming what strategic question it answers, not by describing what it measures.
  3. Explicitly exclude the rest, with reasons. This is the step students skip and it is worth real marks. The coffee shop answer rejects total customers served, employee sick days, Instagram followers and coffee beans purchased, and gives a different reason for each.

Study the rejection reasons from the coffee shop case, because they are reusable templates. Total customers served "provides baseline volume context, but repeat customer volume and average spend offer far deeper insight into loyalty and revenue quality", that is the volume without quality rejection. Employee sick days is "an internal HR data point, not directly indicative of customer satisfaction unless persistent staffing shortages degrade service times", the internal operational input rejection. Instagram followers is "top-of-funnel marketing that does not demonstrate conversion to store visits or cash flow", the no proven link to outcome rejection. Coffee beans purchased "reflects resource consumption rather than business performance", the input not output rejection.

Four reusable rejection reasons

Volume without quality · internal operational input · no proven link to a business outcome · input consumed rather than output produced. Almost every distractor metric an examiner plants falls into one of these four buckets.

Question 6.1 · easy · 3 marks

Distinguish a KPI from a metric, and explain why "total hours logged by the development team" is a metric rather than a KPI.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from The KPIs vs Operational Metrics table (KPI lecture, Section 2). The examiner picked hours logged because it is one of the lecture's own listed metric examples, so the expected answer maps onto the table directly.

The distinction. A metric tracks the tactical performance of a specific task, process or activity, and its focus is process execution and operational status. A KPI measures high-level progress toward a defined strategic objective, and its focus is business outcome, effectiveness and strategic impact. Every KPI is a metric; the reverse does not hold.

Why hours logged is a metric. It records effort consumed, which is an input to the work rather than a result of it. It is not linked to any strategic objective on its own: a team can log more hours and deliver less, or log fewer hours and deliver more, so movement in the number tells a manager nothing about whether the project is succeeding and prompts no specific corrective action. The lecture's effectiveness criteria require a KPI to be linked to strategic targets and to be actionable, and hours logged satisfies neither. The corresponding KPI would be on-time project delivery rate, which measures the outcome the hours were meant to produce.

Topic 07

Business growth and diversification

The strategy vocabulary. Cases about growth choices are built on this table.

Business growth, in your lecture's framing, is a stage where an organisation experiences sustained increases in market reach and profit avenues: revenue rises, more products or services are produced, and the customer base expands. Note that all three move together in the definition. A company where revenue rises purely because prices rose is not what this lecture means by growth.

Organic versus strategic growth

Organic growth means expanding through internal operations using the company's own resources, rather than reaching for outside capital. The mechanism the lecture emphasises is efficiency: produce more in less time, so you get higher sales from proportionally less effort. The worked example is deliberately homely. You set aside part of your operating revenue to buy a second machine. That second machine doubles capacity without taking on debt, which lets you accept larger orders, which generates the revenue that funds a third machine. Growth compounds out of its own earnings.

Strategic growth means deliberate long-term initiatives requiring significant dedicated resources and funding. The lecture's sequencing advice is the examinable part: businesses typically pursue organic growth first, to build internal capital, and only then invest in strategic initiatives. Strategic growth is described as a major undertaking demanding intensive effort, patience through a slow start, or hiring skilled talent to speed things up.

Why a growth plan is needed at all

Three reasons, and they are easy marks if a question asks for them:

  1. Funding. Investors expect a defined growth roadmap, so presenting a structured plan is a competitive edge when raising money.
  2. Insurance. Growth creates financial padding, a buffer against unforeseen crises. The lecture's example is the economic disruption faced by brick-and-mortar retail in 2020.
  3. Credibility and creditability. A clear roadmap is what makes loans obtainable and debt serviceable, since sustainable profitability requires managing debt against a plan.

The four growth strategies

These four are the core of the lecture. The organising logic underneath them is a two-by-two: are you selling an existing or a new product, into an existing or a new market? Your lecture does not draw this grid or name it, so treat the grid itself as supplementary; it is simply a memory aid for the four strategies the lecture does define, and it happens to be the standard framework these four belong to.

The two-by-two underneath the four strategies

              EXISTING PRODUCT     NEW PRODUCT
EXISTING MARKET  Market penetration    Product development
NEW MARKET       Market expansion      Diversification

1. Market penetration. Increase market share with existing products in existing markets, through price differentiation (lowering prices to capture share, or premium pricing to reach a distinct segment) or by repositioning your marketing. The lecture's case is IKEA, which initially penetrated by pricing furniture far below competitors, using cheaper raw materials, consolidating warehouses, and eliminating assembly-line and storage overhead through flat-pack design. Note that the price cut was made possible by a cost structure change, not by simply accepting thinner margins.

2. Market expansion, also called market development. Take existing products into completely new or underserved markets: new geographies, new demographics, global reach. The example is an established watch brand sold only in the US being taken into European and Asian markets.

3. Product development. Modify, upgrade or introduce new products for your existing customer base, to satisfy changing expectations and encourage repeat purchase and referral. The lecture's example is Apple: annual iPhone iterations with new designs, features and processors, plus ecosystem additions such as the HomePod.

4. Diversification. Introduce completely new products or services into new markets. The lecture flags this as the highest-risk option, because you are unfamiliar with both the operations and the market at the same time.

Why it helps: the four strategies your lecture defines are the same four the wider literature calls the Ansoff matrix, and seeing the grid drawn out makes the existing-versus-new logic stick far better than a list does. Your lecture never uses the name, so use the video for the intuition and the lecture's vocabulary in your answer.

The four diversification quadrants

Diversification then splits four ways, and this is where students lose marks, because the distinctions are fine. The trick is to ask two questions about the new product: is it technologically related to what we already do? and are we selling it to the same customers?

TypeStrategic focusExample
ConglomerateExpand into businesses entirely unrelated to current products, markets or technologies. Aim is spreading financial risk across uncorrelated industries.A food and beverage enterprise acquiring an apparel brand
ConcentricAdd products technologically, operationally or commercially related to the existing business, leveraging core competencies.A camera manufacturer producing optical lenses and photography accessories

The decision rule that separates concentric from horizontal

Both involve new products sold to a related audience, so students mix them constantly. The discriminator is technological relatedness. Concentric: the new product uses your existing technology or expertise (camera maker to lenses). Horizontal: the new product is technologically unrelated but sold to the same customers through the same channels (shoe maker to apparel). If you can build it with the skills you already have, it is concentric. If you had to learn a new craft but you already own the customer, it is horizontal.

The worked case: PureGlow Cosmetics

PureGlow makes skincare, face creams, cleansers and moisturisers, with an established brand and loyal customers. It launches scented candles and home fragrances, sold to the same self-care and beauty demographic, through the same retail stores and online store.

The verdict in lecture is horizontal diversification, and the justification is worth learning as a sentence pattern: the new line is technologically distinct from skincare formulation, but it operates at the same stage of the supply chain and targets the existing customer base through identical channels. Three clauses, technology, value-chain position, customer and channel, and they are the three things every diversification justification should mention.

Topic 08

IT project cost analysis

The first of the two calculation topics. Not asked in Fall 2025, which is exactly why you should prepare it.

Every project spends money, and the question a project manager has to answer at any moment is whether the spending is under control. That sounds obvious, and it is surprisingly hard, because "we have spent 60% of the budget" tells you nothing until you know how much of the work is done. This lecture builds the machinery for answering that properly. It starts with what kinds of cost exist, then how you turn them into a controllable plan, then how you estimate them before you know much, and finally, in the next section, how you measure performance against them.

The four cost categories

Direct costs are expenses you can point at a project deliverable and say "that one caused this". Hardware, software licences, developer tools, specialised equipment, dedicated labour. The test is attributability: if the project vanished, would this expense vanish with it?

Indirect costs are overheads that support the project without being tied to any single task or deliverable. Administrative overhead, general training, utilities, meeting room rentals. They are real costs of doing the project; they just cannot be traced to one work package.

Opportunity costs are the forgone benefits of the next best alternative you gave up by committing resources here. Note the phrasing in your lecture: the next best alternative, not all alternatives added together. If your team could have built either product A or product B, and B would have earned BDT 3 million, then choosing A carries a BDT 3 million opportunity cost even though no money leaves the account.

Risk-related costs are what you allocate to manage, mitigate and respond to uncertainty: contingency reserves, project insurance, recovery response funds. This category is the bridge into the risk lecture, since contingency reserves are what a project sets aside against the identified risks you will quantify with EMV in Topic 10. Worth being precise about the source: the cost lecture does not itself say how the reserve is sized, and the risk lecture only says to allocate contingency funds to protect ROI, so treat "reserve sized from the total EMV" as standard practice rather than something the faculty stated.

Watch out

Opportunity cost is the one students get wrong, because no invoice is ever issued for it. It never appears in an accounting system, which is precisely why an exam asks about it. If a question describes a team choosing between two projects, the opportunity cost of the chosen one is the value of the rejected one.

Cost baseline versus project budget

These two are not the same thing, and the difference is where a common exam question lives.

The cost baseline is the approved, time-phased budget version used to monitor and control cost performance. Three properties define it. It is time-phased, meaning expenditure is mapped against milestones and calendar time rather than sitting as one lump sum. It is fixed, meaning changing it requires formal change control. And it excludes management reserves: it contains the project work estimates plus contingency reserves, and nothing more.

The relationship
\[ \text{Project Budget} = \text{Cost Baseline} + \text{Management Reserves} \]

Why are the two reserves treated differently? Because they cover different kinds of uncertainty. Contingency reserves cover known unknowns, which is the lecture's own phrase: risks you have identified and quantified, such as "the vendor might deliver two weeks late". Those sit inside the baseline, because you knew about them when you planned. Management reserves cover what is conventionally called unknown unknowns (that term is standard usage rather than the lecture's wording, though it follows directly from the lecture's known-unknowns): things nobody anticipated at all. Those sit outside the baseline and typically require management authorisation to release, which is what keeps the baseline honest as a performance yardstick. If unforeseen money could silently flow into the baseline, you could never tell whether you were over budget.

The five steps to build a cost baseline

  1. Work breakdown structure (WBS). Decompose the project into manageable work packages.
  2. Cost estimation. Estimate each work package using analogous, parametric or bottom-up methods.
  3. Cost aggregation. Sum the package costs by phase or deliverable.
  4. Add contingency reserves. Incorporate the calculated reserves for identified risks.
  5. Develop the time-phased budget. Map the aggregate cost across timeline milestones, which produces the baseline.

Your lecture illustrates this with a cake project: buy ingredients (500), prepare batter (200), preheat oven (50), bake (300), cool (50), prepare frosting (100), frost (150), set up party table (200), serve (100), running to BDT 1,650 over six hours. Plotted cumulatively, hour by hour, the spend goes 500, 700, 1,000, 1,300, 1,500, 1,650.

Your lecture calls this the S-curve, and the general idea is that cumulative spending on a project starts slowly while few activities are running, steepens in the middle when the project is at full execution with several work packages in flight, and flattens toward the end as work winds down to finishing touches. Slow, fast, slow, which traces an S.

A discrepancy in the cake example worth knowing about

The cake project's own numbers do not actually trace an S. Its hourly increments are 500, 200, 300, 300, 200, 150, so the largest spend is in hour one, when the ingredients are bought, and the curve is concave rather than S-shaped throughout. The lecture describes it as an S-curve anyway, and it is fine to follow the lecture in an exam, but do not be confused if you plot the figures and see no S. The S shape is the general pattern for large projects, where mobilisation is slow and the middle is where the labour concentrates; a six-hour cake with a single expensive purchase at the start is simply too small to show it.

Cost estimation models

Analogous, or top-down, estimating uses historical actuals from similar past projects. You use it early, when detailed requirements do not exist yet. The lecture's diagram shows projects A, B, C and D as bars beside a new project; project C is identified as the closest structural and domain match, so C's duration and cost are mapped directly onto the new project as the baseline estimate. It is fast and cheap and not very accurate, which is a fair trade when you have nothing else.

Single-factor ratio estimation adjusts a historical actual by one scaling factor.

Single-factor ratio
\[ E_{\text{new}} = E_{\text{old}} \times F \]

where \(E_{\text{new}}\) is the new estimate, \(E_{\text{old}}\) is the historical actual, and \(F\) is the scaling factor. The lecture's example: a historical cost of $100,000 with a new scope 25% larger gives \(F = 1.25\) and \(C_{\text{new}} = 100{,}000 \times 1.25 = \$125{,}000\).

Multi-factor ratio model does the same thing with several factors multiplied together, which is more realistic because projects differ in more than one way at once.

Multi-factor ratio
\[ C_{\text{new}} = C_{\text{old}} \times F_{\text{size}} \times F_{\text{complexity}} \times F_{\text{team}} \]
The sign convention that decides these questions

Factors above 1.0 push cost up; factors below 1.0 push it down. Size and complexity behave intuitively: bigger and harder cost more. Team experience is the one that catches people out, because a more experienced team gets a factor below 1.0. In the lecture's worked example, high experience is 0.85, meaning an experienced team costs 15% less than the moderate baseline, since they work faster and rework less. If you write 1.15 for a strong team you will invert the whole answer.

It matches, and the direction is right: a project that is 60% bigger, harder, and staffed by a weaker team must cost substantially more than the original.

One weakness of the estimate. The model assumes cost scales linearly with size, since \(F_{\text{size}}\) is a plain ratio. Real software rarely behaves that way: as a system grows, integration points, communication overhead and testing surface grow faster than the line count, so a 60% larger system often costs more than 60% extra. A second defensible answer is that the model rests on a single historical project, so any peculiarity of that one project, an unusually easy client or an unusually clean codebase, is baked silently into every future estimate derived from it.

Question 8.3 · medium · 5 marks

Distinguish between contingency reserves and management reserves. Explain why including management reserves inside the cost baseline would undermine cost control, and identify the cost category to which both belong.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from Draws on Cost Baseline vs Project Budget and the IT Project Cost Categories list (Cost Analysis lecture, Sections 1 and 2). The third clause is a small trap: it asks you to connect the reserves back to the cost-category taxonomy from the start of the lecture, which most students treat as a separate topic.

The distinction. Contingency reserves cover known unknowns: risks that have been identified and quantified during planning, such as a vendor delivering late or a specific integration failing. Because they are anticipated, they are folded into the cost baseline, which the lecture defines as containing project work estimates plus contingency reserves. Management reserves cover unknown unknowns: events nobody foresaw and therefore nobody could price. They sit outside the baseline and are added to it to give the project budget.

Why including management reserves in the baseline would undermine control. The baseline exists to be a fixed yardstick. The lecture stresses two of its properties for exactly this reason: it is fixed, requiring formal change control to modify, and it is time-phased, so actual spend can be compared against planned spend at any milestone. Both properties depend on the baseline representing what the project was genuinely expected to cost. If unforeseen expenditure could be absorbed from a reserve already sitting inside the baseline, then overruns would be invisible: the project would appear to be on budget while consuming money set aside for events that have not happened. Every earned value measure that uses the baseline as BAC, cost variance, CPI, estimate at completion, would be flattered by the same amount, and the early warning those measures exist to provide would arrive too late to act on. Keeping the reserve outside, under management authorisation, forces an overrun to be visible and consciously approved.

Cost category. Both belong to risk-related costs, which the lecture defines as costs allocated to manage, mitigate and respond to project uncertainties, listing contingency reserves, project insurance and recovery response funds as examples.

Topic 09

Earned value analysis

The most formula-dense topic in the course, and the easiest marks on the paper once the idea clicks.

Start with the problem, because the formulas make no sense without it. Suppose you are three months into a six-month project with a BDT 100,000 budget, and you have spent BDT 55,000. Are you in trouble?

You cannot say. If 80% of the work is done, you are doing brilliantly. If 20% is done, you are in serious trouble. Money spent, on its own, is meaningless, because it tells you about consumption and nothing about production. Earned value analysis fixes this by insisting on three numbers instead of two, and the third one is the invention that makes the whole thing work.

The core idea, in one paragraph

You need to compare three things measured in the same currency: what you planned to have finished by now (PV), what you actually finished by now, valued at the planned price (EV), and what you actually paid to finish it (AC). The clever move is EV: instead of measuring progress in percent, you measure it in taka, by asking "how much did we budget for the work that is now complete?" Once all three are in taka, you can subtract them, and every earned value formula is a subtraction or a division of these three numbers.

The four fundamental quantities

SymbolNamePlain meaningFormula
BACBudget at CompletionThe whole approved budgetgiven
PVPlanned ValueValue of work we planned to have done by now\( \text{BAC} \times \%\text{ planned} \)
EVEarned ValueBudgeted value of work actually done\( \text{BAC} \times \%\text{ actual} \)
ACActual CostMoney actually spent on that workgiven
The one thing to get right

EV uses the budgeted rate, never the actual rate. If you budgeted BDT 100,000 for the whole project and 45% of the work is finished, EV is BDT 45,000 regardless of whether you spent 20,000 or 200,000 getting there. EV is a measure of output, priced at plan. AC is the measure of input. Keeping those separate is the entire trick.

Variances and indices

Once you have PV, EV and AC, everything else is comparison. The variances subtract (giving an amount in taka) and the indices divide (giving a ratio). Both come in a cost flavour and a schedule flavour, and both always start from EV.

Variances — how much, in taka
\[ CV = EV - AC \qquad SV = EV - PV \]

Positive CV means under budget; negative means over budget. Positive SV means ahead of schedule; negative means behind.

Indices — how efficiently, as a ratio
\[ CPI = \frac{EV}{AC} \qquad SPI = \frac{EV}{PV} \]

Greater than 1 is good in both cases: cost efficient, ahead of schedule. Less than 1 is bad: cost inefficient, behind schedule.

A memory aid that removes all guesswork here: EV always comes first in every one of the four expressions. Then pair it with AC for anything about cost, and with PV for anything about schedule. Subtract for a variance, divide for an index. Four formulas, one rule.

Now the interpretation, which is where the marks are. CPI has a clean plain-English reading: it is how much value you get per taka spent. A CPI of 0.82 means every 1.00 BDT you spend is buying only 0.82 BDT worth of budgeted work, so you are losing 18 paisa on the taka. SPI reads similarly as a rate: an SPI of 0.90 means you are progressing at 90% of the planned delivery rate.

Forecasting the ending

The last three formulas answer the question a sponsor actually asks: given how we have performed so far, what will this project finally cost?

Forecasting
\[ EAC = \frac{BAC}{CPI} \qquad ETC = EAC - AC \qquad VAC = BAC - EAC \]

EAC: Estimate at Completion, the forecast final cost. ETC: Estimate to Complete, what remains to be spent from today. VAC: Variance at Completion, the forecast overrun or underrun. A negative VAC forecasts a budget overrun.

The logic of \(EAC = BAC / CPI\) is worth one sentence because it makes the formula unforgettable. It assumes the inefficiency you have shown so far will continue. If you are only getting 0.82 taka of value per taka spent, then to buy the whole BAC worth of work you will need to spend BAC divided by 0.82. Dividing by a number below 1 makes the forecast bigger, which is exactly right for a project running inefficiently.

A rounding discrepancy in your lecture, and what to do about it

In the lecture's worked EVA problem (BAC 100,000; planned 50%; actual 45%; AC 55,000), CPI is calculated as \(45{,}000/55{,}000 \approx 0.8182\), then rounded to 0.82, and EAC is given as 121,951 BDT. A software project has BAC = BDT 500,000. At the review point, 60% of the work was planned to be complete, 55% is actually complete, and BDT 320,000 has been spent. Calculate PV, EV, CV, SV, CPI, SPI, EAC, ETC and VAC, and interpret the project's status in two sentences.

Show worked solution

Work through it yourself first, then check each line against the reason given.

  1. \( PV = BAC \times \%\text{planned} = 500{,}000 \times 0.60 \)

    Definition of Planned Value: the budgeted cost of work scheduled. Convert 60% to the decimal 0.60 before multiplying.

  2. \( PV = 300{,}000 \text{ BDT} \)

    Arithmetic. By now the plan said BDT 300,000 worth of work should be finished.

  3. \( EV = BAC \times \%\text{actual} = 500{,}000 \times 0.55 = 275{,}000 \text{ BDT} \)

    Definition of Earned Value: the budgeted value of work performed. Note it uses BAC, the budgeted rate, not the actual spend, which is why EV measures output rather than consumption.

  4. \( CV = EV - AC = 275{,}000 - 320{,}000 = -45{,}000 \text{ BDT} \)

    Cost variance formula. EV first, then subtract AC. Negative means over budget: BDT 275,000 of value was bought for BDT 320,000 of spending.

  5. \( SV = EV - PV = 275{,}000 - 300{,}000 = -25{,}000 \text{ BDT} \)

    Schedule variance formula. Same EV, now compared against the plan. Negative means behind schedule by BDT 25,000 worth of work.

  6. \( CPI = \dfrac{EV}{AC} = \dfrac{275{,}000}{320{,}000} \)

    Cost performance index: EV divided by AC. Keep it as a fraction for now so no rounding error propagates into EAC.

  7. \( CPI = \dfrac{55}{64} \approx 0.859 \)

    Cancel 5,000 from both numerator and denominator: \(275/320 = 55/64\). Below 1.0, confirming cost inefficiency, and consistent with the negative CV found in step 4.

  8. \( SPI = \dfrac{EV}{PV} = \dfrac{275{,}000}{300{,}000} = \dfrac{11}{12} \approx 0.917 \)

    Schedule performance index. Below 1.0, so the project is progressing at about 92% of the planned rate. Agrees in sign with the negative SV, which is the check that you have not swapped a formula.

  9. \( EAC = \dfrac{BAC}{CPI} = 500{,}000 \times \dfrac{64}{55} \)

    Estimate at completion. Dividing by the fraction 55/64 is the same as multiplying by 64/55, which keeps the arithmetic exact. This assumes current cost efficiency persists for the rest of the project.

  10. \( EAC \approx 581{,}818 \text{ BDT} \)

    \(500{,}000 \times 64 = 32{,}000{,}000\), then \(\div 55 = 581{,}818.18\). Larger than BAC, which is correct: dividing by a CPI below 1 must increase the forecast.

  11. \( ETC = EAC - AC = 581{,}818 - 320{,}000 \approx 261{,}818 \text{ BDT} \)

    Estimate to complete: the forecast total minus what has already gone out the door, leaving what is still to be spent.

  12. \( VAC = BAC - EAC = 500{,}000 - 581{,}818 \approx -81{,}818 \text{ BDT} \)

    Variance at completion. Negative, so a forecast overrun. Note it is bigger than the current CV of −45,000, because the overrun compounds across the remaining work.

Question 9.3 · hard · 5 marks · reverse problem

A project reports CPI = 0.80 and actual cost to date of BDT 240,000, against a budget at completion of BDT 800,000. Without being told the completion percentage, determine the earned value, the actual percentage of work complete, the estimate at completion and the variance at completion. Then explain why this project is in worse trouble than a project with the same CPI but 70% of the work already done.

Show worked solution

This one runs the formulas backwards, which is a favourite way of testing whether you understand them rather than memorised them.

  1. \( CPI = \dfrac{EV}{AC} \implies EV = CPI \times AC \)

    Rearrange the CPI definition by multiplying both sides by AC. EV is the unknown, and CPI and AC are both given, so this is the only formula that can start the problem.

  2. \( EV = 0.80 \times 240{,}000 = 192{,}000 \text{ BDT} \)

    Substitute the given values. Sensible: EV must be less than AC when CPI is below 1.

  3. \( EV = BAC \times \%\text{actual} \implies \%\text{actual} = \dfrac{EV}{BAC} \)

    Rearrange the earned value definition to isolate the completion percentage, dividing both sides by BAC.

  4. \( \%\text{actual} = \dfrac{192{,}000}{800{,}000} = 0.24 = 24\% \)

    Divide. Only 24% of the work is complete, which the question deliberately withheld because it is the key to the second half.

  5. \( EAC = \dfrac{BAC}{CPI} = \dfrac{800{,}000}{0.80} = 1{,}000{,}000 \text{ BDT} \)

    Estimate at completion. Dividing by 0.80 is multiplying by 1.25, so the forecast is 25% above budget.

    Variance at completion. Negative: a forecast overrun of BDT 200,000.

  7. \( ETC = EAC - AC = 1{,}000{,}000 - 240{,}000 = 760{,}000 \text{ BDT} \)

Answer

EV = BDT 192,000 · work complete = 24% · EAC = BDT 1,000,000 · VAC = −BDT 200,000 (with ETC = BDT 760,000).

Why this is worse than the same CPI at 70% complete. CPI is a rate, not a total, so an identical CPI can hide very different amounts of remaining exposure. At 24% complete, 76% of the work is still ahead, and every taka of it is forecast to be spent at the same 0.80 efficiency, so the overrun keeps accumulating for a long time. At 70% complete the same inefficiency has only 30% of the work left to damage, so although the EAC formula gives the same 1,000,000 forecast, far more of that cost is already sunk and known rather than still to be incurred under an unproven assumption.

There is a second, more practical reason. The EAC formula assumes the current CPI continues unchanged, and that assumption is far more fragile early in a project, when a small early problem, an underestimated component or a slow start, can distort the ratio, than it is at 70% when the rate has been measured across most of the work. So the early project has both more exposure remaining and a less reliable forecast of it. The lecture's own interpretation of CPI as value generated per taka spent is what makes this visible: the number describes a rate that will be applied to whatever work remains, and how much work remains is exactly what CPI does not tell you.

Topic 10

SWOT and risk (EMV)

The second calculation topic. Short, formulaic, and very likely to appear.

SWOT analysis

SWOT is the oldest framework in the course and the easiest to answer badly, because everyone thinks they already know it. The part people forget is the axis that actually organises it: strengths and weaknesses are internal, opportunities and threats are external. Internal means inside your control, things you own or lack. External means in the environment, things that happen to you.

DimensionNatureDefinitionProject examples
StrengthsInternalUnique capabilities, resources and advantagesVeteran engineering team, strong brand equity, patented IP
WeaknessesInternalDeficiencies and constraints hindering executionTight budget, legacy technical debt, missing niche expertise
OpportunitiesExternalFavourable market trends and strategic openingsGrowing demand, technology integrations, regulatory support
ThreatsExternalExternal risks capable of disrupting outcomesMacroeconomic downturn, new competitors, supply chain disruption
The classic SWOT error

"Our marketing budget is small" is a weakness, not a threat, because it is a property of your own organisation. "A well-funded competitor is entering our market" is a threat, because it exists outside you. Students routinely file every negative under threats and lose marks for it. Ask one question: is this thing inside my company or outside it?

Project risk and Expected Monetary Value

Your lecture's definition of risk is broader than everyday usage, and the breadth is examinable: a risk is any uncertain event or condition that, if it occurs, exerts a positive or negative impact on project scope, schedule, cost or quality. Positive risks are called opportunities; negative risks are called threats. So a sudden surge in demand is a risk in the technical sense, even though it is good news, because it is uncertain and it would change your outcomes.

Two numbers quantify any risk. Probability \(P\), between 0.0 and 1.0, is how likely it is. Impact \(I\) is the financial or operational effect if it happens, signed positive for opportunities and negative for threats. Multiply them and you get that risk's expected contribution; sum across all risks and you get the project's total exposure.

Expected Monetary Value
\[ \text{EMV} = \sum (P_i \times I_i) \]

What does the resulting number actually mean? It is a probability-weighted average, so it is the amount you would expect to gain or lose on average if this exact situation played out many times. No individual project will experience precisely the EMV, because in reality each risk either happens or does not. Its usefulness is in sizing contingency reserves and comparing options, not in predicting any single outcome.

The lecture works two examples. The small one has a supplier delay (P 0.30, I −$10,000, giving −$3,000), a competitor launch (P 0.50, I −$15,000, giving −$7,500), and a demand surge (P 0.40, I +$12,000, giving +$4,800), for a total EMV of −$5,700. The fitness app case is larger: marketing budget limitation (0.70 × −$50,000 = −$35,000), competition from established apps (0.50 × −$100,000 = −$50,000), post-launch bugs (0.40 × −$30,000 = −$12,000), and a demand surge (0.60 × +$75,000 = +$45,000), summing to −$52,000. The lecture's strategic reading is the part to imitate: management must mitigate marketing reach and bug testing while allocating contingency funds to protect ROI.

Exam technique for EMV

Always lay it out as a table with a signed impact column and a product column, then sum the product column. Three things earn marks reliably: signs (threats negative, opportunities positive), a stated total, and an interpretation sentence that says what the number means for the project. Losing the sign on one opportunity is the single most common way students turn a correct method into a wrong answer.

Question 10.3 · hard · 5 marks · decision problem

A project manager must choose one of two vendors.

Vendor A: 70% chance of on-time delivery, saving BDT 300,000; 30% chance of delay, costing BDT 500,000.
Vendor B: 50% chance of on-time delivery, saving BDT 400,000; 50% chance of delay, costing BDT 250,000.

Calculate the EMV for each and recommend a vendor. Then explain one circumstance under which the higher-EMV vendor would be the wrong choice.

Show worked solution

  1. \( \text{EMV}_A = (0.70 \times +300{,}000) + (0.30 \times -500{,}000) \)

    Apply the EMV formula to Vendor A's two outcomes. Each branch is a possible event with its own probability and signed impact, and the two probabilities sum to 1.0, which confirms the outcomes are exhaustive.

  2. \( = 210{,}000 - 150{,}000 \)

    Multiply each branch out: \(0.70 \times 300{,}000 = 210{,}000\) and \(0.30 \times 500{,}000 = 150{,}000\), the second carrying a negative sign because a delay is a threat.

  3. \( \text{EMV}_A = +60{,}000 \text{ BDT} \)

    Sum the branches, as the formula's summation requires.

  4. \( \text{EMV}_B = (0.50 \times +400{,}000) + (0.50 \times -250{,}000) \)

    Same method applied to Vendor B.

  5. \( = 200{,}000 - 125{,}000 \)

    Multiply each branch out.

    Sum. Both options are positive overall, so the decision is between two acceptable choices rather than between a good and a bad one.

  7. \( \text{EMV}_B - \text{EMV}_A = 75{,}000 - 60{,}000 = 15{,}000 \text{ BDT} \)

    Compare the two. Quantifying the gap matters, because a BDT 15,000 advantage is small relative to the sums at stake, which is the opening for the final part of the question.

Answer

EMVA = +BDT 60,000, EMVB = +BDT 75,000. On expected monetary value alone, choose Vendor B, which is better by BDT 15,000.

When the higher-EMV choice would be wrong. EMV is an average across many hypothetical repetitions, but this decision is made once, and averages hide the spread of outcomes. Vendor B carries a 50% chance of a delay, against 30% for Vendor A, so B fails almost twice as often even though its failure costs less. If the project cannot absorb a delay at all, for example because it is tied to a regulatory deadline, a contractual penalty, or a launch date already announced to customers, then the probability of failure matters more than its average cost, and Vendor A is the correct choice despite the lower EMV.

The general principle worth stating: EMV is the right decision rule when the organisation faces many such decisions and can absorb individual bad outcomes, because the averages then genuinely materialise over time. It is the wrong rule for a single decision whose bad branch is unsurvivable, since you never get to experience the average. The BDT 15,000 advantage is small enough that any meaningful asymmetry in consequences overturns it.

Topic 11

The 5-mark answer engine

The method. If you internalise one section of this guide, make it this one.

Everything above is content. This section is technique, and technique is what converts content into marks when you have twelve minutes and a case study you have never seen.

The first five minutes: reading the case

Do not read the case study like a story. Read it like an examiner who has hidden five answers in it. Every case in this pattern contains the same kinds of planted evidence, and knowing what to hunt for turns a page and a half of prose into a structured set of notes.

As you read, underline and label in the margin:

  • Personality scores, if present. They are Q4, guaranteed. Note which are extreme, above 80 or below 50.
  • A before-and-after comparison, usually a table of Year 1 versus Year 3. Whatever was deleted between the two columns is the answer to Q1 and Q3.
  • Churn or exit survey percentages. The largest category is nearly always the diagnosis.
  • Support ticket counts. The top driver tells you what customers want and are not getting.
  • A pile of raw numbers of mixed quality. That pile is Q2, the KPI selection question.
  • A named crisis, usually a person resigning or an investor imposing a deadline. That is the trigger for Q5.
  • Two or three strategic options listed at the end. These are for a recommendation question, and the right answer is usually the one that fits the company's proven capability and funding position, not the largest number.
The single most useful reading habit

Find the sentence in the case where a customer says what went wrong, in their own words. In the Fall 2025 paper it was "It was a learning community. Now it's just another course platform." That sentence is the answer to Q1, restated by the examiner in plain language. Cases in this style almost always contain one.

The answer template

Every question in this pattern can be answered with the same four-move structure. It takes about 150 words, which is right for five marks.

The four moves

1. Name it. State the concept from lecture in its proper vocabulary, in one sentence.
2. Anchor it. Quote a specific fact, number or phrase from the case.
3. Explain the mechanism. Say how the concept produces the observed outcome. This is where the marks concentrate.
4. Consequence or recommendation. Say what it costs the business, or what should be done.

Here it is running on a Q1-style question, with the moves labelled so you can see the joints:

Worked example of the template

[Name it] The core failure is a breakdown of value proposition delivery at scale. [Anchor it] At 200 students the product was weekly live Q&A run by the founder with an active peer community; at 18,000 it is a recorded video library with a three to five day response delay and a dead community. [Mechanism] What customers were paying for was accountability and access to a human, not content, so scaling by adding recorded video preserved the visible artefact while deleting the mechanism that made it work, which is why 38% of churned users say the material is "too theoretical, not applicable at work". [Consequence] Stripped of that differentiation the product now competes with Coursera and YouTube on passive content volume, where it cannot win on price, which is the commoditisation trap the 18% who left for cheaper platforms represent.

That is four sentences, and it contains three quoted case facts, one named lecture concept, one causal mechanism and one business consequence. This is what a five out of five looks like.

Each of the five slots has a natural opening sentence. Memorise these and you never lose time deciding how to start.

A note on the structural question

Q5 is the one with the least direct lecture support, so here is the reasoning it expects. The Fall 2025 answer recommends appointing a Chief Operating Officer or Head of Product and establishing a formal Investment and Strategy Committee with the co-founder. The justification runs: this shields the founder from operational over-customisation and personnel bottlenecks, enforces rigorous unit economics and execution discipline, and frees the founder to focus on the things she is actually good at, pedagogy, brand and community.

The generalisable logic is that a structural recommendation should do three things. It should remove the founder from the bottleneck the case has demonstrated. It should install a decision-forcing mechanism where the case shows paralysis, which is what a committee with the investor does. And it should let the founder keep doing what they are demonstrably good at, since the answer is to redesign the role, not to criticise the person. If you build your answer on those three legs it will fit almost any case in this pattern.

Time management

Ninety minutes, thirty marks. Budget it as: 10 minutes reading and annotating the case, then 14 minutes per question (70 minutes total), leaving 10 minutes to finish anything short. If you fall behind, write the four moves as four short labelled sentences rather than a paragraph. An examiner can find the marks in a terse structured answer; they cannot find them in an unfinished elegant one.

Question 11.1 · drill

Practise the template on this fragment without writing a full answer. A subscription meal service reports: Year 1, 120 subscribers, 14 home chefs, personalised menus, 96% on-time delivery. Year 3, 3,200 subscribers, one central kitchen, fixed rotating menu, 78% on-time delivery. Churn survey: 41% say "food quality inconsistent". Write only the first sentence of a diagnosis answer, using the "Name it" move.

Show model answer
What this drills Move 1 of the four-move template, applied to a Q1-style diagnosis. The skill being trained is naming a lecture concept in the first sentence rather than beginning with a description of the case, which is what weak answers do.

A strong first sentence: "The core failure is the same value proposition breakdown at scale seen when a personalised, high-touch service is industrialised: the company scaled its volume of meals while deleting the personalisation and reliability that customers were actually buying."

Why it works. It names the concept in lecture vocabulary within the first eight words, and it states the mechanism (volume scaled, value deleted) before any case detail arrives. The examiner reading quickly can already see you know what the question is about.

A weak first sentence, for contrast: "In Year 1 the company had 120 subscribers and 14 home chefs, but by Year 3 it had grown to 3,200 subscribers with a central kitchen." This is true, it is relevant, and it earns nothing, because it restates the case back to the examiner instead of interpreting it. Save the case facts for move 2, where they support a claim you have already made.

What moves 2 to 4 would then carry: anchor with the on-time delivery fall from 96% to 78% and the 41% citing inconsistent quality; explain the mechanism, that 14 chefs cooking personalised menus produced variety and ownership, while one central kitchen on a fixed rotation produced neither, so the operational change silently rewrote the product; then state the consequence, that the service now competes on price against ordinary caterers rather than on the personalisation that justified its premium.

MVP

Leanest functional version enabling maximal validated learning with minimal engineering. Skateboard, not a wheel.

Big Five, the short version

Neuroticism always harmful. Conscientiousness helps early, hinders at scale. Openness and agreeableness help early only. Extraversion does nothing.

KPI vs metric

KPI measures strategic outcome; metric measures process execution. Test: can it rise while the business declines?

SWOT axis

Strengths and weaknesses internal. Opportunities and threats external. A small budget is a weakness, not a threat.

Diversification

Concentric = technologically related. Horizontal = unrelated tech, same customers. Vertical = up or down the value chain. Conglomerate = unrelated everything.
