How to use this guide

Syllabus item 5 — Cyber Law, with "sections" called out explicitly.

Your syllabus lists exactly one sub-point under cyber law: sections. That is a strong signal. It means the examinable core is the statutory table — which offence falls under which section, and what penalty attaches to it — and Section 3 of this guide is built entirely around drilling that until it is automatic.

The rest of the lecture (domains, cybercrime categories, cyber insurance, professional ethics, due diligence) is covered here too, because it supplies the context those sections are applied in and because scenario questions draw on it. But if you are short of time, Section 3 is the one to memorise cold.

Why section questions are the best marks on the paper

They are pure recall with unambiguous answers. There is no judgement to exercise and no arithmetic to slip on — either you know that Section 27 carries up to 14 years and up to 10,000,000 BDT, or you don't. Ninety minutes of drilling the table in Section 3 is the highest-return revision available to you across all five topics.

One honest note about the legislation

Bangladesh's cyber legislation has been revised more than once, and the framework has been renamed across those revisions. Your lecture presents this material as the Digital Security Act (DSA) / Cyber Security Framework with the section numbers and penalties reproduced below. Use the faculty's figures in the exam — the marking scheme is written against the lecture, not against whichever amendment is currently in force. If you want to reference the framework by name safely, write "under the Digital Security Act / Cyber Security Framework as covered in the course."

Topic 01

What cyber law is — and its six domains

The definition and the domain table. Quick marks.

Cyber Law — also called Internet Law — is the comprehensive legal framework governing internet operations, digital telecommunications, computing hardware, electronic data privacy and online transactions.

Its purpose is simple to state: the physical world already had laws for theft, forgery, trespass and sabotage, but those laws were written for physical objects and physical places. Cyber law extends legal protection into a space where nothing is physical, an offender may be in another country, and the "property" stolen can be copied without being removed.

The six key domains

Domain Covers
1. CybercrimeHacking, malware propagation, identity theft, DDoS
2. Data privacyPersonal data governance, cross-border data flows (GDPR)
3. Intellectual propertyCopyright, software piracy, patents, digital media
4. Electronic commerceSmart contracts, digital signatures, consumer rights
5. National securityCritical information infrastructure, cyber terrorism
6. Freedom of speechBalancing public expression against online defamation
Note the sixth domain

Domain 6 is the only one that is a balancing act rather than a prohibition. The other five say "this is illegal"; freedom of speech requires weighing legitimate expression against defamation. If a question asks which domain is most contested, or which raises the hardest policy questions, that is the one — and it is where cyber law intersects with human rights concerns.

Topic 02

Cybercrime categories and cyber insurance

Two short lists, both quotable.

The five major cybercrime classifications

  • DDoS attacks and botnets — overwhelming online infrastructure to disrupt service availability. (Attacks the availability pillar.)
  • Identity theft and social engineering — stealing digital identities, credentials or PII via phishing and impersonation. (Attacks authentication and confidentiality.)
  • Cyberstalking and online harassment — systematic harassment, surveillance or intimidation of individuals online. Note this is the one category whose primary victim is a person rather than a system.
  • PUPs and malware — unauthorised distribution of trojans, spyware and ransomware. (PUP = Potentially Unwanted Program.)
  • Software piracy — unauthorised duplication, sharing or unlicensed installation of copyrighted software. This one sits in the intellectual property domain rather than the cybercrime domain proper.

Cyber insurance — the five coverage areas

Recall from the Risk Management topic that insurance is the Transfer treatment response. Here is what a policy actually covers:

  1. Direct financial loss — losses from phishing, invoice redirection and email spoofing.
  2. Fraudulent transactions — unauthorised electronic banking or card transfers.
  3. System restoration — the cost of cleaning malware and rebuilding corrupted databases.
  4. Extortion losses — forensic investigation and ransom negotiation under legal oversight.
  5. Legal liability and defence — legal representation and damages arising from privacy lawsuits.
Watch out — the boundary of insurance

Every item on that list is money. Insurance does not restore reputation, does not undo regulatory sanction, and does not discharge the due diligence obligation in Section 4 — under which an organisation retains legal liability even when it is the victim. Whenever an exam scenario mentions insurance, say this: transfer moves financial liability, not responsibility.

Topic 03 · priority

The statutory sections and penalties

Syllabus 5.1. This is the section your syllabus names. Memorise the table.

Six sections, each defining an offence and its maximum penalty. Read the table once, then use the memory system below it, then test yourself on the scenario drill.

Section Offence Maximum penalty
Section 18 Unauthorised computer access
Accessing a computer system without permission		 Simple access: up to 6 months prison or 200,000 BDT fine
Malicious intent / substantial harm: up to 3 years or 1,000,000 BDT
Section 19 Damage to systems, malware infiltration, data corruption
Infiltrating malware, damaging databases, denial of service		 Up to 7 years imprisonment or up to 11,000,000 BDT
Section 20 Source code modification and tampering
Altering, concealing or destroying source code		 Up to 3 years imprisonment or up to 300,000 BDT
Section 22 Digital and electronic forgery
Fabricating digital signatures, identities or documents		 Up to 5 years imprisonment or up to 500,000 BDT
Section 27 Cyber terrorism and infrastructure sabotage

How to memorise it in ten minutes

Pattern 1 — the matched pair

Section 20 → 3 years / 300,000 BDT. Section 22 → 5 years / 500,000 BDT. In both, the fine is the number of years × 100,000. Learn them as a pair and each one reminds you of the other. They are also both "document-level" offences — tampering with code, forging documents — which is why they carry the mildest sentences of the six.

Pattern 2 — the twin maximums

Sections 27 and 32 are identical: 14 years / 10,000,000 BDT. These are the two most serious offences — cyber terrorism and hacking — and they sit at the ceiling. If a scenario is grave, the answer is one of these two, and you only have to decide which: is national security or critical infrastructure involved (27), or is it intrusion, deletion and modification of an ordinary target (32)?

Pattern 3 — the two odd ones out

Section 18 is the only section with a two-tier penalty, which makes it easy to spot: simple access is 6 months / 200,000 BDT, and access with malicious intent or substantial harm escalates to 3 years / 1,000,000 BDT. Section 19 is the only one with an unusual fine figure — 11,000,000 BDT, higher than the ceiling sections' 10,000,000, paired with a lower 7-year sentence. That oddity is itself the memory hook.

The distinction that decides most scenario questions

Sections 18, 19 and 32 overlap in ordinary language — all three sound like "hacking." The examiner is testing whether you can separate them, so learn the boundary precisely:

If the offender… Section
…got in and merely looked, changing nothing18 — unauthorised access
…deployed malware, corrupted data, or ran a denial of service19 — damage / malware infiltration
…intruded on a network and deleted or modified data32 — hacking
…altered, hid or destroyed source code specifically20 — source code tampering
…faked a signature, identity or document22 — digital forgery
…targeted national security or critical infrastructure27 — cyber terrorism
Watch out

A real incident usually engages several sections at once, and a good answer says so. Someone who breaks into a network (18 or 32), deploys ransomware (19), and forges an administrator's credentials to do it (22) has committed three distinct offences. Do not hunt for the single "correct" section when the facts support more than one — list them all with the penalty for each, and name the most serious.

Question 1 · easy · recall drill

State the offence and the maximum penalty for each of Sections 18, 19, 20, 22, 27 and 32.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from Pure recall of the statutory offences and penalties table (Cyber Law lecture §3) — the exact item your syllabus flags as "sections." There is no reasoning here at all, only accuracy. Write it as a table and include both tiers for Section 18.

Section 18 — Unauthorised computer access. Simple access: up to 6 months' imprisonment or a fine up to 200,000 BDT. With malicious intent or causing substantial harm: up to 3 years or up to 1,000,000 BDT.

Section 19 — Damage to systems, malware infiltration, data corruption (infiltrating malware, damaging databases, denial of service). Up to 7 years' imprisonment or a fine up to 11,000,000 BDT.

Section 20 — Source code modification and tampering (altering, concealing or destroying source code). Up to 3 years or up to 300,000 BDT.

Section 22 — Digital and electronic forgery (fabricating digital signatures, identities or documents). Up to 5 years or up to 500,000 BDT.

Section 27 — Cyber terrorism and infrastructure sabotage (threatening national security, state ties or critical databases). Up to 14 years or up to 10,000,000 BDT.

Section 32 — Hacking offences (unauthorised data deletion, modification or network intrusion). Up to 14 years or up to 10,000,000 BDT.

Self-check before you move on

Cover the table and answer these five from memory: Which section has two penalty tiers? (18) Which two are identical? (27 and 32) Which has the highest fine? (19, at 11,000,000 BDT — not 27 or 32) What is Section 20's fine? (300,000) What is Section 22's sentence? (5 years). If you got all five, this topic is done.

Question 2 · medium · scenario mapping

For each of the following, identify the applicable section and state the maximum penalty. Where more than one section applies, list all of them.

  1. A student guesses a lecturer's password, logs into the results portal, reads the exam paper and logs out without changing anything.
  2. A former employee remotely deploys ransomware that encrypts the company's customer database.
  3. A developer, angry at being passed over for promotion, deletes portions of the company's application source code from the repository before resigning.
  4. A fraudster creates a fake digital signature to authorise a bank transfer in the name of a company director.
  5. A group breaches the national power grid's control systems intending to cause a blackout.
  6. An attacker intrudes into a hospital's network and deletes patient records.
Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from The statutory sections (§3) applied to facts — the most likely format for this topic on the paper, because it tests whether the table is understood rather than merely memorised. The discriminator is the 18 / 19 / 32 boundary: access alone, damage caused, or intrusion with deletion or modification.

1. Student reads the exam paper without changing anything. Section 18 — unauthorised computer access. The key fact is that nothing was altered, deleted or damaged, so Sections 19 and 32 do not engage. Which tier applies is arguable and a good answer says so: it is more than idle curiosity — accessing exam papers causes substantial harm to the institution's assessment integrity — so the higher tier of up to 3 years or up to 1,000,000 BDT is defensible. If treated as simple access, up to 6 months or 200,000 BDT. Note also that guessing a password to impersonate the lecturer edges toward Section 22 if any credential was fabricated rather than guessed.

2. Former employee deploys ransomware encrypting the customer database. Section 19 — damage to systems, malware infiltration and data corruption, which explicitly covers infiltrating malware and damaging databases. Up to 7 years or up to 11,000,000 BDT. Because the employee is former, their access was necessarily unauthorised, so Section 18 also engages, and if data was modified or the network intruded upon, Section 32 (up to 14 years or 10,000,000 BDT) applies as well. List all three and identify Section 32 as the most serious.

3. Developer deletes portions of the source code. Section 20 — source code modification and tampering, which covers altering, concealing or destroying source code. Up to 3 years or up to 300,000 BDT. This is the section's exact target, and it is why Section 20 exists separately from the general damage provisions. Note that the developer had legitimate repository access, so Section 18 does not obviously apply — the offence is what they did with authorised access, not the access itself.

4. Fake digital signature to authorise a bank transfer. Section 22 — digital and electronic forgery, covering the fabrication of digital signatures, identities or documents. Up to 5 years or up to 500,000 BDT. The fraudulent transfer itself would additionally attract ordinary financial fraud provisions outside this table.

5. Breach of the national power grid intending a blackout. Section 27 — cyber terrorism and infrastructure sabotage, since it threatens national security and critical infrastructure. Up to 14 years or up to 10,000,000 BDT. Section 32 (hacking) and Section 19 (damage) would also engage on the same facts, but 27 is the governing charge because the target's character — critical national infrastructure — is what the section exists to address. Naming 27 and explaining why it outranks 32 here is the full-mark answer.

6. Intrusion into a hospital network, deleting patient records. Section 32 — hacking offences, which covers unauthorised data deletion, modification and network intrusion — all three elements are present. Up to 14 years or up to 10,000,000 BDT. Section 19 also applies to the damage caused to the database, and Section 18 to the unauthorised access. Whether Section 27 is engaged is a genuine question worth raising: a hospital may qualify as critical infrastructure, in which case cyber terrorism could be charged if intent to threaten public safety were shown.

Marking tip

Three habits earn marks here. Quote the penalty every time, in both years and BDT. List every applicable section rather than picking one, then name the most serious. And justify the boundary in one clause — "nothing was altered, so Section 18 rather than 32" is worth more than the bare section number.

Topic 04

Professional ethics and the duty of due diligence

The do's and don'ts table, and what the law requires when you are breached.

The professional code of conduct

Category Do Don't
Data privacyAccess sensitive data only with written authorisationNever use internal privileged data for private profit
System integrityPerform routine security patching and auditsNever deploy unvetted, pirated or cracked software
User safetyEducate end-users on phishing and threat hygieneDo not fall for lottery/prize schemes or click suspicious links
AccountabilityAcknowledge errors and correct defects promptlyNever shift blame to colleagues or conceal breaches

Two of these connect directly to statutory sections and are worth flagging. "Access sensitive data only with written authorisation" is what keeps a professional on the right side of Section 18 — the authorisation is the entire legal difference. And "never conceal breaches" connects to the statutory reporting duty below.

The statutory duty of due diligence when compromised

Three obligations, and the third is the one examiners build questions around.

  1. Preserve forensic evidence. Retain uncorrupted server access logs, network packet captures and memory dumps. Note the tension with incident response: the instinct is to wipe and rebuild immediately, but that destroys the evidence, so preservation must happen before eradication.
  2. Statutory reporting. Promptly notify law enforcement bodies and regulatory authorities — in Bangladesh, BGD e-Gov CIRT (the Computer Incident Response Team).
  3. Due diligence obligation. Organisations maintain legal liability even when victimised. Failure to exercise reasonable cybersecurity diligence can result in severe civil regulatory penalties.
The sentence to memorise verbatim

"An organisation retains legal liability even when it is the victim; failure to exercise reasonable cybersecurity diligence can attract severe civil regulatory penalties." This one line is usable in almost every scenario question across the whole syllabus — risk management, VAPT, SOC and cyber law alike. It is why "we were attacked" is not a defence.

Question 3 · long answer · story-based

A mid-sized Bangladeshi e-commerce company discovers on a Monday morning that its customer database has been copied and is being offered for sale online. Investigation reveals that an attacker used credentials belonging to a junior developer, obtained through a phishing email sent three weeks earlier, and that the company had not applied a vendor security patch published four months ago.

The IT manager's first action is to wipe and rebuild the affected server. The CEO decides not to notify anyone, reasoning that the company is the victim and disclosure would damage the brand.

Analyse this scenario. Identify the offences committed against the company and the applicable sections, evaluate the company's own legal and ethical position, and state what should have been done.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from This is the faculty's story format built to test three things at once: the statutory sections (§3), the duty of due diligence (§4), and the professional code of conduct (§4). Crucially it has two wrongdoers — the attacker and, in a different sense, the company — and the examiner is checking whether you spot the second. It also links to Risk Management, since the unapplied patch is the Equifax root cause exactly.

Part 1 — offences committed against the company.

  • Section 18 — unauthorised computer access. The attacker used credentials they were not entitled to use. If the higher tier is engaged by the substantial harm caused, up to 3 years or 1,000,000 BDT; otherwise up to 6 months or 200,000 BDT.
  • Section 32 — hacking offences. Network intrusion and unauthorised access to data. Up to 14 years or up to 10,000,000 BDT, and this is the most serious charge available on these facts.
  • Section 22 — digital and electronic forgery is arguable, since the attacker used another person's digital identity to authenticate. If the phishing email impersonated the company or a legitimate service, the fabrication of that identity strengthens the case. Up to 5 years or 500,000 BDT.
  • Section 19 is not clearly engaged on these facts, and saying so demonstrates precision. The database was copied, not damaged, corrupted or denied — Section 19 addresses damage, malware infiltration and denial of service. If the phishing payload involved malware, Section 19 would then apply.

In the classification scheme from §2 this is identity theft and social engineering (the phishing) leading to a data breach.

Part 2 — the company's own position, which is where the marks concentrate. The company is a victim, and it is also in breach of its obligations in three separate ways.

(a) Failure of due diligence. The vendor patch had been published four months earlier and was not applied. Under the statutory duty of due diligence, an organisation maintains legal liability even when victimised, and failure to exercise reasonable cybersecurity diligence can result in severe civil regulatory penalties. A four-month-old unapplied patch is very difficult to characterise as reasonable diligence — this is precisely the Equifax root cause, a systemic failure in vulnerability management despite published vendor alerts. The CEO's reasoning that "we are the victim" is therefore legally wrong, and stating that plainly is the central point of the answer.

(b) Destruction of forensic evidence. The IT manager's immediate wipe-and-rebuild violated the first duty: preserve forensic evidence — uncorrupted server access logs, network packet captures and memory dumps. The consequences are serious and compounding. Law enforcement now cannot attribute the attack. The company cannot establish the breach's true scope, so it cannot tell affected customers what was taken. And it has destroyed the evidence that might have helped its own regulatory position by demonstrating what it did and did not do. Note the tension worth naming: the SOC incident response lifecycle does require eradication and recovery, but investigation comes first, at phase 3, precisely so that evidence is captured before systems are rebuilt.

(c) Failure to report, and concealment. The second duty is statutory reporting: prompt notification of law enforcement and regulatory authorities, specifically BGD e-Gov CIRT. The CEO's decision not to notify breaches this. It is also an ethical violation under the professional code, whose accountability row states plainly: never shift blame to colleagues or conceal breaches. And the reputational calculation is poor on its own terms — a breach discovered later, with concealment attached, is far more damaging than one disclosed promptly, as the Yahoo case demonstrates, where the delay between the 2013–14 breaches and 2016 disclosure contributed to a $350 million reduction in the acquisition price.

Part 3 — what should have been done.

  1. Before the incident. Apply vendor security patches within a defined SLA — the single control that would most likely have prevented this, per the professional code's "perform routine security patching and audits." Deliver phishing awareness training and simulation, since the entry vector was a phished credential and the code requires educating end-users on phishing and threat hygiene. Enforce MFA, which would have rendered the stolen credential insufficient on its own — the Colonial Pipeline lesson.
  2. On discovery. Preserve evidence first: capture memory dumps, packet captures and access logs, and image the affected server before touching it. Only then contain — isolate the host, revoke the compromised credentials — and only then eradicate and rebuild. Containment does not require destroying evidence.
  3. Reporting. Notify BGD e-Gov CIRT and the relevant regulatory authority promptly, and notify affected customers so they can protect themselves against credential reuse and fraud.
  4. Afterwards. Post-incident review, patch management remediation, and — recalling the Transfer response — note that a cyber insurance policy would have covered system restoration, forensic investigation and legal defence, though not the regulatory liability arising from the company's own negligence.
Marking tip

The question has a trap and a payoff. The trap is answering only Part 1 — listing the attacker's offences and stopping. The payoff is recognising that the company has three failures of its own: due diligence, evidence preservation and reporting. Lead Part 2 with the memorised line about retaining liability even when victimised, and the answer practically structures itself.

Question 4 · medium · cross-topic

A security consultant is hired to test a company's web application. Midway through, she discovers a flaw in a partner company's system that is connected to her client's network, and exploits it to demonstrate the risk. She reports both findings to her client.

Has she committed an offence? Identify the applicable sections and discuss her position with reference to professional ethics.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from A cross-topic question joining Cyber Law sections (§3) to the VAPT pre-engagement stage and to professional ethics (§4). The examiner is testing the single most important idea in penetration testing law: authorisation is scope-bound. A consultant with a contract is not thereby licensed to touch anything she can reach.

Short answer: yes, in respect of the partner company's system.

The client's application — lawful. She holds authorisation from the system owner. Under the VAPT methodology's pre-engagement stage, the signed rules of engagement define target scope, testing windows and legal authorisation, and that consent is what converts otherwise criminal acts into a contracted service. No offence.

The partner's system — unlawful. Her authorisation came from her client, and her client cannot consent on behalf of a third party. Consent must come from the owner of the system being tested. The fact that the partner's system was reachable from her client's network is irrelevant — reachability is not permission. The applicable sections:

  • Section 18 — unauthorised computer access. She accessed a system without the owner's permission. Up to 6 months or 200,000 BDT, or the higher tier of up to 3 years or 1,000,000 BDT if substantial harm resulted.
  • Section 32 — hacking offences would engage if the exploitation involved network intrusion or any modification of data. Up to 14 years or up to 10,000,000 BDT.
  • Section 19 would apply if the demonstration caused damage, corrupted data or disrupted the service. Up to 7 years or up to 11,000,000 BDT.

Does good intent help? Not as a defence to the access itself. Her motive was legitimate and she disclosed responsibly, which is likely to matter a great deal in practice — to prosecutorial discretion and to sentencing — but the offence under Section 18 is the unauthorised access, and intent is relevant chiefly to which tier applies rather than to whether the section is engaged.

Professional ethics. She has breached the code's first requirement: access sensitive data only with written authorisation. The ACM/IEEE Software Engineering Code makes the same demand at Principle 2 (Client and Employer) — utilise employer or client property only with formal authorisation and consent — and the partner's system is neither her client's property nor within her consent. Principle 1 (Public) is also in tension: the public interest in disclosing a genuine risk is real, but the code requires it be pursued through legitimate channels rather than by unauthorised action.

What she should have done. Stop at the point of discovery. Report to her client that a connected partner system appears vulnerable, without exploiting it, and recommend the client contact the partner to arrange properly authorised testing with its own rules of engagement. If she believed the risk was urgent, the correct route is coordinated disclosure to the partner, not demonstration. The lesson to state explicitly: a penetration testing contract is a permission slip with edges, and testing outside the agreed scope is unauthorised even though a contract exists.

Question 5 · short answer

Explain the statutory duty of due diligence. Why does an organisation remain liable even when it is the victim of an attack, and what should it do immediately upon discovering a compromise?

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from The statutory duty of due diligence (Cyber Law §4). Short, high- value and entirely predictable. The "why" half is the discriminator — it asks you to explain the principle, not just recite the three obligations.

The three obligations. On discovering a compromise an organisation must: (1) preserve forensic evidence — retain uncorrupted server access logs, network packet captures and memory dumps; (2) report promptly to law enforcement bodies and regulatory authorities, in Bangladesh the BGD e-Gov CIRT; and (3) satisfy the due diligence obligation — organisations maintain legal liability even when victimised, and failure to exercise reasonable cybersecurity diligence can result in severe civil regulatory penalties.

Why liability survives victimhood. Because the organisation holds other people's data and other people's trust, and it accepted a duty of care when it did so. The law distinguishes between being attacked and being negligently attacked. An organisation that patched promptly, monitored continuously and trained its staff, and was still breached by a novel technique, has met its duty. An organisation breached through a four-month-old unapplied patch, or through credentials with no MFA, has not — and the harm to its customers flows as much from its own omission as from the attacker's act. Liability tracks that omission. This is why the Equifax and Colonial Pipeline root causes matter legally and not only technically.

What to do immediately, in order. Preserve evidence first — image the affected systems and capture logs, packet captures and memory before anything is changed, because containment and rebuilding destroy exactly the artefacts an investigation needs. Then contain: isolate affected hosts and revoke compromised credentials. Then report to CIRT and the regulator, and notify affected individuals. Only then eradicate and recover. The sequencing matters: the SOC incident response lifecycle places investigation at phase 3, before containment, eradication and recovery, for precisely this reason.

The common mistake to name. Wiping and rebuilding a compromised server as the first response feels decisive and is a serious error — it destroys the evidence, prevents scope determination, and leaves the organisation unable to demonstrate what happened either to regulators or in its own defence.
