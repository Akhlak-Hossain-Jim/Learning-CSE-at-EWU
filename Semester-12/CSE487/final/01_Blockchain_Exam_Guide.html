Topic 01

Features of blockchain — the 5 pillars

Syllabus 1.1. Near-certain to appear as a "list and explain" question.

Before the features, the reason blockchain exists at all. Think about how money moved before it. If you want to send 5 dollars to a friend, you don't hand the money over yourself — you ask a bank to do it. The bank keeps a private notebook (a ledger: simply a record of who owns what and who paid whom), it subtracts 5 from your line and adds 5 to your friend's line, and everyone accepts the result because everyone trusts the bank.

That arrangement has three weaknesses, and the lecture named all three. The bank is a central intermediary, so the transfer is never truly person-to-person — the bank has to be awake and willing. The bank is a single point of failure: if its server dies, every transfer dies with it. And the bank can censor or delay — it can freeze your account or hold your transfer for three days, and you have no recourse because it owns the only copy of the notebook.

Blockchain's answer is blunt: get rid of the single notebook. Give everybody a copy, make it impossible to erase a line once written, and let the network agree on new lines by mathematical rule rather than by trusting an institution. The five features below are just that idea broken into parts.

The five pillars, one at a time

1. Distribution. The ledger is not stored in one place. A copy of it lives on every participating computer. Each of those computers is called a node — that word just means "one machine taking part in the network." If a hundred nodes are running, there are a hundred copies of the whole history. Knock out ninety-nine and the record survives.

2. Decentralisation. Distribution is about where the data sits; decentralisation is about who decides. No single entity has authority to approve, reject or rewrite a transaction. Decisions are made collectively by the nodes following an agreed rule. You can have one without the other — a company could copy its database to fifty of its own servers (distributed) while still being the only one allowed to write to it (centralised). Blockchain insists on both.

3. Immutability. "Immutable" means unchangeable. Once a transaction is written into the chain it is write-once and append-only: you may add new records to the end, but you may never edit or delete an old one. Section 4 shows the machinery that enforces this — it is not a policy, it is arithmetic.

4. Tokenisation. A token is a digital stand-in for something of value, recorded on the chain. It might represent a currency unit, a share of a building, a university certificate, or a barrel of crude oil. Tokenisation is what lets a blockchain track real-world assets and not just numbers.

5. Encryption / Cryptography. Cryptography is the branch of maths used to hide and to prove things. Blockchain uses two pieces of it: hashing, which fingerprints data (Section 4), and asymmetric key pairs, which prove who authorised a transaction (Section 3). Together they secure identity and integrity.

Key idea

Memorise them as D-D-I-T-E: Distribution, Decentralisation, Immutability, Tokenisation, Encryption. In the exam, name the pillar and say what breaks without it — that second half is where the marks are.

Watch out

Distribution and decentralisation get mixed up constantly, and an examiner who asks for five distinct pillars will not accept the same answer twice. One-line separator: distribution = many copies of the data; decentralisation = no single controller of the decisions.

Question 1 · short answer

State the five core pillars of blockchain and explain, for each, what problem of the traditional banking model it removes.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from This tests recall of the 5 core pillars (Lecture 1, §3 "Blockchain Foundations") plus your ability to tie them to the limitations of traditional banking from §1 of the same lecture. The examiner is not asking for a list — the phrase "what problem it removes" means they want the pillar → banking weakness mapping. Answer in pairs and you will collect nearly full marks.

1. Distribution. The ledger is replicated across every node, so there is no one machine holding the only copy. This removes the single point of failure the lecture identified in centralised banking — where a server outage stops all transactions.

2. Decentralisation. No single authority controls validation. This removes the central intermediary requirement: transactions become genuinely peer-to-peer, so the bank no longer has to be online and approve every exchange, and it removes the bank's power to censor or delay a transfer.

3. Immutability. Records are append-only and tamper-evident. This removes the risk the lecture called silent modification by database administrators — in the centralised comparison table, message modification is "possible silently"; on-chain it is "practically impossible."

4. Tokenisation. Real and digital assets can be represented on-chain, which lets the ledger settle ownership of things banks traditionally could not track directly — land deeds, certificates, commodities (the Lecture 1 case studies).

5. Encryption / cryptography. Hashing plus asymmetric key pairs secure identity and integrity, which replaces the model where trust sits in an institution with a model where trust sits in the protocol — exactly the "trust model" row of the lecture's centralisation/decentralisation table.

Marking tip

Five pillars, five named banking weaknesses, one sentence each. If you have time, close with the summary line: trust moves from an entity to a protocol. That single sentence is the thesis of the whole lecture.

Topic 02

Scenarios — why blockchain is needed

Syllabus 1.2. The faculty's three worked scenarios, plus the "story" format he favours.

Your faculty teaches this topic through stories, and you said his theory questions are often "here is a situation — explain it using the concept." So learn these three scenarios as arguments, not as anecdotes. Each one has the same shape: a centralised record exists, someone can quietly change it or hide it, and the injured party has no way to prove anything.

Scenario 1 — Alice pays Bob $5

Alice wants to send Bob five dollars. The bank is the sole ledger keeper, so the payment only happens if the bank's systems are up, and only if the bank permits it. Failure modes: downtime (single point of failure) and censorship/latency. What blockchain changes: the transaction is broadcast to a peer-to-peer network and settled by consensus, so no single party can stop or stall it.

Scenario 2 — the land ownership dispute

Land records sit in a centralised land office. A clerk with database access alters a record, or the paper file is destroyed in a fire, and suddenly two people hold deeds to the same plot. Failure modes: silent modification and record loss. What blockchain changes: immutability plus distribution — the deed history is append-only and replicated, so an altered record is detectable (Section 4 explains why) and a destroyed copy is irrelevant because every node has one.

Scenario 3 — charity fund transparency

Donors give money for flood victims through a centralised collection. There is no way to verify that 100% of the money reached the victims, because the only accounts are the ones the charity chooses to publish. Failure mode: opacity — the donor must simply trust. What blockchain changes: a transparent, publicly-readable ledger means each donation and each disbursement is independently verifiable by anyone, without asking the charity's permission.

Key idea

The three scenarios map to three distinct properties, and an examiner wants that mapping: payments → decentralisation (removes the gatekeeper), land records → immutability (removes silent tampering), charity → transparency (removes the need to trust a report).

Question 2 · long answer · story-based

A city hospital keeps every patient's medical history in one central database managed by its IT department. A patient discovers that a diagnosis recorded three years ago has been altered, and no one can determine when it changed or who changed it. Meanwhile the patient cannot get a copy of their own file released to a specialist at another hospital without the IT department's approval, which takes weeks.

Analyse this story. Identify the failures, map each to the blockchain property that addresses it, and describe how a blockchain-based system would handle the same case.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from This is the faculty's signature format: an unseen story that is structurally identical to a taught scenario. It tests whether you can take the centralisation failure modes (Lecture 1 §2) and the 5 pillars (§3), and apply them to a case you have not been given — here, a variant of Case Study 03, Medical Report Access Control (§6). The examiner wants failure → property → mechanism, three columns, not a general essay about how good blockchain is.

Step 1 — name the failures. There are three, and they are the three the lecture lists for centralised architectures.

  • Silent modification. A record changed with no trace is exactly the "message modification: possible silently by database admins" row of the centralisation table. The database stores the current value, not the history, so there is nothing to audit.
  • Single point of failure and control. One IT department owns the only copy, so it is both the outage risk and the gatekeeper.
  • Censorship and latency. The weeks-long approval to release a file is the same failure the lecture describes when a bank can "freeze, delay, or censor" a transaction — the intermediary must act before anything can happen.

Step 2 — map each failure to the property that fixes it.

  • Silent modification → immutability. Records are append-only, so a correction is written as a new entry with its own timestamp rather than overwriting the old one. The three-year-old diagnosis and the change to it both remain visible, which answers "when" and "who".
  • Single point of failure → distribution. The ledger is replicated across nodes (in a hospital consortium, one node per participating institution), so no single IT department's outage takes the record offline.
  • Censorship / gatekeeping → decentralisation, with access granted by the patient's own key rather than by an administrator's approval.

Step 3 — describe the mechanism, using Case Study 03. The lecture's medical model does not put the record itself on the chain, and you should say so explicitly because it is the subtle point. The full encrypted health record stays off-chain in a secure database; what goes on-chain is the record's cryptographic hash plus the access permissions. That gives three things at once: the hash proves the off-chain file has not been altered (change one character and the hash no longer matches), the on-chain permission list means the patient grants the specialist view rights directly with their private key, and patient privacy is preserved because no diagnosis is ever published to the network. The same pattern appears in Case Study 02, where universities put certificate hashes on Hyperledger Fabric and keep the documents off-chain.

Step 4 — the honest limitation. Strong answers add one line of balance: blockchain guarantees that the record has not been altered since it was written. It cannot guarantee the diagnosis was correct when first entered. Immutability protects integrity, not accuracy.

Marking tip

Answer in the three-column shape — failure → property → mechanism — and name the specific case study. Naming Case Study 03 and the on-chain-hash / off-chain-document split is what separates a top answer from a generic one.

Topic 03

Elements of blockchain

Syllabus 1.3. The defining equation, the five layers, and what is inside a block.

Your faculty gives blockchain a compact definition as a sum of three ingredients. Learn it in this exact form — it is the cleanest thing to open an answer with.

The faculty's definition
\[ \text{Blockchain} = DL + CP + C \]

Reading it out: \(DL\), the Distributed Ledger — the shared database replicated across participants. \(CP\), the Consensus Protocol — the algorithmic rules by which the whole network agrees on what is true (Section 7). \(C\), Cryptography — hashing and asymmetric key pairs, which secure the data (Section 4).

Strip any one out and it stops being a blockchain. No \(DL\) and you have a normal database. No \(CP\) and the copies drift apart with no way to reconcile them. No \(C\) and anyone can forge a transaction or edit history undetected.

The five architectural layers

The lecture stacks blockchain into five layers. Learn them top-down, because the top is what a user touches and the bottom is what actually runs code.

  1. Application layer — what people interact with: dApps (decentralised applications), user interfaces, and smart contracts. A smart contract is a program stored on the chain that executes automatically when its conditions are met — Solidity contracts on Ethereum, or Go/Java chaincode on Hyperledger Fabric.
  2. Data layer — blocks, Merkle trees, digital signatures, transactions. This is where the record physically lives, and it is Section 4's territory.
  3. Consensus layer — PoW, PoS, DPoS, PBFT, PoET, PoAh, Raft. The agreement rules (Section 7).
  4. Network layer — the peer-to-peer network, node discovery, sharding (splitting the network into partitions so transactions can be processed in parallel, raising throughput), and DAG structures.
  5. Execution layer — the virtual machines that actually run the compiled contract code: the EVM (Ethereum Virtual Machine), Docker containers, compilers.

Inside a single block

Every block has two parts, and knowing which field sits in which part is a frequent one-mark question.

The block header holds the metadata: block number, timestamp, nonce, previous block hash, and Merkle root hash. The block body holds the actual list of verified transactions.

Two of those header fields do the structural work. The previous block hash is the link that chains block \(N\) to block \(N-1\) — that is what makes it a chain. The Merkle root is a single fingerprint of every transaction in the body — that is what makes the body tamper-evident. They are different things and the exam will try to make you swap them.

Watch out — this one cost you in the diagnostic

Previous block hash ≠ Merkle root. The previous block hash points backwards, between blocks, and secures history. The Merkle root points downwards, inside one block, and secures that block's transactions. Both are in the header; only one of them links blocks together.

Digital signatures — proving who sent it

Hashing proves data has not changed; it does not prove who wrote it. That is the job of asymmetric cryptography. Every participant holds a mathematically linked pair of keys: a private key, kept secret, and a public key, shared with everybody.

To send a transaction, Alice takes the message digest (the hash of her transaction) and signs it with her private key. Anyone on the network can then run a verification using Alice's public key, which succeeds only if the signature really was produced by the matching private key and only if the message has not changed since signing. So one signature proves two things at once: authenticity (it was Alice) and integrity (nothing was edited).

Key idea

Sign with the private key, verify with the public key. The direction is the whole exam question. Only Alice can produce the signature; everybody can check it.

Topic 04 · priority

Merkle root — built from scratch

Syllabus 1.4. The diagnostic flagged this as a blank spot, so this section starts from zero.

First: what a hash function actually is

A cryptographic hash function is a machine that takes any input \(M\) — one letter, a sentence, a 4 GB video — and returns a fixed-length string called the digest or hash. Bitcoin uses SHA-256, which always returns 256 bits regardless of input size.

Three properties matter here, and only three:

  • Deterministic. The same input always gives the same digest. Hash "hello" a thousand times and you get the same answer a thousand times.
  • Avalanche effect. Change the input by one bit and the digest changes completely and unpredictably — not slightly, completely. This is the property that makes tampering detectable.
  • One-way. Given a digest you cannot work backwards to the input.

Notation you need: \(\text{Hash}(x)\) means "the digest of \(x\)", and the symbol \(\|\) means concatenation — glue two things end to end into one string before hashing. So \(\text{Hash}(T1 \| T2)\) means "stick \(T1\) and \(T2\) together, then hash the combined string." It is not addition, and it is not two separate hashes.

Now the problem the Merkle tree solves

A block might contain three thousand transactions. Suppose you want to check that none of them has been altered. The clumsy way is to keep a copy of all three thousand and compare them one by one — enormous storage, slow check.

The clever way is to compress all three thousand into one value in such a way that changing any single transaction changes that value. Then you store the one value in the block header, and any node can verify the entire block body by recomputing it. That single value is the Merkle root, and the pairwise hashing structure that produces it is the Merkle tree (also called a binary hash tree).

The construction, in the faculty's notation

Take a block with four transactions \(T1, T2, T3, T4\). Build upward in pairs:

Merkle root for four transactions — the faculty's form
\[ H1 = \text{Hash}(T1 \| T2) \] \[ H2 = \text{Hash}(T3 \| T4) \] \[ \text{Merkle Root} = \text{Hash}(H1 \| H2) \]

The picture is a pyramid: transactions at the bottom, pairs hashed together to make the level above, and so on until only one value is left at the top.

The tree, drawn
Key idea — the answer to the diagnostic question

The Merkle root is the single hash at the top of the pairwise hash tree built from all transactions in a block. The header stores it because it lets any node verify that no transaction in the body was altered — recompute the tree, compare one value. It is a proof of integrity, not merely a space-saving summary, and it is not the previous block hash.

Why tampering is caught — the argument in full

Suppose an attacker edits \(T3\), changing the payment amount. Follow the consequences upward:

  1. \(T3\) changed, so by the avalanche effect \(\text{Hash}(T3\|T4)\) is now a completely different value. \(H2\) has changed.
  2. \(H2\) feeds into \(\text{Hash}(H1\|H2)\), so the Merkle root changes too.
  3. The Merkle root sits in the block header, so the header's content has changed — which means the hash of the whole block changes.
  4. The next block stores that value as its previous block hash. It no longer matches. The chain is visibly broken at that point.
  5. To hide the damage the attacker would have to recompute this block's proof of work and every block after it, faster than the honest network is extending the chain — while also convincing a majority of nodes to accept the rewrite.

That cascade — one transaction, one leaf, one root, one broken link — is the complete answer to "how does blockchain achieve immutability?" Write it as a chain of consequences and it is very hard to lose marks.

Odd numbers of transactions

The tree needs pairs, so what happens with five transactions? The standard rule is that the last unpaired hash is duplicated and paired with itself. With \(T1 \ldots T5\): pair \(T1{+}T2\) and \(T3{+}T4\) normally, then \(T5\) has no partner, so compute \(\text{Hash}(T5 \| T5)\). Now three values sit on that level; the last one is again unpaired, so it doubles with itself again, and you continue until one value remains.

Watch out — faculty notation vs. textbook notation

Your lecture writes the leaves as the transactions themselves: \(H1=\text{Hash}(T1\|T2)\). Most textbooks first hash each transaction individually (\(HA=\text{Hash}(T1)\)) and then pair the hashes. Both give a valid Merkle tree; the shape and the argument are identical. Use the faculty's form in the exam — it is what the marking scheme will be written against — but do not panic if a textbook diagram has one extra level.

Question 3 · easy

A block contains four transactions \(T1, T2, T3, T4\). Write the full computation of the Merkle root, showing every intermediate value, and state which part of the block each result is stored in.

Show worked solution

Attempt it first. Each line below says why it is allowed to follow the line above.

  1. \( T1,\; T2,\; T3,\; T4 \)

    Start from the four transactions in the block body. These are the leaves of the tree.

  2. \( H1 = \text{Hash}(T1 \| T2) \)

    Pair the first two and concatenate: \(\|\) glues \(T1\) and \(T2\) into one string, and the hash function fingerprints the result. One hash now stands for two transactions.

  3. \( H2 = \text{Hash}(T3 \| T4) \)

    Same rule applied to the remaining pair. Every node on a level is formed the same way — that regularity is what makes it a binary hash tree.

  4. \( \text{Merkle Root} = \text{Hash}(H1 \| H2) \)

    Repeat one level up. Only two values remain, so concatenating and hashing them collapses the whole block into a single digest. No values are left to pair, so the construction stops.

Answer

\( \text{Merkle Root} = \text{Hash}\big(\text{Hash}(T1\|T2)\;\|\;\text{Hash}(T3\|T4)\big) \)

Storage: \(T1\ldots T4\) live in the block body; \(H1\) and \(H2\) are intermediate values that need not be stored at all; only the Merkle root is written into the block header. Sanity check: four leaves halve to two, then to one — \(4 \to 2 \to 1\), so the tree has \(\log_2 4 = 2\) levels above the leaves, which matches the two hashing rounds performed.

Question 4 · medium

A block contains five transactions \(T1 \ldots T5\). Construct the Merkle root, showing every level. State the rule you used to handle the odd transaction and how many hashing operations were required in total.

Show worked solution

  1. \( T1,\; T2,\; T3,\; T4,\; T5 \)

    Five leaves. Five is odd, so at least one level will not pair cleanly — flag that before you start rather than discovering it halfway.

  2. \( H1 = \text{Hash}(T1\|T2), \quad H2 = \text{Hash}(T3\|T4) \)

    Pair left to right as far as the pairs last. Two hashing operations so far.

  3. \( H3 = \text{Hash}(T5 \| T5) \)

    Duplication rule for an odd leaf: \(T5\) has no partner, so it is paired with a copy of itself. This keeps the tree binary without inventing a fake transaction. Three operations.

  4. \( H4 = \text{Hash}(H1\|H2) \)

    Move up one level and apply the same pairing to \(H1, H2, H3\). The first two pair normally. Four operations.

  5. \( H5 = \text{Hash}(H3 \| H3) \)

    Duplication rule again — this level also has an odd count (three values), so the leftover \(H3\) doubles with itself. Five operations.

  6. \( \text{Merkle Root} = \text{Hash}(H4 \| H5) \)

    Two values remain, so one final pairing finishes the tree. Six operations.

Answer

\( \text{Merkle Root} = \text{Hash}(H4 \| H5) \), where \(H4=\text{Hash}(H1\|H2)\) and \(H5=\text{Hash}(H3\|H3)\). Rule used: an unpaired node at any level is duplicated and hashed with itself. Total: 6 hashing operations.

Sanity check: level sizes go \(5 \to 3 \to 2 \to 1\). Count the hashes produced at each level: \(3 + 2 + 1 = 6\). Matches.

Question 5 · hard · analytical

An attacker gains write access to one node and modifies transaction \(T2\) inside block number 500 of a chain that is 900 blocks long. Trace, step by step, exactly what the network detects and why. Then explain what the attacker would have to do to make the tampering succeed, and why it is infeasible.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from This tests whether you can chain together three separate lecture pieces: the hash function's avalanche property and Merkle tree (Data layer, §4), the block header fields — specifically previous block hash (§4), and the consensus layer (§4/§5). The examiner wants a causal chain with no gaps, and then the economic/computational argument. This is the standard way "explain immutability" is asked at distinction level.

What breaks, in order:

  1. \(T2\) is altered. Because the hash function has the avalanche property, \(\text{Hash}(T1\|T2)\) — that is, \(H1\) — becomes a completely different digest, not a slightly different one.
  2. \(H1\) is an input to \(\text{Hash}(H1\|H2)\), so the Merkle root of block 500 changes.
  3. The Merkle root is a block header field, so block 500's header content changes, and therefore the hash of block 500 changes.
  4. Block 501 stores block 500's hash in its previous block hash field. That stored value now disagrees with the recomputed value. The chain is broken at exactly block 500 — and note that the break is localised, so the network learns not just that tampering occurred but precisely where.
  5. Any honest node validating the chain recomputes the Merkle root from the body it holds, sees the mismatch, and rejects the tampered copy. Because the ledger is distributed, the other nodes still hold the original block 500, so the majority version wins and the attacker's node is simply out of sync.

What the attacker would need to do. To make the fraud stick they would have to re-mine block 500 — find a new nonce satisfying the proof-of-work difficulty target for the altered header — and then re-mine blocks 501 through 900 as well, because each one's previous-block-hash field depends on the block before it. That is 400 blocks of work.

Why it is infeasible. Three reasons, and a good answer gives all three. Computationally: the honest network is simultaneously extending the chain, so the attacker must not only redo 400 blocks but out-pace the entire network's combined hash power while doing it — which in practice requires controlling more than 50% of that power (the "51% attack"). Structurally: even with the work done, consensus means the other nodes must accept the rewritten chain, and honest nodes follow the longest valid chain they have independently verified. Economically: acquiring that much hash power costs far more than the value of one altered transaction.

Marking tip

The five-step cascade is the core and is worth most of the marks. Name the mechanism at each hop — avalanche → Merkle root → header → block hash → previous block hash mismatch — rather than saying "the hash changes so it's detected." Then add consensus and the 51% argument for the top band.

Topic 05

Operation — the end-to-end transaction flow

Syllabus 1.5. Six steps, in the faculty's order. Learn them as a sequence you can recite.

This is the lecture's Alice-pays-Bob-$5 walkthrough. The six steps are worth memorising verbatim because "describe the end-to-end operation of a blockchain transaction" is about as predictable as exam questions get.

  1. Transaction request. Alice creates the transaction in her wallet and signs it with her private key, then broadcasts it. The signature is what proves it came from her.
  2. Propagation. The peer-to-peer gossip network spreads the transaction from node to node until it has reached the network. "Gossip" simply means each node forwards to its neighbours, who forward on again.
  3. Block creation and validation. Miners or validators gather pending transactions into a candidate block — a proposed block that is not yet accepted.
  4. Consensus execution. Nodes run the consensus algorithm. Under proof of work this means solving a hash puzzle by trying different nonce values (a nonce is an arbitrary number varied until the block's hash meets the network's difficulty target).
  5. Block appending. The validated block is permanently chained on by storing the previous block hash, so the new block is cryptographically welded to the one before it.
  6. State settlement. The ledger updates across all nodes and Bob can access the funds using his private key.
Watch out

Consensus (step 4) happens before the block joins the chain (step 5), not after. A very common wrong answer appends the block and then has the network vote on it. Also: the signature happens at step 1, by the sender — not by the miners.

Question 6 · medium

Alice sends $5 to Bob on a public blockchain. Describe the complete journey of that transaction from Alice's wallet to Bob's balance, naming the cryptographic mechanism used at each stage where one applies.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from This is the end-to-end transaction flow from Lecture 1 §5, and the phrase "naming the cryptographic mechanism" tells you the examiner also wants the digital signature and hashing material from §4 folded in. So the mark scheme is really two lists interleaved: the six process steps, and the cryptography that secures each one.

1. Creation and signing. Alice composes the transaction in her wallet. She hashes it to a message digest and signs that digest with her private key. Mechanism: asymmetric digital signature — it proves authenticity (only Alice's private key could produce it) and integrity (the signature fails if the transaction is altered).

2. Broadcast and propagation. The signed transaction goes out to the peer-to-peer network and spreads by gossip, node to node, until it reaches the wider network. Receiving nodes verify the signature using Alice's public key before forwarding it — invalid transactions die at the first hop.

3. Block assembly. A miner/validator collects pending transactions into a candidate block, computes the Merkle root of those transactions, and writes it into the block header along with the timestamp, block number and previous block hash. Mechanism: cryptographic hashing in a binary hash tree.

4. Consensus. The network runs its consensus protocol. Under proof of work, the miner varies the nonce and re-hashes the header until the resulting hash falls below the difficulty target — hard to find, trivial for everyone else to check. Mechanism: one-way hash function, exploited as a puzzle.

5. Appending. The winning block is added to the chain, storing the previous block hash so it is welded to its predecessor. Other nodes independently verify the work and the Merkle root before accepting it. Mechanism: hash chaining, which is what delivers immutability.

6. Settlement. Every node updates its copy of the ledger. Bob's balance now reflects the $5, and he spends it by signing a new transaction with his own private key.

Marking tip

Close by contrasting with Scenario 1: at no point did a bank approve anything, so the transfer cannot be censored, delayed or lost to a single server outage. Tying the flow back to the motivating scenario is a cheap extra mark.

Topic 06

Centralisation vs. decentralisation

Syllabus 1.6. The faculty's six-row comparison table — learn every row.

In a centralised architecture, every client talks to one central server. In a decentralised architecture, nodes talk to each other directly in a mesh — there is no middle. The lecture's comparison table is the highest-value thing in this section; reproduce it row for row and the question is answered.

ControlSingle central authorityDistributed network of peer nodes
Data storageOne central database serverReplicated across multiple node copies
Message modificationPossible silently by database adminsPractically impossible / tamper-evident
Failure riskSingle point of failure (SPOF)Highly fault-tolerant
Trust modelTrust placed in a central entity/serverTrust placed in cryptographic protocol

Key idea

The trust model row is the one to lead with. Everything else follows from it: centralised systems relocate risk onto an institution, decentralised systems relocate it onto mathematics. Institutions can be bribed, hacked or shut down; a hash function cannot.

Blockchain categories — a related exam favourite

Decentralisation is not all-or-nothing. The lecture gives three categories, and picking the right one for a scenario is a standard question.

  • Public (Ethereum, Polygon, Solana, BNB Chain) — permissionless: anyone may join, read and validate. For open dApps, DeFi, public cryptocurrency.
  • Private (Hyperledger Fabric, Quorum) — one organisation controls membership. For internal enterprise automation and proprietary data.
  • Consortium (Corda, Hyperledger) — a fixed group of organisations jointly governs it. For multi-bank settlement or inter-organisational federations, where the members do not fully trust each other but must share a record.
Question 7 · medium · story-based

Six banks in Bangladesh want a shared settlement ledger. No bank will accept another bank running it, none wants the transaction data visible to the public, and all six must be able to verify every entry. Which blockchain category should they deploy, and why are the other two unsuitable? Justify using the centralisation/decentralisation trade-offs.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from This connects blockchain categories (Lecture 1 §7) to the trust model row of the centralisation table (§2). The examiner is testing whether you treat decentralisation as a spectrum rather than a switch, and whether you can justify by elimination — which is what "why are the other two unsuitable" is explicitly asking for.

Answer: a consortium blockchain (Corda or Hyperledger), with one validating node per participating bank.

Why it fits. The requirement has two halves that pull in opposite directions: mutual distrust between the six banks, and confidentiality from everyone else. A consortium chain is built for exactly that. Governance is shared across the member organisations, so no single bank is the authority — which satisfies the objection to one bank running it — while membership stays permissioned, so the ledger is not publicly readable. Each bank runs its own node, so each holds a full verifiable copy and can independently confirm every entry, which is the distribution pillar doing the work.

Why not private. A private blockchain places membership and write authority under a single organisation. That reproduces precisely the problem the banks objected to — it is decentralised in data storage but centralised in control, so the trust model reverts to "trust that one entity", the very row of the comparison table this scenario rejects.

Why not public. A public chain is permissionless, so anyone can read the ledger. That violates the confidentiality requirement outright. It also brings costs the scenario does not need — public consensus such as proof of work is slow and expensive relative to the throughput inter-bank settlement demands, and the anonymity of validators is unacceptable in a regulated financial setting where participants must be identifiable.

Closing link. The general principle: choose the category by asking who must be able to write and who must be able to read. Public = anyone/anyone, private = one org/one org, consortium = a known group/a known group. This case is a known group on both counts.

Topic 07

Consensus protocol

Syllabus 1.7. The \(CP\) in \(DL + CP + C\), and the last core section.

Here is the problem consensus solves. Thousands of nodes each hold a copy of the ledger, and there is no boss. Two nodes might receive different transactions at the same moment, or a node might lie deliberately. Something has to decide which version of the ledger is the real one, using only rules the nodes can each check for themselves. A consensus protocol is that set of rules — the algorithmic procedure by which a decentralised network agrees on a single true state.

Your lecture lists seven at the consensus layer: PoW, PoS, DPoS, PBFT, PoET, PoAh, Raft. Two of them carry almost all the exam weight.

Proof of Work (PoW)

Nodes called miners compete to solve a hash puzzle. Each repeatedly changes the nonce in the block header and re-hashes until the output falls below a difficulty target. Finding a valid nonce takes enormous trial-and-error; checking one takes a single hash. That asymmetry is the whole trick: work is expensive to produce and cheap to verify, so honest nodes can instantly confirm that a miner really did the work. The first to succeed publishes the block and claims the reward. Used by Bitcoin. Cost: massive electricity consumption.

Proof of Stake (PoS)

Instead of burning electricity, participants called validators lock up ("stake") their own cryptocurrency as collateral. The protocol selects a validator to propose the next block, weighted by stake size. Cheat, and the stake is destroyed — so honesty is enforced economically rather than thermodynamically. Used by Ethereum since its move away from PoW. Far more energy-efficient; the standard criticism is that influence tracks wealth.

The rest, in one line each

  • DPoS (Delegated Proof of Stake) — token holders vote for a small set of delegates who validate on their behalf. Faster, more centralised.
  • PBFT (Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance) — nodes vote in rounds and reach agreement provided fewer than one-third are faulty or malicious. Suits permissioned chains with a known membership.
  • PoET (Proof of Elapsed Time) — each node waits a randomly assigned time in a trusted execution environment; shortest wait wins. A lottery instead of a race.
  • PoAh (Proof of Authentication) — lightweight authentication-based consensus aimed at resource-constrained IoT devices.
  • Raft — a leader-election protocol for permissioned networks; tolerates crashes but not malicious nodes.
Watch out

"Byzantine fault tolerant" means the protocol survives nodes that are actively lying, not merely nodes that have crashed. PBFT is Byzantine fault tolerant; Raft is only crash-fault tolerant. That distinction is a classic short-answer question.

Question 8 · medium

Define a consensus protocol and explain its role in the equation \(\text{Blockchain} = DL + CP + C\). Compare Proof of Work and Proof of Stake on the mechanism of selection, the source of security, and the cost.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from Two lecture points welded together: the defining equation (Lecture 1 §3) and the consensus layer (§4). Quoting the equation and then explaining what collapses without \(CP\) is the structure the examiner is looking for — a definition alone will not reach full marks because the question says "explain its role in".

Definition. A consensus protocol is the set of algorithmic rules by which a decentralised network of independent nodes agrees on a single, shared true state of the ledger, without any central authority and without the nodes having to trust one another.

Role in \(DL + CP + C\). The distributed ledger \(DL\) gives every node a copy, and cryptography \(C\) makes each copy tamper-evident. Neither of those settles disagreement. If two nodes receive conflicting transactions, or a node broadcasts a false one, \(DL\) and \(C\) alone leave the network with several internally-consistent but mutually contradictory histories. \(CP\) is what forces convergence on one. Remove it and the copies simply drift apart — you have replication without agreement, which is not a blockchain.

Comparison.

  • Selection mechanism. PoW: miners compete — the first to find a nonce producing a hash below the difficulty target wins. PoS: the protocol selects a validator, with probability weighted by the size of the stake they have locked up.
  • Source of security. PoW: computational expenditure — rewriting history means redoing the work faster than the whole honest network, which requires over 50% of its hash power. PoS: economic collateral — a dishonest validator has their stake destroyed, so attacking costs the attacker directly.
  • Cost. PoW: very high, in electricity and specialised hardware, and the cost is ongoing. PoS: low energy; the cost is capital locked up rather than energy consumed. The recognised criticism of PoS is that influence scales with wealth, which pushes back toward concentration.

Closing line. Both replace trust in an institution with a cost that makes dishonesty irrational — PoW makes cheating physically expensive, PoS makes it financially expensive. That sentence ties the comparison back to the trust-model row of the centralisation table.

Question 9 · hard · stretch

A university wants to stop fake degree certificates. Design a blockchain-based verification system: state which category of blockchain you would use, which consensus protocol, what data goes on-chain versus off-chain, and how an employer verifies a certificate. Then identify one limitation of your design.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from This is Case Study 02, Educational Certificate Verification (Lecture 1 §6) turned into a design question. It pulls together four separate syllabus points at once — categories (§7), consensus (§4), hashing (§4) and applications (§6) — which is why it is a stretch question. The examiner wants design decisions with justifications, not a description of the case study.

Category: consortium (Hyperledger Fabric), as the lecture's case study specifies. Universities and the accrediting authority are the members. It is not private, because a single university issuing and validating its own certificates recreates the trust problem the system is meant to solve. It is not public, because student records must not be world-readable and the network membership needs to be identity-verified.

Consensus: a permissioned protocol — PBFT or Raft. Justification: the membership is known and fixed, so there is no need for PoW's expensive open-entry defence, and issuing certificates is a low-volume, high-value operation where finality matters more than throughput. PBFT is preferable to Raft here because member institutions may have incentives to misbehave, and PBFT is Byzantine fault tolerant while Raft only tolerates crashes.

On-chain vs off-chain. On-chain: the SHA-256 hash of each certificate, the issuing institution's identifier, the issue date, and a revocation flag. Off-chain: the certificate document itself and all personal data, held in the university's secure database. This split is the point of the case study — putting the hash on-chain gives tamper-evidence without publishing anyone's transcript, and it keeps block size small.

Verification flow. The graduate gives the employer the certificate file. The employer hashes it and looks the digest up on the chain. If a matching record exists, was issued by a legitimate member institution, and is not flagged revoked, the certificate is genuine. If the document has been altered by even one character, the avalanche property means the recomputed hash will not match anything on the chain, and the forgery is caught. Crucially the employer never has to contact the university, which removes the delay and the phone-call fraud vector of the current process.

Limitation. The system guarantees that a certificate matches what the university issued; it cannot guarantee the university should have issued it. If a corrupt registrar issues a genuine certificate to someone who never studied there, the chain will faithfully confirm that forgery forever. This is the same boundary as the medical-records case: blockchain secures the record, not the real-world fact behind it — the "garbage in, garbage forever" problem. A complete answer names this explicitly.

Last-minute revision — Blockchain

The definition

\( \text{Blockchain} = DL + CP + C \) — Distributed Ledger + Consensus Protocol + Cryptography.

5 pillars

Distribution, Decentralisation, Immutability, Tokenisation, Encryption. (D-D-I-T-E)

Merkle root — 4 tx

\( H1=\text{Hash}(T1\|T2) \), \( H2=\text{Hash}(T3\|T4) \), \( \text{Root}=\text{Hash}(H1\|H2) \).

Odd leaf rule

Duplicate it and hash with itself: \( \text{Hash}(T5\|T5) \). Repeat at every level with an odd count.

Header vs body

Header: block number, timestamp, nonce, previous block hash, Merkle root. Body: the transactions.

Tamper cascade

Alter tx → leaf hash changes → Merkle root changes → header changes → block hash changes → next block's previous-hash mismatches. Rejected.

Signatures

Sign with the private key, verify with the public key. Proves authenticity + integrity.

5 layers

Application → Data → Consensus → Network → Execution.

Transaction flow

Request & sign → propagate (gossip) → candidate block → consensus → append via previous hash → settlement.

PoW vs PoS

PoW: compete on nonce-hashing, secured by electricity. PoS: selected by stake weight, secured by collateral at risk.

Categories

Public = anyone (Ethereum). Private = one org (Fabric, Quorum). Consortium = a known group (Corda).

Trust model line

Centralised trusts an entity; decentralised trusts a protocol. Lead with this in any comparison.

Common trap

Merkle root secures transactions inside a block; previous block hash links blocks to each other. Never swap them.

PBFT vs Raft

PBFT survives lying nodes (Byzantine). Raft survives only crashed nodes.
