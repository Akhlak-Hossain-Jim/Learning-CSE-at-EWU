Topic 01

What is a SOC?

Syllabus 2.1. Definition plus the six core functions.

Picture a large hospital's emergency department, but for an organisation's computer network. Alarms arrive continuously from every corner of the estate. Someone has to look at each one, decide whether it is real, decide how serious it is, treat the serious cases, and keep records afterwards. That is a Security Operations Center.

The faculty's definition — learn it verbatim

A Security Operations Center (SOC) is a centralised organisational unit and facility responsible for continuously monitoring, detecting, analysing and responding to cybersecurity threats across an enterprise infrastructure.

Two words in that definition carry weight. Centralised: the point of a SOC is that security visibility is pooled in one place instead of scattered across separate teams who each see a fragment. Continuously: it runs 24/7, because attackers do not observe office hours.

The six core functions

  1. Prevention and proactive monitoring — round-the-clock scanning of network perimeters, endpoints and cloud assets.
  2. Alert management and triage — aggregating, correlating and filtering security alerts to eliminate false positives. (Triage is the medical term for sorting patients by urgency; it means the same here.)
  3. Incident response — systematic containment, eradication and recovery from active attacks.
  4. Log management — centralised log ingestion, indexing and compliance auditing.
  5. Security intelligence and threat hunting — integrating external threat feeds to find zero-day or stealth campaigns. (A zero-day is a vulnerability with no vendor patch available yet.)
  6. Security posture refinement — continuously hardening systems and tuning detection rules.

Notice that functions 1 and 6 are proactive while 2 and 3 are reactive. If a question asks whether a SOC is preventive or responsive, the answer is both, and naming functions on each side proves it.

Question 1 · short answer

Define a Security Operations Center and describe its core functions. Explain why an organisation would centralise these functions rather than leave security to individual IT teams.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from The definition and six core functions come straight from Lecture "SOC & Threat Intelligence" §1. The second half — "why centralise" — is not stated as a bullet in the slides, so the examiner is testing whether you can derive it from the correlation idea in the SIEM material (§2, Technology) and the alert fatigue challenge (§7). This is the classic "recall plus one step of reasoning" question.

Definition. A SOC is a centralised organisational unit and facility responsible for continuously monitoring, detecting, analysing and responding to cybersecurity threats across an enterprise infrastructure.

Core functions. Prevention and proactive 24/7 monitoring of perimeters, endpoints and cloud; alert management and triage to filter false positives; incident response covering containment, eradication and recovery; centralised log management for indexing and compliance auditing; security intelligence and threat hunting using external feeds to catch zero-day and stealth campaigns; and continuous security posture refinement through hardening and detection-rule tuning.

Why centralise. Three arguments, and the strongest is the first.

  • Correlation is only possible with pooled data. A multi-stage attack shows up as one failed login on the VPN, one odd DNS lookup on the proxy, and one new scheduled task on an endpoint. Each team looking at its own tool sees three unremarkable events. A SOC feeding all three into a SIEM sees one attack chain. Detection that depends on joining evidence across domains is structurally impossible in a decentralised model.
  • Scarce expertise is concentrated where it pays. The lecture lists a skills shortage in qualified Tier 2/3 analysts and reverse engineers as a major challenge. No individual IT team can staff a malware reverse engineer; one SOC can, and serves the whole estate.
  • Consistency and cost. Documented SOPs and playbooks mean every incident is handled the same way regardless of which system it hit, and the very high infrastructure cost of SIEM licensing and multi-terabyte log retention is paid once instead of per team.
Topic 02

The three pillars of a SOC

Syllabus 2.2. People, Process, Technology. A guaranteed question.

Everything a SOC is made of falls into one of three buckets. The lecture draws it as a single diagram, and the reason it matters is that a SOC fails if any one pillar is missing — great tools with untrained staff, or great staff with no documented procedure, both produce chaos.

The three pillars

Pillar 1 — People

Covered in full in Section 3, because your syllabus lists roles and responsibilities separately.

Pillar 2 — Process

Two artefacts, and the difference between them is examinable.

SOPs (Standard Operating Procedures) are the general documented workflows: how alerts are handled, the escalation matrix that says who passes what to whom, and how the SOC communicates with other teams. Incident playbooks are specific: an actionable, step-by-step sequence tailored to one attack type — a phishing playbook, a ransomware playbook, a brute-force playbook, a SQL injection playbook.

One-line separator: SOPs govern how the SOC operates in general; playbooks govern what to do about one particular kind of attack.

Pillar 3 — Technology

Four systems, and you should be able to say what each is for in one sentence.

  • SIEM — Security Information and Event Management. Central log aggregation, correlation rules and event visualisation. Examples: Splunk, IBM QRadar, Wazuh. This is the SOC's central nervous system: it is where logs from everywhere land and where correlation rules turn many events into one alert.
  • SOAR — Security Orchestration, Automation and Response. Automated playbooks for rapid containment and workflow orchestration. Where SIEM detects, SOAR acts — automatically blocking an IP or isolating a host without waiting for a human.
  • EDR / XDR — Endpoint (or Extended) Detection and Response. Deep visibility into hosts, behavioural anomaly detection and remediation.
  • TIP — Threat Intelligence Platform. Feeds real-time indicators of compromise (IoCs) and threat context into the monitoring engines.

EDR vs XDR — the comparison table

Dimension EDR XDR
Monitoring scopeEndpoints only (workstations, servers)Multi-layered: endpoints, network, cloud, identity, email

Watch out

Do not confuse SIEM with SOAR. SIEM collects and correlates to produce an alert; SOAR takes an alert and executes an automated response. Section 9, Exercise 8 quantifies exactly what adding SOAR does to the metrics — that is the connection between the theory and the maths half of this topic.

Question 2 · long answer

"A SOC is only as strong as its weakest pillar." Explain the three pillars of a SOC and, for each, describe a realistic failure that would cripple the SOC even if the other two pillars were world-class.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from The three pillars come from §2 of the SOC lecture. The "failure" half asks you to combine them with the operational challenges in §7 — alert fatigue, skills shortage, infrastructure cost. The examiner is testing whether the pillars are a memorised list or something you understand as interdependent, so structure the answer as three paired blocks: what the pillar is, then what its absence does.

Pillar 1 — People. The tiered analyst structure: Tier 1 triage specialists monitoring SIEM/EDR consoles and filtering false positives; Tier 2 incident responders doing deep-dive investigation and containment; Tier 3 threat hunters performing proactive hunting, malware reverse engineering and detection-rule refinement; and above them the SOC Manager and Incident Response Coordinator, reporting to the CISO.

Failure mode: the skills shortage the lecture names. Suppose the SOC has a superb SIEM and mature playbooks but cannot hire Tier 3 staff. Nothing proactive happens — no threat hunting for APTs, no reverse engineering of novel malware, and critically no one refining the correlation rules. The detection logic slowly decays against evolving attacker TTPs, and the SOC becomes purely reactive to threats its out-of-date rules already know about.

Pillar 2 — Process. SOPs define alert handling workflows, escalation matrices and cross-team communication; incident playbooks give attack-specific step-by-step responses for phishing, ransomware, brute-force and SQL injection.

Failure mode: excellent analysts with excellent tools and no documented escalation matrix. Every incident is improvised. Two analysts handle identical ransomware alerts in two different ways, containment is delayed while people work out who has authority to isolate a production server, and the delay shows up directly in the metrics as an inflated MTTR. Worse, without SOPs there is no consistent post-incident record, so the same mistake repeats and the lecture's Phase 7 — reporting and continuous improvement — never happens.

Pillar 3 — Technology. SIEM for aggregation and correlation, SOAR for automated response, EDR/XDR for endpoint and cross-domain visibility, TIP for IoC feeds.

Failure mode: skilled analysts following good procedure but with poor log coverage or untuned correlation rules. This produces alert fatigue, the first challenge in §7: the volume of low-fidelity alerts is so high that critical threats are overlooked inside the noise. The maths half of this topic makes it concrete — a false positive rate of 16% against 42,000 monthly alerts means analysts wade through roughly 6,700 wrong alerts, and genuine detections get buried.

Conclusion. The three failures are not independent — they compound. Weak technology creates alert volume, which overloads people, which causes procedure to be skipped under pressure. That mutual dependence is what the quoted statement means, and saying so explicitly is the closing mark.

Topic 03

Roles and responsibilities

Syllabus 2.3. The tier hierarchy — who does what, and what escalates upward.

The People pillar is organised as a ladder. Work rises up it: routine filtering at the bottom, investigation in the middle, and the hardest, least-defined work at the top. Learn each tier as a job description plus a handover point.

Tier 1 — Triage Specialist

  • Ingests raw logs and continuously monitors SIEM/EDR consoles.
  • Conducts preliminary alert triage and false-positive filtering to reduce alert fatigue.
  • Escalates confirmed security events to Tier 2.

Tier 1 is a filter, not an investigator. The question they answer is "is this real, and how urgent is it?" — not "what happened?"

Tier 2 — Incident Responder

  • Conducts deep-dive investigations and scope analysis across affected systems — that is, working out how far the compromise has spread.
  • Executes containment: endpoint isolation, network ACL blocks, and system remediation.

Tier 2 owns the incident once it is confirmed. They answer "what happened, how far did it go, and how do we stop it spreading?"

Tier 3 — Threat Hunter / Expert

  • Proactively hunts stealthy, undetected threats and APTs (Advanced Persistent Threats — well-resourced attackers who maintain long-term covert access).
  • Performs malware reverse engineering, vulnerability assessments and penetration tests.
  • Refines SIEM detection rules and analytics models.

Tier 3 is the only tier whose work starts without an alert. Tiers 1 and 2 are triggered by something the system flagged; Tier 3 goes looking for what the system missed. That is the single most important distinction in this section.

SOC Manager and Incident Response Coordinator

  • Oversee operational performance, metrics, staffing and reporting.
  • Report directly to the CISO (Chief Information Security Officer).

Note that the metrics in Sections 7–9 are the SOC Manager's instrument panel. That is the join between this topic and the maths.

Key idea

Ladder of questions: Tier 1 — "is it real?" · Tier 2 — "what happened and how do we contain it?" · Tier 3 — "what have we not detected at all?" · Manager — "how well is the SOC performing?" Answer any roles question in that shape and it practically writes itself.

Question 3 · long answer · story-based

At 02:14 the SIEM raises an alert: a finance department workstation has made an outbound connection to a domain flagged on a threat intelligence feed. Twenty minutes later a second alert shows the same workstation authenticating to three file servers it has never touched before.

Describe how this incident travels through the SOC's tier structure. State what each tier does, at what point the incident is handed on, and which pillar of the SOC each activity draws on.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from A story wrapped around two lecture structures at once: the tier roles and responsibilities (§2, People) and the 7-phase incident response lifecycle (§4). The examiner wants you to notice that the tiers and the phases line up — Tier 1 owns triage, Tier 2 owns investigation through recovery, Tier 3 and the manager own the improvement phase. The two alerts in the story are deliberate: the second is lateral movement, which is what forces escalation.

Phase 1 — Alert (Technology pillar). The SIEM generates the alert because a correlation rule matched a threat intelligence indicator — the outbound connection to a known malicious domain. In the lecture's correlation-rule matrix this is the botnet activity use case: telemetry from firewall, IDS, DNS and proxy, with the alert condition "outbound connection to known C2 domain".

Phase 2 — Triage (Tier 1, People + Process). The Tier 1 triage specialist picks the alert off the console and validates its authenticity, severity and urgency. They check whether the destination is a genuine malicious indicator or a stale feed entry, and whether the workstation's behaviour has a benign explanation. Here it does not — and the second alert, showing authentication to three servers the host has never contacted, is lateral movement, which no false positive explains. Handover point: Tier 1 confirms this is a real security event and escalates to Tier 2. Tier 1 does not investigate further; that is the boundary of the role.

Phase 3 — Investigation (Tier 2). The Tier 2 incident responder performs the deep-dive: forensic artefact analysis and timeline reconstruction to establish the initial access vector, and scope analysis to determine which other systems are affected. The three file servers must now be examined, because lateral movement means the blast radius is larger than the one workstation.

Phase 4 — Containment (Tier 2). Endpoint isolation of the workstation, network ACL blocks against the C2 domain, and revocation of the credentials used to reach the file servers. This halts lateral spread and is executed under the relevant incident playbook — the Process pillar — so the analyst is not improvising at 02:40.

Phase 5 — Eradication (Tier 2, supported by Tier 3). Purging malware artefacts, patching the exploited vulnerability, and closing any backdoors. If the malware is novel, Tier 3 performs reverse engineering to establish what it does and what indicators it leaves — the point where the expert tier is pulled in.

Phase 6 — Recovery. Restoring affected systems from trusted backups and validating operational integrity before returning them to service.

Phase 7 — Reporting and continuous improvement (Tier 3 + SOC Manager). A post-incident review. Tier 3 refines the SIEM detection rules so that the lateral-movement pattern raises an alert on its own next time rather than being noticed twenty minutes later, and updates the playbook. The SOC Manager records the metrics this incident contributes to — its detection time feeds MTTD, its response time feeds MTTR — and reports upward to the CISO.

Marking tip

Name the two handover points explicitly: Tier 1 → Tier 2 on confirmation of a genuine event, and the pull-in of Tier 3 for reverse engineering and rule refinement. Stating where responsibility transfers, and why, is what the question means by "at what point the incident is handed on."

Topic 04

The four pillars of Threat Intelligence

Syllabus 2.4. Strategic, Tactical, Operational, Technical — and who consumes each.

First the definition. Threat Intelligence (TI) is the disciplined process of gathering, analysing and applying actionable data about adversary motives, capabilities and indicators of compromise, in order to strengthen defensive decisions.

The operative word is actionable. A list of malicious IP addresses is data; knowing which of them a group targeting Bangladeshi banks is currently using is intelligence. The four types differ by who reads it and how long it stays useful.

Type Content Consumer
StrategicHigh-level trends, business impact, long-term risk pictureCISO, board members, executives
TacticalDirect threat indicators — IoCs, signatures — for automated blockingTier 1 / Tier 2 analysts, automated controls
OperationalContext on adversary campaigns, motivations and attack vectorsIncident responders (Tier 2)
TechnicalSpecific artefacts, malware compiler tools, exploit detailsSecurity researchers, Tier 3

Your lecture also plots these on two axes — complexity against long-term focus — with Strategic and Tactical on the high-complexity row and Technical and Operational on the low-complexity row. If a diagram is asked for, reproduce that quadrant layout; the four names and their consumers are what earn the marks.

The Pyramid of Pain (David Bianco)

This ranks indicators by how much it hurts the attacker when you block them. Bottom = trivial for the attacker to change, so blocking achieves little. Top = expensive to change, so blocking really damages their operation.

  1. Hash values (SHA-256, MD5) — trivial. Recompiling the malware changes the hash in seconds.
  2. IP addresses — easy. Rent another server.
  3. Domain names — simple. Register a new one.
  4. Network / host artefacts — annoying. Requires modifying the tooling's behaviour.
  5. Tools (Mimikatz, Cobalt Strike) — challenging. The attacker must find or build a replacement and retrain.
  6. TTPs (Tactics, Techniques and Procedures) — tough. Detecting behaviour rather than artefacts forces the adversary to change how they operate, which is slow and expensive.

The MITRE ATT&CK framework formalises the top level. Tactics are the attacker's goal (Initial Access, Privilege Escalation). Techniques are the method used to achieve it (Phishing, Pass-the-Hash). Procedures are the exact step-by-step implementation observed in a real attack.

Data formats — STIX and TAXII

These two always appear together and are always confused. STIX (Structured Threat Information Expression) is the language — a standardised structured format for representing threat data. TAXII (Trusted Automated Exchange of Intelligence Information) is the transport — the application protocol that moves STIX data over HTTPS.

Memory hook

STIX = the language you write it in. TAXII = the taxi that carries it. That is genuinely how to keep them straight, and it is a one-mark question almost every year.

Question 4 · long answer

Define threat intelligence and explain its four types, identifying the consumer of each. Then use the Pyramid of Pain to argue why a SOC that blocks only file hashes and IP addresses is doing low-value work.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from Two sections joined: the 4 types of TI (§5) and the Pyramid of Pain (§6). The link the examiner wants is that the pyramid explains why the different TI types have different value — hash and IP blocking is tactical intelligence at the bottom of the pyramid, while TTP-based detection is operational/technical intelligence at the top. Answer the definition and the four types crisply, then spend most of your time on the argument, because that is where the discrimination is.

Definition. Threat intelligence is the disciplined process of gathering, analysing and applying actionable data regarding adversary motives, capabilities and indicators of compromise, in order to strengthen defensive decisions.

The four types.

  • Strategic — high-level trends and business impact, written for CISOs, board members and executives. Long time horizon; informs budget and risk appetite, not day-to-day blocking.
  • Tactical — direct threat indicators such as IoCs and signatures, consumed by Tier 1/2 analysts and by automated blocking controls. Short-lived and immediately actionable.
  • Operational — context around adversary campaigns, motivations and attack vectors, for incident responders who need to understand what an attacker is trying to achieve.
  • Technical — specific technical artefacts, malware compiler tooling and exploit details, used by researchers and Tier 3.

The Pyramid of Pain argument. Bianco's pyramid ranks indicators by the cost imposed on the adversary when a defender blocks them. Hash values sit at the very bottom, labelled trivial: recompiling the same malware produces a completely different hash, so a blocklist of hashes is defeated in the time it takes to rebuild. IP addresses are next, labelled easy — the attacker rents a different server. Domain names are simple to replace by registration.

So a SOC that blocks only hashes and IPs is operating entirely on the bottom two rungs. Every block it lands costs the attacker minutes. The defensive work is real but it is also infinitely repeatable by the adversary at near-zero cost, which means the SOC is permanently reactive: it can only ever block what it has already seen, and it will see the same actor again under a new hash the following week. In metric terms this is why such a SOC's detection rate stays stuck — the missed attacks are the same adversary wearing a new indicator.

Moving up the pyramid changes the economics. Detecting on tools such as Mimikatz or Cobalt Strike forces the attacker to source and learn a replacement — challenging. Detecting on TTPs, formalised through MITRE ATT&CK as tactics, techniques and procedures, means the defender is matching on behaviour: the act of pass-the-hash, or the pattern of privilege escalation, regardless of which binary or IP performs it. To evade that the adversary must change how they operate, which is slow, expensive and often requires retraining their operators.

Conclusion. The four TI types map onto the pyramid: tactical intelligence supplies the bottom rungs and is cheap to consume but cheap to evade; operational and technical intelligence supply the top rungs and are what let a SOC impose real cost. A mature SOC uses tactical feeds for automated blocking and builds TTP-based detections — the first buys immediate coverage, the second buys durable advantage.

Topic 05

How a SOC prevents attacks

Syllabus 2.5. The 7-phase incident response lifecycle plus the correlation rule matrix.

"How does a SOC prevent attacks" is really two questions: what it does before an attack lands, and what it does once one has. Cover both or you lose half the marks.

Before — the preventive layer

  • Continuous monitoring of perimeters, endpoints and cloud, so an intrusion is visible early rather than discovered months later.
  • Threat intelligence integration: TIP feeds IoCs into the SIEM, so known-bad indicators are blocked before they can act.
  • Proactive threat hunting by Tier 3, searching for what detection rules did not catch.
  • Security posture refinement — hardening, patching, and tuning detection rules so both the attack surface and the noise shrink.
  • Automated blocking via SOAR, which acts faster than any human can.

During and after — the 7-phase incident response lifecycle

  1. Alert — the SIEM fires, triggered by a correlation rule or a threat intelligence match.
  2. Triage — Tier 1 validates authenticity, severity and urgency.
  3. Investigation — Tier 2 performs forensic artefact analysis and timeline reconstruction.
  4. Containment — network segmentation, credential revocation and host isolation to halt lateral spread.
  5. Eradication — purging malware artefacts, patching the exploited vulnerability, closing unauthorised backdoors.
  6. Recovery — restoring from trusted backups and validating operational integrity.
  7. Reporting and continuous improvement — post-incident review, playbook updates, detection-logic tuning.
Watch out — the order is examinable

Containment comes before eradication, and eradication before recovery. The logic: stop the bleeding, then remove the cause, then restore service. Restoring a system from backup while the attacker still holds access simply re-infects it. Phase 7 is the one candidates forget, and it is the phase that makes the SOC improve rather than merely survive.

The standard correlation rule matrix

Four use cases the lecture tabulates. These are extremely quotable — they let you answer "give an example" instantly.

Failures then a success is a password that has just been guessed, and that is why the action is to lock the account rather than merely warn.

The three operational challenges

  • Alert fatigue — high alert volume causes critical threats to be overlooked. Quantified by the false positive rate.
  • Skills shortage — scarcity of qualified Tier 2/3 analysts and reverse engineers.
  • High infrastructure costs — expensive licensing and computational storage for multi-terabyte log retention.
Question 5 · long answer · story-based

A manufacturing company is hit by ransomware. The initial infection came through a phishing email opened by an employee in accounts; within four hours the malware had encrypted two file servers and was spreading toward the production network.

Explain how a properly functioning SOC would have (a) reduced the chance of this incident occurring at all, and (b) handled it once it began. Map your answer to the incident response lifecycle and name the technologies involved at each stage.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from This is the "how does a SOC prevent attacks" question (syllabus 2.5) asked as a story. The two-part structure — (a) prevent, (b) respond — is a direct instruction to cover both the proactive core functions (§1) and the 7-phase incident response lifecycle (§4), with the Technology pillar (§2) named throughout. The examiner is checking you do not treat "prevent" as a synonym for "respond quickly."

(a) Reducing the chance of occurrence.

The TIP feeds indicators of compromise into the SIEM and the mail gateway continuously, so the sending domain and any known-malicious attachment hashes associated with the campaign are blocked before delivery — tactical threat intelligence doing its job. Continuous monitoring of endpoints and the perimeter means that if the payload does execute, the resulting outbound C2 connection matches the botnet activity correlation rule (firewall, IDS, DNS and proxy telemetry) and is flagged in minutes rather than hours. Security posture refinement — routine patching and hardening — closes the vulnerabilities ransomware relies on to escalate privileges. Tier 3 threat hunting proactively searches for precursor activity that no rule caught. And the SOC's role in security awareness: the lecture's own professional-conduct material places educating end-users on phishing among the core duties, which addresses the actual entry point in this story.

Honest framing for full marks: none of this makes the incident impossible. Prevention reduces likelihood and shrinks the window; the SOC's real guarantee is that the incident is detected early and contained, not that it never happens.

(b) Handling the incident — the 7 phases.

  1. Alert. The SIEM fires. Two rules plausibly trigger: the virus outbreak rule, whose condition is \(\ge 3\) distinct infected endpoints within 10 minutes, and the botnet rule on the C2 callback. Technology: SIEM (Splunk / QRadar / Wazuh), EDR.
  2. Triage. Tier 1 validates authenticity, severity and urgency. Mass encryption across multiple hosts is unambiguous, so this is graded critical immediately and escalated to Tier 2 without delay.
  3. Investigation. Tier 2 reconstructs the timeline back to the phishing email, identifies patient zero in accounts, and performs scope analysis to determine every host reached so far — vital here, because the malware is moving toward the production network. Technology: EDR/XDR for cross-domain visibility linking the email, the endpoint and the network activity.
  4. Containment. The decisive phase, and the one where speed translates directly into money saved. Isolate the infected endpoints, apply network segmentation to sever the path between the office network and the production network, and revoke the compromised credentials. Technology: SOAR executing an automated ransomware playbook — machine speed matters because the malware is spreading now.
  5. Eradication. Purge the malware artefacts from every affected host, patch the exploited vulnerabilities, and close any backdoors or persistence mechanisms so it cannot re-establish.
  6. Recovery. Restore the two encrypted file servers from trusted offline backups and validate operational integrity before reconnecting them. Because eradication came first, the restored servers are not immediately re-encrypted.
  7. Reporting and continuous improvement. Post-incident review; Tier 3 tunes detection logic so mass file-modification behaviour raises an alert in its own right; the ransomware playbook is updated with what was learned; the SOC Manager reports impact and metrics to the CISO.
Marking tip

Two things lift this into the top band. First, explicitly justify the containment-before-eradication-before-recovery order — restoring backups while the attacker still has access simply re-infects the servers. Second, if the paper allows it, quantify: the whole point of the metrics half of this topic is that a lower MTTD and MTTR mean fewer encrypted servers, and Exercise 8 shows SOAR cutting both roughly in half.

Question 6 · short answer

Distinguish EDR from XDR. A company already runs EDR on all endpoints and is considering XDR — give one concrete scenario that EDR would miss and XDR would catch.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from The EDR vs XDR comparison table (§3 of the SOC lecture). The "concrete scenario" half is the discriminator — it tests whether you understand the row that says XDR detects complex, multi-stage attack chains across domains while EDR detects host-based anomalies.

Distinction. EDR monitors endpoints only — workstations and servers — using host system logs and process telemetry, giving device-level visibility, and responds by isolating an endpoint or killing a process. XDR extends that across multiple layers — endpoints, network, cloud, identity and email — correlating integrated cross-telemetry into end-to-end visibility, and responds across vectors: blocking at the firewall, revoking an identity token, isolating a host. EDR is agent-based and moderate to deploy; XDR is more complex because it needs cross-platform API integration.

Scenario EDR misses. An attacker phishes a set of credentials — no malware, no file dropped. They sign in to the company's cloud email from an unusual country, create a mail-forwarding rule to exfiltrate invoices, and then use the same credentials to authenticate to a cloud storage service. No endpoint is ever compromised, so EDR sees nothing at all: there is no malicious process to observe and no host anomaly to flag. XDR correlates the identity telemetry (impossible-travel sign-in), the email telemetry (a newly created forwarding rule) and the cloud telemetry (unusual data access) into a single multi-stage detection, and can respond by revoking the session token — a response option EDR does not have because the compromise never touched an endpoint.

Link back. This is the same argument as Question 1's case for centralising a SOC: detection that depends on joining evidence across domains is impossible when each tool sees only its own domain. XDR is that principle applied at the product level.

Topic 06

SOC metrics — the maths, from zero

Syllabus 2.6.1. Every formula the faculty gave, grouped so you can pick the right denominator.

None of this maths is hard. Every single formula on the sheet is one of two shapes:

The only two shapes on the whole sheet
\[ \text{a mean} = \frac{\text{total time}}{\text{number of things}} \qquad\qquad \text{a rate} = \frac{\text{part}}{\text{whole}} \times 100 \]

So the entire skill is choosing the right denominator. Almost every mark lost in this topic is lost by dividing by total alerts when the formula wanted total incidents, or by total incidents when it wanted actual attacks. The three groups below are organised by denominator for exactly that reason.

The denominator rule

Ask what the metric is about. If it is about incidents, divide by incidents. If it is about alerts, divide by alerts. If it is about attacks that really happened, divide by actual attacks. Write the denominator down before you reach for the calculator.

Group A — time-based metrics (denominator: a count of events)

Time metrics
\[ \text{MTTD} = \frac{\text{Total Detection Time}}{\text{Number of Incidents}} \qquad \text{MTTR}_{\text{resp}} = \frac{\text{Total Response Time}}{\text{Number of Incidents}} \] \[ \text{MTTR}_{\text{rec}} = \frac{\text{Total Recovery Time}}{\text{Number of Incidents}} \qquad \text{MTTA} = \frac{\text{Total Acknowledgement Time}}{\text{Number of Alerts}} \] \[ \text{MTBF} = \frac{\text{Total Operating Time}}{\text{Number of Failures}} \qquad \text{MTBI} = \frac{\text{Total Operating Time}}{\text{Number of Incidents}} \]

What each one actually means, in plain terms:

  • MTTD — Mean Time to Detect. On average, how long from an attack starting to the SOC noticing it. Lower is better. This is the SOC's eyesight.
  • MTTR — Mean Time to Respond. How long from noticing to acting. Lower is better. The SOC's reflexes.
  • MTTR (Recovery) — Mean Time to Recover. How long to get the systems back to normal service. Lower is better. The SOC's healing rate.
  • MTTA — Mean Time to Acknowledge. How long an alert sits unclaimed before an analyst picks it up. Note this one is per alert, not per incident — the denominator changes and that is deliberate.
  • MTBF — Mean Time Between Failures. Average uptime between system failures. Higher is better — this is the one metric on the sheet where up is good.
  • MTBI — Mean Time Between Incidents. Average calm period between security incidents. Higher is better.
Watch out — the MTTR ambiguity

"MTTR" is used for two different metrics: Mean Time to Respond and Mean Time to Recover. Your faculty lists both, and in his exercises both appear in the same question with different totals. Always label which one you are computing — write "MTTR (Response)" and "MTTR (Recovery)" in full. Recovery is always the larger number, because recovering takes longer than reacting.

Group B — rate and quality metrics

Rate & quality metrics — note which denominator each uses
\[ \text{Resolution Rate} = \frac{\text{Resolved Incidents}}{\text{Total Incidents}} \times 100 \qquad \text{Detection Rate} = \frac{\text{Detected Threats}}{\text{Actual Threats}} \times 100 \] \[ \text{False Positive Rate} = \frac{\text{False Positives}}{\text{Total Alerts}} \times 100 \qquad \text{False Negative Rate} = \frac{\text{Missed Threats}}{\text{Actual Threats}} \times 100 \] \[ \text{TPR (Recall)} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN} \times 100 \qquad \text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP} \times 100 \] \[ F1 = 2 \times \frac{\text{Precision} \times \text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}} \qquad \text{Alert Accuracy} = \frac{\text{Correct Alerts}}{\text{Total Alerts}} \times 100 \]

Two of these are worth a second look before you go near a question.

Detection Rate and Recall are the same number. "Actual threats" equals the threats you caught plus the threats you missed, which is \(TP + FN\). So \(\frac{\text{Detected}}{\text{Actual}}\) and \(\frac{TP}{TP+FN}\) are the same fraction written two ways. Your faculty's own worked example makes this explicit: detection rate \(=190/200=95\%\) and recall \(=190/(190+10)=95\%\).

Detection Rate and False Negative Rate always sum to 100%. Every actual attack was either detected or missed. So if detection rate is 94%, FNR is 6% without any calculation. Use this as a free sanity check on every question.

Group C — operational and efficiency metrics

Operational metrics
\[ \text{SLA Compliance} = \frac{\text{Resolved Within SLA}}{\text{Total Incidents}} \times 100 \qquad \text{Escalation Rate} = \frac{\text{Escalated Incidents}}{\text{Total Incidents}} \times 100 \] \[ \text{Analyst Utilisation} = \frac{\text{Time Spent Handling}}{\text{Total Working Time}} \times 100 \qquad \text{Alert Volume per Analyst} = \frac{\text{Total Alerts}}{\text{Number of Analysts}} \] \[ \text{Incident Closure Rate} = \frac{\text{Closed Incidents}}{\text{Total Incidents}} \times 100 \qquad \text{Cost per Incident} = \frac{\text{Total SOC Operating Cost}}{\text{Number of Incidents}} \] \[ \text{Threat Containment Rate} = \frac{\text{Contained Threats}}{\text{Detected Threats}} \times 100 \qquad \text{Automation Rate} = \frac{\text{Automated Responses}}{\text{Total Responses}} \times 100 \] \[ \text{Security Incident Rate} = \frac{\text{Security Incidents}}{\text{Total Systems}} \times 100 \qquad \text{Log Coverage} = \frac{\text{Systems Sending Logs}}{\text{Total Systems}} \times 100 \]

Two traps live in this group. Threat containment rate divides by detected threats, not actual threats — you cannot contain what you never saw. And security incident rate and log coverage both divide by total systems, which is the only place that denominator appears; if a question gives you an endpoint or system count, one of those two is almost certainly being asked for.

ROSI — Return on Security Investment

Not in the formula box but used in the faculty's Exercise 6, so learn it.

Return on Security Investment
\[ \text{ROSI} = \frac{\text{Loss Avoided} - \text{SOC Cost}}{\text{SOC Cost}} \times 100 \] \[ \text{where } \text{Loss Avoided} = \text{Breaches Prevented} \times \text{Average Loss per Breach} \]

Read the numerator as net benefit: what you saved, minus what you spent. Dividing by cost expresses that net benefit as a percentage of the investment. A ROSI of 800% means every taka spent returned eight in avoided loss, on top of getting the original taka's worth back.

Watch out — a genuine inconsistency in the slides

The faculty defines False Positive Rate two different ways. In the formula sheet it is \(\frac{FP}{\text{Total Alerts}}\times100\), which is the operational definition — what fraction of my analysts' work was wasted. But in his confusion-matrix exercise he computes \(\frac{FP}{FP+TN}\times100\), the statistical definition — what fraction of genuinely benign events were wrongly flagged.

These give different numbers from the same data and both are correct in their own context. The rule for the exam: if the question gives you a confusion matrix with TN in it, use \(\frac{FP}{FP+TN}\). If it gives you a total alert count and a false positive count with no TN, use \(\frac{FP}{\text{Total Alerts}}\). State which definition you used in one line — that turns a possible mark loss into a demonstration that you understood the difference.

Topic 07 · priority

The confusion matrix — precision vs. recall

The diagnostic gap. Read this section before attempting Exercise 4.

A detection system looks at events and labels each one "attack" or "normal." It can be right or wrong in two directions each way, which gives four boxes. The grid of those four counts is the confusion matrix, and every metric in this part of the syllabus is built from it.

The four outcomes

Now the two metrics, and the trick that makes them impossible to mix up.

Precision and Recall
\[ \text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP} \times 100 \qquad\qquad \text{Recall (TPR)} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN} \times 100 \]

Precision reads across the top row of the matrix. Its denominator, \(TP+FP\), is everything the system flagged. So precision answers: "of all the alarms I raised, what fraction were real?" It is a measure of how much you can trust an alert. Low precision means your analysts keep chasing nothing.

Recall reads down the left column of the matrix. Its denominator, \(TP+FN\), is everything that was actually an attack. So recall answers: "of all the real attacks, what fraction did I catch?" It is a measure of coverage. Low recall means attacks are getting through unseen.

The one line that fixes this permanently

Precision is about the alerts you raised. Recall is about the attacks that existed. Or geometrically: precision is the top row, recall is the left column. They share \(TP\) on top; only the denominator differs, and the denominator is the whole question.

Which matters more to a SOC — and why

Your faculty answers this explicitly in his own solved exercise, so it is almost certainly a discussion question on the paper. The answer is recall, and the reasoning matters more than the verdict:

  • A false positive costs analyst time. It is expensive and it causes alert fatigue, but the organisation is still safe. The damage is operational.
  • A false negative is an attack in progress that nobody knows about. The adversary keeps escalating privileges, moving laterally and exfiltrating data for as long as they go unseen. The damage is existential.

So the asymmetry is: a false positive wastes an hour, a false negative can cost the business. That is why a SOC tunes for high recall and then manages the resulting false positive burden with SOAR automation, alert enrichment and rule tuning — rather than tuning for precision and quietly missing breaches.

Watch out — the trade-off is real

Precision and recall pull against each other. Loosen the detection rules to catch more attacks and recall rises while precision falls (more alarms, more of them wrong). Tighten them and precision rises while recall falls. That is what the F1 score is for — the harmonic mean of the two, giving a single number that only stays high when both are high. If a question asks for one overall figure of merit, F1 is the answer; if it asks which to prioritise, the answer is recall.

F1 score and Accuracy
\[ F1 = 2 \times \frac{\text{Precision} \times \text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}} \qquad\qquad \text{Accuracy} = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + FN + FP + TN} \times 100 \]

One warning about accuracy. In security data, \(TN\) is enormous — most events are benign. A detector that flagged nothing at all would still score around 95% accuracy simply by being right about all the normal traffic, while catching zero attacks. So accuracy looks impressive and says very little. If a question asks you to evaluate a detector rather than just compute, saying this earns marks.

Compute F1 in fractions, not percentages

Convert precision and recall to decimals first (84.15% → 0.8415), compute \(F1\), then multiply by 100 at the end. Feeding percentages straight into the formula gives an answer 100× too large. The faculty does it this way in his worked example, and so should you.

Exercise 1 — time metrics · easy · warm-up

Over one month a SOC recorded the following. Total incidents: 180. Total detection time: 2,340 minutes. Total alerts: 450. Total acknowledgement time: 1,350 minutes. Total system operating time: 7,200 hours with 9 failures. Compute the MTTD, MTTA and MTBF, and state for each whether a higher or lower value is better.

Show worked solution

  1. \( \text{MTTD} = \dfrac{\text{Total Detection Time}}{\text{Number of Incidents}} \)

    Choose the denominator first. MTTD is about incidents, so it divides by the incident count — not the alert count sitting in the same question as a distractor.

  2. \( \text{MTTD} = \dfrac{2{,}340}{180} = 13 \text{ min} \)

    Substitute and divide. Units: minutes ÷ incidents = minutes per incident, which is what a "mean time" must be.

  3. \( \text{MTTA} = \dfrac{\text{Total Acknowledgement Time}}{\text{Number of Alerts}} = \dfrac{1{,}350}{450} \)

    Different denominator, deliberately. MTTA is the one time metric measured per alert, because acknowledgement happens to alerts before they are ever classified as incidents.

  4. \( \text{MTTA} = 3 \text{ min} \)

    Divide. Three minutes from an alert appearing to an analyst claiming it.

  5. \( \text{MTBF} = \dfrac{\text{Total Operating Time}}{\text{Number of Failures}} = \dfrac{7{,}200}{9} \)

    Third denominator: failures. MTBF measures the gap between failures, so the count of failures is what the operating time is spread across.

  6. \( \text{MTBF} = 800 \text{ hours} \)

    Divide. Units stay in hours because the operating time was given in hours — do not convert unless asked.

Answer

MTTD = 13 minutes (lower is better — faster detection). MTTA = 3 minutes (lower is better — alerts picked up sooner). MTBF = 800 hours (higher is better — longer stretches without failure).

Sanity check: 7,200 hours is about ten months of continuous running, and 9 failures across it gives roughly one failure a month — 800 hours is about 33 days, which matches. Order of magnitude confirmed.

Exercise 2 — rate metrics · easy · warm-up

A SOC handled 12,000 alerts in a quarter, of which 1,920 were false positives. There were 480 actual attacks, of which 456 were detected. Of 450 incidents, 414 were resolved within SLA and 72 were escalated to Tier 2. Of 1,200 total responses, 840 were automated. The estate has 2,400 systems, of which 2,280 send logs to the SIEM.

Compute: false positive rate, detection rate, false negative rate, SLA compliance, escalation rate, automation rate and log coverage.

Show worked solution

This exercise is entirely about denominator discipline — four different denominators appear.

  1. \( \text{FPR} = \dfrac{\text{False Positives}}{\text{Total Alerts}} \times 100 = \dfrac{1{,}920}{12{,}000} \times 100 = 16\% \)

    Denominator = alerts. No TN is given, so this is the operational definition of FPR from the formula sheet, not the confusion-matrix one.

  2. \( \text{Detection Rate} = \dfrac{\text{Detected Threats}}{\text{Actual Threats}} \times 100 = \dfrac{456}{480} \times 100 = 95\% \)

    Denominator = actual attacks. Detection rate asks what share of real threats you caught, so the base is what really happened, not what you alerted on.

  3. \( \text{Missed} = 480 - 456 = 24 \)

    Every actual attack was either detected or missed, so the missed count is the remainder.

  4. \( \text{FNR} = \dfrac{24}{480} \times 100 = 5\% \)

    Same denominator as detection rate, because FNR is also measured against actual threats. Check: \(95\% + 5\% = 100\%\) — detection rate and FNR must always sum to 100.

  5. \( \text{SLA Compliance} = \dfrac{414}{450} \times 100 = 92\% \)

    Denominator = incidents. The SLA is a promise about incidents, so total incidents (450) is the base — not the 12,000 alerts.

  6. \( \text{Escalation Rate} = \dfrac{72}{450} \times 100 = 16\% \)

    Also per incident: escalation is something that happens to a confirmed incident on its way from Tier 1 to Tier 2.

  7. \( \text{Automation Rate} = \dfrac{840}{1{,}200} \times 100 = 70\% \)

    Denominator = total responses. A fifth base appears here — automation rate compares automated responses against all responses, so neither alerts nor incidents is correct.

  8. \( \text{Log Coverage} = \dfrac{2{,}280}{2{,}400} \times 100 = 95\% \)

    Denominator = total systems. Whenever a system or endpoint count appears in a question, log coverage or security incident rate is being asked for — those are the only two metrics that use it.

Answer

FPR 16% · Detection rate 95% · FNR 5% · SLA compliance 92% · Escalation rate 16% · Automation rate 70% · Log coverage 95%.

Sanity check: detection rate + FNR = 100%. ✓ And 5% of log sources missing (120 systems) is a real finding worth one line of comment: those 120 systems are invisible to the SIEM, so any attack that touches only them cannot be detected at all.

Exercise 3 — monthly SOC performance · medium · core exam format

A SOC reports the following for one month:

  • Total incidents: 320 · Total detection time: 4,160 min · Total response time: 6,720 min
  • Total recovery time: 12,800 min · Resolved incidents: 304 · Total alerts: 10,000
  • False positives: 1,650 · Actual attacks: 350 · Detected attacks: 329

Compute MTTD, MTTR (Response), MTTR (Recovery), incident resolution rate, false positive rate and detection rate. Then evaluate the SOC's performance, identifying strengths and weaknesses.

Show worked solution

  1. \( \text{MTTD} = \dfrac{4{,}160}{320} = 13 \text{ min} \)

    Total detection time ÷ incidents. All three time metrics in this question share the same denominator of 320 — only the numerator changes, so compute the denominator once.

  2. \( \text{MTTR (Response)} = \dfrac{6{,}720}{320} = 21 \text{ min} \)

    Same denominator, response-time numerator. Label it "Response" — the next line computes the other MTTR.

  3. \( \text{MTTR (Recovery)} = \dfrac{12{,}800}{320} = 40 \text{ min} \)

    Same denominator, recovery-time numerator. Check: recovery (40) exceeds response (21), as it must — reacting is always quicker than restoring.

  4. \( \text{Resolution Rate} = \dfrac{304}{320} \times 100 = 95\% \)

    Resolved ÷ total incidents. Switching from a mean to a rate means the \(\times 100\) reappears.

  5. \( \text{FPR} = \dfrac{1{,}650}{10{,}000} \times 100 = 16.5\% \)

    Denominator changes to alerts. This is the trap in this question: 320 is incidents, 10,000 is alerts, and FPR is about alerts.

  6. \( \text{Detection Rate} = \dfrac{329}{350} \times 100 = 94\% \)

    Denominator changes again, to actual attacks. Three different denominators in one question — 320, 10,000 and 350 — which is exactly how the faculty builds these.

Answers

MTTD 13 min · MTTR Response 21 min · MTTR Recovery 40 min · Resolution rate 95% · FPR 16.5% · Detection rate 94%.

Evaluation — the marks here are for judgement, not arithmetic. Structure it as strengths, then weaknesses, then a recommendation.

Strengths. Detection is fast at 13 minutes, comfortably inside a typical sub-15-minute target. The 95% resolution rate shows incidents are being closed out rather than accumulating. Response at 21 minutes is acceptable and close to the common 20-minute SLA.

Weaknesses. Three, in priority order. The detection rate of 94% means \(350-329=21\) real attacks were never seen at all — the FNR is 6%, and those 21 are live exposure. The false positive rate of 16.5% means 1,650 of the 10,000 alerts were wrong, driving alert fatigue and, very likely, contributing to those missed attacks. The recovery time of 40 minutes is nearly double the response time, suggesting restoration is manual and under-rehearsed.

Recommendation. Tune the SIEM correlation rules to cut the false positive rate, invest in behavioural analytics to close the 6% detection gap, and automate recovery and host isolation through SOAR playbooks to bring the 40-minute recovery down.

Exercise 4 — confusion matrix · medium · priority

A machine-learning detection engine was evaluated on 8,000 network events with the following results:

  • True Positives (TP) = 1,380 · False Negatives (FN) = 120
  • False Positives (FP) = 260 · True Negatives (TN) = 6,240

Compute precision, recall, false positive rate, false negative rate, accuracy and F1 score. Then discuss which metric is most critical for a SOC and justify your choice.

Show worked solution

  1. \( \text{Total} = 1{,}380 + 120 + 260 + 6{,}240 = 8{,}000 \)

    Verify the matrix first. The four cells must sum to the stated sample size. If they do not, you have misread a value — catch it now, not three metrics later.

  2. \( \text{Precision} = \dfrac{TP}{TP+FP} = \dfrac{1{,}380}{1{,}380+260} = \dfrac{1{,}380}{1{,}640} \)

    Precision reads the top row: everything the system flagged, right or wrong. \(TP+FP\) is that row's total.

  3. \( \text{Precision} = 0.8415 \times 100 = 84.15\% \)

    Divide, then convert. Meaning: of every 100 alarms raised, about 84 were genuine attacks and 16 wasted an analyst's time.

  4. \( \text{Recall} = \dfrac{TP}{TP+FN} = \dfrac{1{,}380}{1{,}380+120} = \dfrac{1{,}380}{1{,}500} = 92.00\% \)

    Recall reads the left column: everything that actually was an attack. \(TP+FN = 1{,}500\) real attacks occurred, and 1,380 were caught.

  5. \( \text{FPR} = \dfrac{FP}{FP+TN} = \dfrac{260}{260+6{,}240} = \dfrac{260}{6{,}500} = 4.00\% \)

    Confusion-matrix definition, because TN is available. The denominator is every genuinely benign event; 4% of them were wrongly flagged. (Had the question given only "total alerts", the formula-sheet version \(FP/\text{Total Alerts}\) would apply instead — say which you used.)

  6. \( \text{FNR} = \dfrac{FN}{TP+FN} = \dfrac{120}{1{,}500} = 8.00\% \)

    Same denominator as recall — both are measured against actual attacks. Check: \(92\% + 8\% = 100\%\). ✓

  7. \( \text{Accuracy} = \dfrac{TP+TN}{\text{Total}} = \dfrac{1{,}380+6{,}240}{8{,}000} = \dfrac{7{,}620}{8{,}000} = 95.25\% \)

    Both correct boxes over everything. Accuracy is the only metric here using the full sample as its denominator.

  8. \( F1 = 2 \times \dfrac{0.8415 \times 0.9200}{0.8415 + 0.9200} \)

    Convert to decimals before substituting. Feeding 84.15 and 92.00 in as percentages gives an answer 100× too large — the single most common error on this formula.

  9. \( F1 = 2 \times \dfrac{0.7742}{1.7615} = \dfrac{1.5484}{1.7615} = 0.8790 \)

    Multiply the numerator out, add the denominator, divide. Doubling can be done before or after the division — same result.

  10. \( F1 = 87.90\% \)

    Convert back to a percentage at the very end.

Answers

Precision 84.15% · Recall 92.00% · FPR 4.00% · FNR 8.00% · Accuracy 95.25% · F1 87.90%.

Sanity check: F1 must lie between precision and recall and closer to the smaller of the two — 87.90% sits between 84.15% and 92.00%, nearer the lower. ✓ Recall + FNR = 100%. ✓

Discussion — which metric matters most.

Recall is the most critical metric for a SOC. The reason is the asymmetry of the two errors. A false positive — one of the 260 here — costs an analyst's time and contributes to alert fatigue, but the organisation remains protected. A false negative — one of the 120 here — is a real attack that nobody knows about, and the attacker continues escalating privileges, moving laterally and exfiltrating data for as long as it goes unseen. One error wastes an hour; the other can cost the business.

Concretely: at 92% recall, 120 genuine attacks reached this network undetected during the evaluation period. That is the number a CISO cares about, and it is why detection engineering tunes for coverage first and then manages the resulting noise with SOAR automation and rule tuning rather than the reverse.

Why not accuracy. Worth one line, because it is the trap. At 95.25% accuracy this detector looks excellent, but the figure is inflated by the 6,240 true negatives — benign traffic is the overwhelming majority of events, so simply being right about normal traffic carries the score. A detector that flagged nothing at all would score \(6{,}500/8{,}000 = 81.25\%\) accuracy while catching zero attacks. Accuracy is misleading on imbalanced security data.

Where F1 fits. If a single overall figure of merit is needed — for instance to compare two vendors' engines — F1 at 87.90% is the right one, because as the harmonic mean it stays low if either precision or recall is poor. But for deciding what to improve, recall is the priority.

  1. \( \text{Alerts per Analyst} = \dfrac{24{,}000}{16} = 1{,}500 \text{ alerts/analyst} \)

    Same headcount denominator, incident numerator. This is the real caseload — the 1,500 alerts mostly evaporate in triage; the 60 confirmed incidents are the work.

  4. \( \text{Total Available Time} = 16 \times 180 = 2{,}880 \text{ hours} \)

    Build the denominator before using it. Utilisation needs total working time across the whole team, which the question gives only per analyst — multiply by headcount first.

  5. \( \text{Analyst Utilisation} = \dfrac{2{,}448}{2{,}880} \times 100 = 85\% \)

Recommendations. Deploy SOAR automation to handle repetitive triage — Exercise 8 shows what that does to the numbers; tune the SIEM rules to cut alert volume at source; and increase Tier 1 headcount to bring utilisation into the 60–75% band. Note that hiring alone is the weakest of the three, because it treats the symptom rather than the alert volume causing it.

Exercise 7 — cost-benefit and ROSI · hard

An enterprise SOC costs $1,800,000 per year to run and employs 15 analysts. Last year it handled 4,500 incidents and, according to the security team's assessment, prevented 360 breaches. The average financial loss per breach in this industry is $45,000.

Compute the cost per incident, the total financial loss avoided, and the Return on Security Investment. Should management approve a budget increase? Justify your recommendation and state one limitation of this analysis.

Show worked solution

  1. \( \text{Cost per Incident} = \dfrac{\text{Total SOC Operating Cost}}{\text{Number of Incidents}} = \dfrac{\$1{,}800{,}000}{4{,}500} \)

    Denominator = incidents handled. Not analysts and not breaches prevented — cost per incident spreads the whole running cost across the caseload.

  2. \( \text{Cost per Incident} = \$400 \text{ per incident} \)

    Divide. Carry the currency through — a bare "400" loses a mark in a financial question.

  3. \( \text{Loss Avoided} = \text{Breaches Prevented} \times \text{Average Loss per Breach} = 360 \times \$45{,}000 \)

    Multiply, do not divide. Each prevented breach saves one average loss, so the total saving scales with the count.

  4. \( \text{Loss Avoided} = \$16{,}200{,}000 \)

    Compute. Order-of-magnitude check: 360 × 45,000 ≈ 360 × 45 thousand ≈ 16,200 thousand = $16.2 million. ✓

  5. \( \text{ROSI} = \dfrac{\text{Loss Avoided} - \text{SOC Cost}}{\text{SOC Cost}} \times 100 = \dfrac{\$16{,}200{,}000 - \$1{,}800{,}000}{\$1{,}800{,}000} \times 100 \)

    Subtract the cost in the numerator first. ROSI measures net return — the benefit above and beyond what was spent — which is why the cost appears in both numerator and denominator.

  6. \( = \dfrac{\$14{,}400{,}000}{\$1{,}800{,}000} \times 100 = 8 \times 100 = 800\% \)

    Divide, then scale. The currency units cancel, leaving a pure ratio — which is correct, since a return is dimensionless.

Answers

Cost per incident $400 · Total loss avoided $16,200,000 · Net benefit $14,400,000 · ROSI = 800%.

Sanity check: $16.2M of benefit for $1.8M of cost is a factor of nine, so the net return is eight times the investment — 800%. ✓ A ROSI of 0% would mean the SOC exactly paid for itself; anything positive means it more than did.

Recommendation: yes, management should approve the budget increase. The SOC returns $9 of avoided loss for every $1 spent, delivering $14.4 million of net financial protection against a $1.8 million outlay. At a cost of $400 per incident handled, it is difficult to name another control in the business with a comparable return. Framed for a board, the argument the risk-management lecture uses applies directly: breach recovery and remediation typically costs five to ten times more than proactive defence, and these figures are consistent with that.

Limitation — say this, it is where the top marks are. The entire calculation rests on "breaches prevented = 360", and that number is inherently unverifiable. You cannot observe an attack that did not happen; it is an estimate produced by the same team requesting the budget, which is a clear conflict of interest. The $45,000 average is also an industry figure that may not reflect this organisation's actual exposure, and averages conceal a skewed distribution — one catastrophic breach can dwarf hundreds of minor ones. A stronger business case would present a range rather than a point estimate, and support it with the harder metrics that are observable: MTTD, MTTR, detection rate and containment rate.

A SOC deployed SOAR automation six months ago. Compare the two periods:

ParameterBefore SOARAfter SOAR

MTTR50 min30 min
Total alerts15,00016,000

Compute the percentage improvement in MTTD and MTTR, the false positive rate before and after, and the relative reduction in false positive rate. Evaluate whether the SOAR deployment was successful.

Show worked solution

  1. \( \text{MTTD reduction} = \dfrac{24 - 10}{24} \times 100 \)

    Percentage change = (old − new) ÷ old. The original value is always the denominator — dividing by the new value is the standard error and inflates the improvement.

  2. \( = \dfrac{14}{24} \times 100 = 58.33\% \text{ reduction} \)

    Divide and scale. Detection time cut by well over half.

  3. \( \text{MTTR reduction} = \dfrac{50 - 30}{50} \times 100 = \dfrac{20}{50} \times 100 = 40\% \)

    Same formula, response figures. Twenty minutes saved on every incident.

  4. \( \text{FPR}_{\text{before}} = \dfrac{2{,}700}{15{,}000} \times 100 = 18\% \)

    Each period uses its own alert total. Comparing raw false positive counts (2,700 vs 1,280) would be misleading because the alert volume changed between periods — the rate normalises for that.

  5. \( \text{FPR}_{\text{after}} = \dfrac{1{,}280}{16{,}000} \times 100 = 8\% \)

    Same formula with the post-deployment figures. Note the denominator rose to 16,000 while the numerator fell — both movements push the rate down.

  6. \( \text{relative reduction} = \dfrac{18 - 8}{18} \times 100 = \dfrac{10}{18} \times 100 = 55.56\% \)

    Percentage change applied to the rates. Quote both forms: a fall of 10 percentage points and a 55.56% relative reduction. They are different statements and examiners look for the distinction.

  7. \( \text{alert volume change} = \dfrac{16{,}000 - 15{,}000}{15{,}000} \times 100 = 6.67\% \text{ increase} \)

    Worth computing even though not asked. It shows the improvement was not achieved by monitoring less — coverage grew while noise fell, which is the strongest possible reading of the data.

Answers

MTTD 58.33% reduction · MTTR 40% reduction · FPR 18% → 8%, a 55.56% relative reduction (10 percentage points) · alert volume +6.67%.

Evaluation: the deployment was a clear success, on every measure.

Detection and response. MTTD fell by 58.33% and MTTR by 40%. Combined, the total time from an attack starting to it being contained went from 74 minutes to 40 minutes — a 46% reduction in the attacker's window. That is the number to quote to management, because dwell time is what determines how much damage an intrusion does.

Alert quality. The false positive rate more than halved, from 18% to 8%. In absolute terms analysts now investigate 1,280 wrong alerts instead of 2,700 — roughly 1,400 hours of wasted effort returned to the team, which directly addresses the alert fatigue and analyst utilisation problems seen in Exercise 6.

The critical detail. Alert volume rose 6.67% while the false positive rate fell. This rules out the obvious sceptical explanation — that the SOC simply narrowed its monitoring and looked at less. Coverage expanded and quality improved simultaneously, which is exactly what SOAR is supposed to do: automate the repetitive triage so humans handle only what needs judgement.

One caveat for full marks. The data contains no detection rate or recall figure for either period. Faster response to the alerts you receive is not the same as missing fewer attacks, and a complete evaluation would confirm that recall held steady or improved before declaring unqualified success.

Model executive summary — the shape to copy

During the review period the SOC processed 42,000 alerts and handled 1,400 security incidents at an operating cost of $385.71 per incident. Response efficiency met expectations: mean time to respond was 18.0 minutes against a 20-minute target, and 96.0% of incidents were resolved, exceeding the 95% threshold.

However, three findings require corrective action. First and most serious, the detection rate of 94.0% against a >98% target means 90 genuine attacks went entirely undetected — this is unremediated exposure and is ranked Priority 1. Second, log coverage of 95.0% leaves 400 systems invisible to the SIEM; these two findings are almost certainly related, since an attack touching only an unmonitored system cannot be detected at all. Third, a 15.0% false positive rate against a <12% target means analysts investigated approximately 6,300 non-events, and at 1,750 alerts per analyst this workload contributed to the missed MTTD target of 11.0 minutes and to a prolonged 40-minute recovery time.

The analytical move that earns the top band is joining findings rather than listing them. Log coverage of 95% and detection rate of 94% are not two separate problems — 400 blind systems are a plausible mechanical cause of undetected attacks, and fixing coverage is therefore the highest leverage action available. Similarly, the false positive rate and the missed MTTD target are linked through analyst workload. Examiners reward causal structure over enumeration.

Last-minute revision — SOC

Definition

Centralised unit that continuously monitors, detects, analyses and responds to threats across the enterprise.

3 pillars

People (Tier 1/2/3 + Manager), Process (SOPs + playbooks), Technology (SIEM, SOAR, EDR/XDR, TIP).

Tier ladder

T1 "is it real?" → T2 "what happened, contain it" → T3 "what did we miss?" → Manager "how are we performing?"

4 TI types

Strategic (execs) · Tactical (IoCs, T1/T2) · Operational (campaigns, IR) · Technical (artefacts, researchers).

IR lifecycle

Alert → Triage → Investigation → Containment → Eradication → Recovery → Reporting. Order is examinable.

STIX vs TAXII

STIX = the language. TAXII = the taxi that transports it over HTTPS.

Time metrics

\( \text{MTTD}=\frac{\text{det. time}}{\text{incidents}} \), \( \text{MTTA}=\frac{\text{ack time}}{\text{alerts}} \), \( \text{MTBF}=\frac{\text{op. time}}{\text{failures}} \).

Precision vs Recall

Precision \(=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}\) — the alerts you raised. Recall \(=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}\) — the attacks that existed.

Which matters most

Recall. A false positive wastes an hour; a false negative is a live breach nobody sees.

F1

\( F1 = 2\times\frac{P \times R}{P + R} \). Use decimals, not percentages. Lies between P and R, nearer the smaller.

ROSI

\( \frac{\text{Loss Avoided} - \text{Cost}}{\text{Cost}}\times100 \), where Loss Avoided = breaches prevented × avg loss.

Free check

Detection rate + FNR = 100%, always. Recovery time > response time, always.

% change

\( \frac{\text{old}-\text{new}}{\text{old}}\times100 \). Divide by the original. Quote percentage points and relative %.

Denominator map

Incidents: MTTD, MTTR, resolution, SLA, escalation, cost/incident. Alerts: FPR, MTTA, accuracy. Actual attacks: detection rate, FNR, recall. Systems: log coverage, incident rate.

Accuracy trap

Huge TN inflates it. A detector flagging nothing still scores ~95%. Never use accuracy alone to judge a SOC.

Utilisation band

Sustainable is 60–75%. Above that = burnout, no threat hunting, no rule tuning.
