Topic 01

Foundations — the 5 pillars of Information Assurance

The vocabulary everything else is built on.

Before risk, the goals. Information Assurance (IA) is the practice of protecting information and the systems that hold it, and the lecture breaks it into five pillars. You almost certainly know the first three as the "CIA triad"; the faculty adds two more, and the two he adds are the ones exam questions target.

  1. Confidentiality — restricting data access strictly to authorised users and processes. Broken by: a data breach.
  2. Integrity — safeguarding information from unauthorised modification, deletion or tampering. Broken by: someone silently editing a record.
  3. Availability — ensuring timely, reliable access to data and systems for authorised users. Broken by: a DoS attack or ransomware.
  4. Authentication — verifying the claimed identity of users, devices or system entities. Broken by: stolen credentials used without MFA.
  5. Non-repudiation — cryptographic assurance that a party cannot deny sending or authorising a transaction or message. Delivered by: digital signatures, which is the direct link to the blockchain topic.
The two that get asked

Authentication answers "are you who you claim to be?" — before the action. Non-repudiation answers "can you later deny you did it?" — after the action. Confidentiality is often confused with authentication too: confidentiality is about who may see the data, authentication is about proving who someone is. You need authentication in order to enforce confidentiality, but they are not the same pillar.

Watch out

Non-repudiation is not the same as integrity. Integrity says the message was not altered. Non-repudiation says the sender cannot deny sending it. A digital signature happens to deliver both, which is why they get merged — but a hash alone gives integrity with no non-repudiation, because anyone could have computed it.

Topic 02

Core risk concepts and the risk equation

Four terms that must not be muddled, and the formula that connects them.

These four words are used loosely in ordinary speech and precisely in this subject. Getting them wrong costs marks in every risk question, so learn them as a set.

  • Asset — any valuable hardware, software, data or personnel belonging to the organisation. Note that personnel counts: the lecture's banking inventory lists C-suite, IT operations and end-users as assets with their own vulnerabilities.
  • Threat — any potential circumstance or event with the capacity to cause harm to a system. A threat exists whether or not you are vulnerable to it. Ransomware is a threat to everyone; it only harms the unprepared.
  • Vulnerability — an unpatched flaw, misconfiguration or weakness in system defences. A vulnerability is a property of your system.
  • Risk — the net negative consequence when a threat agent successfully exploits an active vulnerability.
How the four fit together
The governing risk equation
\[ \text{Risk} = \text{Likelihood (Probability)} \times \text{Impact (Consequence)} \]

Read the equation as a filter. Because the two factors multiply, a risk needs both to be non-trivial. An event that is certain but harmless scores low. An event that would be catastrophic but is effectively impossible also scores low. Only events that are both likely enough and damaging enough rise to the top. That multiplicative structure is why "we must eliminate all risk" is a meaningless goal, and why the lecture has a treatment option called Accept.

The risk evaluation matrix

The faculty's matrix turns the equation into numbers. Likelihood takes values 1.0 (High), 0.5 (Medium) and 0.1 (Low); impact takes 10 (Low), 50 (Moderate) and 100 (High). Multiply them for the risk score.

Likelihood Low impact (10) Moderate impact (50) High impact (100)
High (1.0)Low risk (10)Moderate risk (50)High risk (100)
Medium (0.5)Low risk (5)Moderate risk (25)Moderate risk (50)
Low (0.1)Low risk (1)Low risk (5)Low risk (10)
The banding

Low: 1 to 10 · Medium: above 10 up to 50 · High: above 50 up to 100. Note the boundaries are inclusive at the top: a score of exactly 10 is Low, not Medium, and exactly 50 is Medium, not High. That catches people out.

Question 1 · short answer

Distinguish between threat, vulnerability and risk, using a single running example. Explain why an organisation can face a severe threat and still carry low risk.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from The core risk concepts (Lecture 7 §2) and the risk equation from the same section. The instruction "single running example" is deliberate — it stops you defining three terms in isolation and forces you to show the causal relationship between them. The second half tests the multiplicative structure of \(\text{Risk}=\text{Likelihood}\times\text{Impact}\).

Running example: ransomware against a hospital.

Threat — any potential circumstance or event with the capacity to cause harm. Here, the existence of ransomware groups actively targeting healthcare. This threat exists independently of the hospital: it is a property of the external world, and the hospital cannot remove it.

Vulnerability — an unpatched flaw, misconfiguration or weakness in defences. Here, suppose the hospital runs end-of-life Windows machines missing critical patches — precisely the root cause the lecture identifies for WannaCry. This is a property of the hospital's own systems, and unlike the threat it is entirely within their control.

Risk — the net negative consequence when a threat agent successfully exploits an active vulnerability. Here, the ransomware group exploits the unpatched SMB service, encrypts clinical systems, and emergency services are disrupted — the actual NHS outcome in May 2017.

The relationship is a chain: the threat actor exploits the vulnerability, which causes business impact, and the combination is what constitutes the risk. Remove any link and the risk collapses.

Why a severe threat can still mean low risk. Because \(\text{Risk} = \text{Likelihood} \times \text{Impact}\), and the two multiply. A threat's severity contributes to the impact term, but if the corresponding likelihood is near zero the product is small.

Two concrete routes to this. First, no matching vulnerability: if the hospital has patched every system, enforces MFA, and holds immutable offline backups, the ransomware threat remains as severe as ever, but the probability of successful exploitation collapses, so the risk score falls. Using the faculty's matrix, an impact of 100 with a likelihood of 0.1 scores \(0.1 \times 100 = 10\) — Low risk, despite maximum impact. Second, no meaningful impact: a threat targeting a system the organisation does not operate has an impact of zero regardless of how dangerous it is elsewhere.

The practical conclusion. Organisations cannot reduce threats — those belong to the adversary. They can only reduce vulnerabilities, which lowers likelihood, and impact, through controls like segmentation and backups. Every entry in the six treatment responses works on one of those two levers, never on the threat itself.

Question 2 · easy · matrix scoring

Using the faculty's risk evaluation matrix, score and band each of the following. State the likelihood value, impact value, computed score and risk band for each.

  1. A shared classroom PC with no antivirus, used daily by many students, where malware infection would spread across the local network. Likelihood High, impact High.
  2. A projector with unsecured HDMI input in a lecture theatre; the worst outcome is a prank display. Likelihood Medium, impact Low.
  3. The Wi-Fi router with default admin credentials and remote management enabled; compromise gives full network visibility. Likelihood High, impact High.
  4. A network switch in a locked cabinet; physical access would allow traffic sniffing. Likelihood Low, impact Moderate.
Show worked solution

  1. \( \text{Risk Score} = \text{Likelihood} \times \text{Impact} \)

    Start from the governing equation. The matrix is just this formula with the three likelihood values (1.0 / 0.5 / 0.1) and three impact values (10 / 50 / 100) substituted in.

  2. \( \text{(1) } 1.0 \times 100 = 100 \Rightarrow \textbf{High risk} \)

    High likelihood = 1.0, High impact = 100. Score 100 falls in the band "above 50 up to 100", so High. This is the maximum possible score on the matrix.

  3. \( \text{(2) } 0.5 \times 10 = 5 \Rightarrow \textbf{Low risk} \)

    Medium likelihood = 0.5, Low impact = 10. Score 5 sits in the band "1 to 10", so Low — which is why the classroom inventory rates the projector Low priority despite the vulnerability being real.

  4. \( \text{(3) } 1.0 \times 100 = 100 \Rightarrow \textbf{High risk} \)

    Same values as (1). Note what this means practically: two assets tie at the ceiling, so the matrix alone cannot rank them — you break the tie with judgement about blast radius, which is why the classroom assessment rates the router "Very High" on its finer scale.

  5. \( \text{(4) } 0.1 \times 50 = 5 \Rightarrow \textbf{Low risk} \)

    Low likelihood = 0.1, Moderate impact = 50. Score 5, band Low. The locked cabinet is what drove likelihood down to 0.1 — the control has already done its work, and what remains is residual risk.

Answers

(1) score 100, High · (2) score 5, Low · (3) score 100, High · (4) score 5, Low.

Sanity check: the highest score achievable is \(1.0 \times 100 = 100\) and the lowest is \(0.1 \times 10 = 1\), so every answer must lie in \([1,100]\). ✓

Watch the boundaries: a score of exactly 10 (e.g. High likelihood × Low impact, or Low likelihood × High impact) is Low, not Medium — the Low band runs 1 to and including 10. Similarly exactly 50 is Medium, not High.

Topic 03

The risk management lifecycle and the 6 treatment responses

The process, then the menu of things you can do about a risk.

The lifecycle — five sequential stages plus two continuous ones

  1. Establish context — define the scope, the assets in question, and the organisation's risk appetite.
  2. Risk identification — find what could go wrong: the asset inventory and threat mapping work in Section 4.
  3. Risk analysis — determine likelihood and impact for each identified risk. This is where the quantitative maths of Section 5 lives.
  4. Risk evaluation — score, band and rank the risks against the matrix, deciding which are tolerable.
  5. Risk treatment / response — apply one of the six responses below.

Two activities run alongside all five rather than at any single point: communication and consultation with stakeholders, and monitoring and critical review. The lecture draws them as parallel channels for a reason — a question asking you to "draw or describe the lifecycle" expects both, and candidates who list only the five linear stages lose marks.

Watch out — analysis vs evaluation

These two adjacent stages sound identical and are not. Analysis determines the values — how likely, how damaging, what is the ALE. Evaluation compares those values against criteria and decides whether the risk is acceptable. Analysis produces numbers; evaluation produces decisions.

The six treatment responses

  1. Avoid — eliminate the risk entirely by removing the asset, disabling the vulnerable function, or altering the project architecture. The only response that takes the risk to zero, and the only one that costs you capability.
  2. Mitigate — deploy security controls, firewalls and patches to decrease the likelihood or the impact. The most common response, and the one the ALE cost-benefit maths evaluates.
  3. Transfer — shift the financial liability to a third party: buy cyber insurance, or outsource hosting. Note carefully what transfers — financial liability, not the risk itself and certainly not the reputational damage or the regulatory duty.
  4. Accept — acknowledge the residual risk when the cost of mitigation exceeds the potential loss. This is a legitimate, documented decision, not negligence — and it is exactly what the faculty's DoS calculation concludes.
  5. Contingency plan — formulate disaster recovery and incident response playbooks to execute if the risk materialises. Does not change likelihood; reduces impact when it happens.
  6. Management action — operational process changes, training, or organisational restructuring.
How to pick one in an exam scenario

Work through in this order. Is the function essential? If not, avoid. Does mitigation cost less than the ALE? If yes, mitigate. Is the loss purely financial and insurable? If yes, transfer. Is mitigation more expensive than the loss? Then accept — and document it. Add a contingency plan alongside whichever you chose, because it costs little and reduces impact. Justifying the choice against the ALE is what earns the marks.

Watch out — the favourite trap on transfer

Buying cyber insurance does not mean the risk is gone. Insurance reimburses money; it does not restore customer trust, undo a regulatory penalty, or discharge your legal duty of due diligence. The cyber law lecture is explicit that organisations retain liability even when victimised. Say this whenever transfer appears in a scenario.

The classroom assessment — the attributes and how priority is derived

The classroom exercise assesses twelve asset categories against nine columns: asset, vulnerability, risk, impact, likelihood, priority, control, motivation, attack type. Priority is computed as Impact × Likelihood, banded as Critical / Very High / High / Medium / Low.

The highest-rated items are worth knowing because they show the reasoning:

  • USB drives — Critical. Impact Very High, likelihood High. Malware carrier with no access control or encryption; the control is to disable USB storage via Group Policy or MDM. Rated highest because it is a trivially easy infiltration vector with severe consequences.
  • Wi-Fi router — Very High. Impact Very High, likelihood High. Default credentials, weak encryption, remote management enabled; compromise yields the entire classroom's traffic via man-in-the-middle.
  • Student PCs and laptops/tablets — Very High. Shared logins, no encryption, malware spreading laterally.
  • Microphones, network switch, projector — Low. Real vulnerabilities, but low likelihood or low impact, so they sit at the bottom of the queue.
If asked to build a risk assessment table

Use the faculty's nine columns and derive priority as Impact × Likelihood. The two columns candidates forget are motivation (what the attacker wants — disruption, data, admin privilege) and attack type (the method — malware, MitM, physical theft). Including them signals you used his framework rather than a generic one.

Question 3 · long answer · story-based

A small clinic in Dhaka runs a patient records system on a single server in a back office. The server has no offline backup. Reception staff share one login. The clinic's Wi-Fi is used by both staff and patients on the same network, and the router still has its factory password. Two doctors carry patient data on unencrypted USB drives between the clinic and their homes.

Produce a risk assessment for this clinic. Identify the assets and vulnerabilities, assess impact and likelihood, assign a priority, and recommend a treatment response for each with justification.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from The faculty's asset inventory and risk assessment methodology (Lecture 7 §4 and the classroom assessment document) applied to an unseen scenario, combined with the 6 treatment responses (§3) and the risk matrix (§3). The examiner wants his table structure reproduced on new material — so answer as a table, then justify below it.

Risk assessment table.

AssetVulnerabilityRiskImpLikPriorityResponse

Justification of each treatment response.

1. Server with no backup — Mitigate plus Contingency plan. Two responses because they address different halves of the equation. Mitigation — immutable offline backups, patching, endpoint protection — reduces likelihood. A contingency plan — a documented, rehearsed restore procedure — reduces impact when it happens anyway. This is the direct lesson of the WannaCry case: organisations that restored from immutable offline backups recovered without paying ransoms, and the root cause there was unpatched end-of-life systems. Backup cost for a single clinic server is trivial against the ALE of losing every patient record, so this is unambiguously worth mitigating.

2. USB drives — Avoid if the workflow allows, otherwise Mitigate. Avoidance is preferred and is genuinely available here: give the doctors secure remote access to the records system instead, and the asset — data on removable media leaving the building — simply ceases to exist. Avoidance is the only response that takes risk to zero, and it is appropriate when the function can be delivered another way. If remote access is not feasible, mitigate with hardware or software encryption plus endpoint USB scanning before mounting, which is the classroom assessment's stated control for the same asset.

3. Wi-Fi router — Mitigate. Change the default admin credentials, deploy WPA3, segment the patient guest network from the clinical network, disable remote web management, and apply firmware updates. Segmentation is the critical item: patients and clinical systems sharing one network means any compromised patient device sits directly alongside the records server. Cost is near zero, so no cost-benefit calculation is even needed.

4. Shared reception login — Mitigate with individual accounts, plus Management action. The security failure here is worth naming in the language of the IA pillars: a shared account destroys non-repudiation, because no action can be attributed to a specific person, and it undermines authentication. For a clinic handling medical records that is also an audit and regulatory problem, not only a technical one. Management action covers the process change of provisioning and de-provisioning accounts as staff join and leave.

5. Staff — Management action. People are assets with vulnerabilities in the faculty's own inventory, and phishing susceptibility is the vulnerability here. Awareness training and phishing simulation are the control. Rated Medium rather than High only because it is an enabling risk rather than a direct one — but note it is upstream of the others, since a successful phish delivers the ransomware that the missing backup then fails to survive.

Overall recommendation and sequencing. Address in this order: router credentials and network segmentation first (near-zero cost, removes the widest exposure), then offline backups (highest impact if it fails), then encrypted or eliminated USB usage, then individual logins, then training. This sequencing — cheapest and broadest first, then highest impact — is itself a mark, because risk treatment must respect a limited budget.

What should not be recommended. Accept is not defensible for any of these. The Accept response applies when mitigation cost exceeds the potential loss, and here every mitigation is inexpensive while the losses — total record loss, medical PII breach — are severe. And a clinic that accepted these risks would fail the due diligence obligation: under the cyber law lecture's framing, an organisation remains legally liable even when victimised, and failure to exercise reasonable cybersecurity diligence attracts civil regulatory penalties.

Topic 05 · priority

Quantitative risk analysis — the AV → EF → SLE → ARO → ALE chain

Syllabus 4.1. Built from zero, because the diagnostic flagged this.

Qualitative risk analysis says "this is a High risk." Quantitative analysis says "this risk costs us $56,000 a year." The second one can be compared against the price of a fix, which is why executives want it and why it is on the exam.

The whole framework is five terms in a chain, where each one feeds the next. Learn it as a chain and you cannot get lost.

The chain

Term by term

AV — Asset Value. The replacement cost of the asset, or the net financial profit earned through it. Two things to notice. First, AV can be a period profit rather than a purchase price — the faculty's DoS problem uses "$20,000 weekly profit" as the AV. Second, and this is the trap: the AV must be measured over the same period the incident affects. If each attack ruins one week's trading, the AV is one week's profit, not the annual figure.

EF — Exposure Factor. The percentage of the asset's value lost when one incident occurs, expressed as a decimal between 0 and 1.

Exposure Factor
\[ \text{EF} = \frac{\text{Loss Amount}}{\text{Total Asset Value}} \]

An EF of 1.0 means total destruction; 0.40 means 40% of the value is lost. If a question gives you a percentage ("a 40% reduction in sales"), that percentage is the EF — divide by 100 and use it. If it gives you a loss amount and an asset value instead, use the formula above to derive it.

SLE — Single Loss Expectancy. The monetary loss expected each time the incident occurs.

Single Loss Expectancy
\[ \text{SLE} = \text{AV} \times \text{EF} \]

ARO — Annualised Rate of Occurrence. How many times the incident is expected in one year. Crucially, ARO does not have to be a whole number. Seven attacks a year gives \(\text{ARO}=7\). Once every ten years gives \(\text{ARO}=0.1\). Three events observed over four years gives \(\text{ARO}=3/4=0.75\). Fractional AROs are common in exam questions about rare, severe events, and candidates who assume ARO must be an integer get them wrong.

ALE — Annualised Loss Expectancy. The expected total financial loss per year from this specific threat. This is the number you compare against the cost of a control.

Annualised Loss Expectancy
\[ \text{ALE} = \text{SLE} \times \text{ARO} \]

TCO — Total Cost of Ownership. The aggregate annual cost of acquiring, maintaining and running the mitigation control. If a vendor quotes monthly, multiply by 12 before comparing — mixing a monthly cost against an annual loss is the single most common error in these questions.

The decision rule

Compute the annual savings the control delivers, then compare:

\[ \text{Net Benefit} = \text{Annual Savings} - \text{TCO} \]

If the net benefit is positive, the control is financially justified — Mitigate. If negative, it is not — Accept the risk, or find a cheaper control. When the control eliminates the threat entirely, the annual savings equal the full ALE; when it only reduces the threat, savings are the difference between the old ALE and the new one.

Watch out — the four errors that cost marks
  1. Comparing a monthly cost to an annual loss. Always annualise the TCO first.
  2. Using an annual AV with a per-event EF. If each event damages one week, the AV is one week's value.
  3. Forgetting that partial mitigation means partial savings. A control that stops 75% of attacks saves 75% of the ALE, not 100%.
  4. Treating a negative result as "the answer is wrong." A negative net benefit is a perfectly valid finding — it is the conclusion the faculty's own worked example reaches.

The qualitative caveat the faculty makes explicitly

His DoS solution ends with a third case: the service remains unjustified on direct sales loss alone, but would become viable if it also protects other revenue channels, prevents contractual penalties, compliance fines or irreversible brand damage, or if the direct calculation underestimates downstream customer churn and restoration costs.

That paragraph is worth marks in almost every cost-benefit question. The arithmetic gives you a verdict; the qualitative caveat shows you understand what the arithmetic left out.

Topic 06

Nine worked exercises

Exercise 1 is the faculty's own problem. The rest are the same shape with new numbers.

Exercise 1 — the faculty's DoS problem · medium · learn this one cold

Your company operates an online retail store generating an average weekly profit of $20,000. It has suffered repeated Denial of Service attacks, each causing an estimated 40% reduction in sales during the affected week. Historical logs show an average of 7 DoS attacks per year. A cybersecurity vendor offers a DoS mitigation service at $10,000 per month, and testing indicates it effectively mitigates DoS attacks.

(a) What is the ARO? (b) What is the ALE? (c) Is subscribing financially justified?

Show worked solution

  1. \( \text{ARO} = 7 \text{ attacks/year} \)

    Read it directly from the question. ARO is defined as the number of threat events per year, and the historical logs give exactly that — no calculation is required. Say so; do not invent working.

  2. \( \text{AV} = \$20{,}000 \text{ per week} \)

    Match the AV period to the incident period. Each attack damages one week's trading, so the asset value in play is one week's profit — not the annual figure. This is the step most people get wrong.

  3. \( \text{EF} = 40\% = 0.40 \)

    Convert the stated percentage to a decimal. "40% reduction in sales during the affected week" is the exposure factor by definition — the fraction of asset value lost per event.

  4. \( \text{SLE} = \text{AV} \times \text{EF} = \$20{,}000 \times 0.40 = \$8{,}000 \text{ per attack} \)

    Apply the SLE formula. Each individual DoS attack costs $8,000 in lost profit.

  5. \( \text{ALE} = \text{SLE} \times \text{ARO} = \$8{,}000 \times 7 = \$56{,}000 \text{ per year} \)

    Annualise: multiply the per-event cost by the number of events per year. This is the figure to compare against the control's price.

  6. \( \text{TCO} = \$10{,}000 \times 12 = \$120{,}000 \text{ per year} \)

    Annualise the vendor's monthly quote before comparing. Comparing $10,000/month directly against $56,000/year would suggest the service is cheap — it is the classic trap in this problem.

  7. \( \text{Net Benefit} = \$56{,}000 - \$120{,}000 = -\$64{,}000 \)

    Savings minus cost. The service is stated to mitigate DoS effectively, so annual savings equal the full ALE of $56,000. The result is negative.

Answers

(a) ARO = 7 attacks/year. (b) SLE = $8,000/attack; ALE = $56,000/year. (c) No — not financially justified. The annual mitigation cost of $120,000 exceeds the expected annual loss of $56,000 by $64,000. The correct treatment response, on this analysis, is Accept the risk (or negotiate a cheaper control).

Sanity check: $8,000 per attack × 7 attacks ≈ $56,000, and $56,000 is well under half of $120,000, so the verdict is not marginal. ✓

The qualitative caveat — write this, it is worth marks. The verdict holds on direct online sales loss alone. The service would become justified if any of the following applied: it also protects other core enterprise systems and revenue channels; it prevents catastrophic contractual penalties, compliance fines or irreversible brand damage; or the direct sales figure materially understates downstream customer churn and infrastructure restoration costs. Quantitative analysis prices what you can measure; the decision should also weigh what you cannot.

Partial-mitigation variant. The faculty also works the case where the service reduces attacks by 75% rather than eliminating them. Then remaining attacks \(= 0.25 \times 7 = 1.75\)/year, new ALE \(= 1.75 \times \$8{,}000 = \$14{,}000\), savings \(= \$56{,}000 - \$14{,}000 = \$42{,}000\), and net benefit \(= \$42{,}000 - \$120{,}000 = -\$78{,}000\) — worse still. Be ready for either version.

Exercise 2 — when the control is worth it · medium

An online retailer earns an average weekly profit of $50,000. Ransomware incidents each wipe out 60% of that week's profit, and the company has averaged 4 incidents per year. A managed detection and response service is offered at $6,000 per month and is expected to prevent these incidents entirely.

Compute the SLE, ALE and TCO, and advise whether to subscribe. State which risk treatment response your advice corresponds to.

Show worked solution

  1. \( \text{AV} = \$50{,}000 \text{/week}, \quad \text{EF} = 60\% = 0.60, \quad \text{ARO} = 4 \)

    Extract the three inputs first. Weekly profit is the AV because each incident damages one week; the stated percentage is the EF; the stated frequency is the ARO. Getting these three right is most of the question.

  2. \( \text{SLE} = \$50{,}000 \times 0.60 = \$30{,}000 \text{ per incident} \)

    SLE = AV × EF. One ransomware event costs $30,000 in lost weekly profit.

  3. \( \text{ALE} = \$30{,}000 \times 4 = \$120{,}000 \text{ per year} \)

    ALE = SLE × ARO. Annualising turns a per-event figure into the yearly exposure.

  4. \( \text{TCO} = \$6{,}000 \times 12 = \$72{,}000 \text{ per year} \)

    Annualise the monthly fee so both sides of the comparison are per-year quantities.

  5. \( \text{Net Benefit} = \$120{,}000 - \$72{,}000 = +\$48{,}000 \)

    Savings minus cost. The service prevents the incidents entirely, so savings equal the full ALE. The result is positive this time.

  6. \( \text{ROI} = \dfrac{\$48{,}000}{\$72{,}000} \times 100 = 66.67\% \)

    Net benefit ÷ cost, as a percentage — the same structure as ROSI. Quoting a return rather than just a difference is what a board would ask for.

Answers

SLE $30,000/incident · ALE $120,000/year · TCO $72,000/year · Net benefit +$48,000/year · ROI 66.67%. Advise: subscribe.

Treatment response: Mitigate — deploying a security control to reduce likelihood, justified because the cost of the control is below the expected loss it removes.

Sanity check: $72,000 is less than $120,000, so the sign must be positive. ✓ Contrast with Exercise 1, where a $120,000 control faced a $56,000 loss and the sign was negative — same method, opposite verdict, which is exactly why the arithmetic matters more than intuition.

Exercise 3 — fractional ARO · medium

A company's data centre equipment is valued at $400,000. A fire would destroy an estimated 25% of it. Regional records suggest a fire of this kind occurs once every ten years. An automatic fire suppression system costs $4,000 per year to maintain and would prevent essentially all such loss.

Compute the SLE, ARO and ALE, and evaluate the investment.

Show worked solution

  1. \( \text{AV} = \$400{,}000, \quad \text{EF} = 25\% = 0.25 \)

    Here the AV is a replacement cost, not a period profit — the equipment itself is the asset. The faculty's definition allows both, and the question's wording ("valued at") tells you which.

  2. \( \text{SLE} = \$400{,}000 \times 0.25 = \$100{,}000 \)

    SLE = AV × EF. One fire costs $100,000 of equipment.

  3. \( \text{ARO} = \dfrac{1 \text{ event}}{10 \text{ years}} = 0.1 \)

    Convert a frequency into events per year. ARO is annualised, so "once every ten years" becomes 0.1 per year. ARO is not required to be a whole number — this is the point of the exercise.

  4. \( \text{ALE} = \$100{,}000 \times 0.1 = \$10{,}000 \text{ per year} \)

    ALE = SLE × ARO. Multiplying by a fraction correctly spreads a large, rare loss across the years between occurrences — that is precisely what annualising means.

  5. \( \text{Net Benefit} = \$10{,}000 - \$4{,}000 = +\$6{,}000 \text{ per year} \)

    Savings minus TCO. The cost is already annual, so no ×12 is needed — check the units the question gives rather than applying the conversion reflexively.

Answers

SLE $100,000 · ARO 0.1/year · ALE $10,000/year · Net benefit +$6,000/year. The investment is justified — Mitigate.

Sanity check: a $100,000 loss expected once a decade averages $10,000 a year, which matches the ALE. ✓

Comment worth adding. Note how ALE flattens a rare catastrophe into a modest annual figure. That is useful for comparing risks on a common scale, but it hides the fact that the loss arrives all at once. An organisation that cannot absorb a sudden $100,000 hit should treat this risk more seriously than the $10,000 ALE suggests — which is an argument for combining Mitigate with Transfer (insurance) and a contingency plan. Recognising the limitation of averaging is a higher-band observation.

Exercise 4 — partial mitigation · hard

A company's customer database is valued at $2,000,000. A data breach would expose an estimated 15% of that value in direct costs. Breaches of this type occur about once every two years.

A data loss prevention platform costing $75,000 per year would not eliminate breaches but is expected to reduce their frequency to once every five years.

Compute the baseline ALE, the residual ALE after the control, the annual savings, the percentage reduction in ALE, and the net benefit. Recommend a treatment response.

Show worked solution

  1. \( \text{SLE} = \$2{,}000{,}000 \times 0.15 = \$300{,}000 \)

    SLE = AV × EF. Note that the control changes how often a breach happens, not how bad each one is — so the SLE is computed once and reused for both scenarios.

  2. \( \text{ARO}_{\text{before}} = \dfrac{1}{2} = 0.5 \)

    Once every two years annualised. Convert the frequency to events per year before it can enter the ALE formula.

  3. \( \text{ALE}_{\text{before}} = \$300{,}000 \times 0.5 = \$150{,}000 \text{/year} \)

    Baseline annual exposure with no control in place.

  4. \( \text{ARO}_{\text{after}} = \dfrac{1}{5} = 0.2 \)

    The control acts on the ARO. Once every five years annualises to 0.2 — this is the only input the control changes.

  5. \( \text{ALE}_{\text{after}} = \$300{,}000 \times 0.2 = \$60{,}000 \text{/year} \)

    Residual ALE: the exposure that remains after mitigation. Risk is reduced, not removed — which is what "residual risk" means in the lifecycle.

  6. \( \text{Annual Savings} = \$150{,}000 - \$60{,}000 = \$90{,}000 \)

    Savings = the difference between the two ALEs, not the full baseline. This is the step people skip, and skipping it overstates the benefit by $60,000.

  7. \( \text{ALE reduction} = \dfrac{\$150{,}000 - \$60{,}000}{\$150{,}000} \times 100 = 60\% \)

    Percentage change = difference ÷ original. A 60% reduction in annual exposure — a useful figure to quote alongside the money.

  8. \( \text{Net Benefit} = \$90{,}000 - \$75{,}000 = +\$15{,}000 \text{/year} \)

    Savings minus TCO. Positive, but only just — which is itself part of the answer.

Answers

SLE $300,000 · ALE before $150,000/year · ALE after $60,000/year · Annual savings $90,000 · ALE reduction 60% · Net benefit +$15,000/year.

Sanity check: if you had wrongly used the full $150,000 as the savings you would have got +$75,000 — double the true figure. Whenever a control reduces rather than eliminates, always compute two ALEs and subtract. ✓

Recommendation: Mitigate — but flag the narrow margin. The control is financially justified, returning $15,000 net on a $75,000 spend (a 20% return). However, the margin is thin enough that the recommendation is sensitive to the estimates: if the true EF were 12% rather than 15%, the savings would fall to $72,000 and the net benefit would turn negative. Since EF and ARO are both estimates, a complete answer recommends proceeding and notes that the decision should be revisited if the breach frequency assumption proves optimistic.

Also note the residual risk. $60,000 per year of exposure remains after the control. That residual should be formally Accepted and documented — combining Mitigate with a documented Accept of what is left is how the treatment stage actually works, and saying so shows you understand the lifecycle rather than just the formula.

Exercise 5 — ranking a portfolio of risks · hard

A logistics company faces three threats:

  • A — Ransomware. Asset value $800,000; exposure factor 0.35; occurs once every two years.
  • B — DDoS on the customer portal. Asset value $120,000 (weekly revenue through the portal); exposure factor 0.50; occurs 6 times a year.
  • C — Insider data theft. Asset value $2,000,000; exposure factor 0.10; occurs once every four years.

Compute the SLE and ALE for each, the total annual risk exposure, and rank them. The company has $200,000 available for controls this year — advise how to allocate it and justify with reference to the treatment responses.

Show worked solution

  1. \( \text{SLE}_A = \$800{,}000 \times 0.35 = \$280{,}000; \quad \text{ARO}_A = \tfrac{1}{2} = 0.5 \)

    Compute SLE and ARO separately for each threat before combining. Ransomware has the largest single-event cost of the three.

  2. \( \text{ALE}_A = \$280{,}000 \times 0.5 = \$140{,}000 \text{/year} \)

    Annualise. A severe but infrequent threat.

  3. \( \text{SLE}_B = \$120{,}000 \times 0.50 = \$60{,}000; \quad \text{ARO}_B = 6 \)

    Smallest SLE of the three — but note the ARO is an order of magnitude larger. Hold judgement until the ALE is computed.

  4. \( \text{ALE}_B = \$60{,}000 \times 6 = \$360{,}000 \text{/year} \)

    Frequency dominates. The cheapest individual event produces the largest annual exposure, because it happens twelve times as often as A.

  5. \( \text{SLE}_C = \$2{,}000{,}000 \times 0.10 = \$200{,}000; \quad \text{ARO}_C = \tfrac{1}{4} = 0.25 \)

    Largest asset value, but a low exposure factor and a low rate.

  6. \( \text{ALE}_C = \$200{,}000 \times 0.25 = \$50{,}000 \text{/year} \)

    Annualise. The most valuable asset yields the smallest annual exposure — the opposite of what intuition suggests.

  7. \( \text{Total ALE} = \$140{,}000 + \$360{,}000 + \$50{,}000 = \$550{,}000 \text{/year} \)

    ALEs are additive across independent threats, which is what makes them useful for portfolio-level budgeting — you can state the organisation's total annual risk exposure as one number.

Answers

A: SLE $280,000, ALE $140,000 · B: SLE $60,000, ALE $360,000 · C: SLE $200,000, ALE $50,000 · Total annual exposure $550,000.

Ranking by ALE: B (DDoS) > A (Ransomware) > C (Insider theft).

Sanity check: B alone is 65% of the total exposure (\(360/550\)), which is a striking result worth stating explicitly. ✓

Budget advice for the $200,000.

Priority 1 — Threat B, DDoS ($360,000 ALE). Direct the bulk of the budget here. It is the largest single exposure by a wide margin, and any control costing under $360,000 a year is justified on the arithmetic alone — so $200,000 spent entirely on DDoS mitigation would still be net positive if it prevented the attacks. Response: Mitigate.

Priority 2 — Threat A, ransomware ($140,000 ALE). The important observation is that the most effective controls here are cheap: immutable offline backups, patching discipline and MFA. These address the root causes identified in the WannaCry and Colonial Pipeline cases (unpatched end-of-life systems; leaked VPN credentials without MFA) and cost a small fraction of the ALE. Response: Mitigate, plus a Contingency plan — a rehearsed restore procedure reduces impact even when prevention fails.

Priority 3 — Threat C, insider theft ($50,000 ALE). Lowest annual exposure, and technical controls against insiders are expensive relative to it. Response: Management action (access reviews, least-privilege enforcement, background screening) which is low cost, combined with formal Accept of the residual — documented, not ignored. If the loss is insurable, Transfer via cyber insurance is also reasonable given the low frequency and high single-event cost.

The analytical point this exercise is built to make. Ranking by asset value would have put C first — it protects a $2,000,000 database. Ranking by single loss would have put A first at $280,000 per event. Only ranking by ALE reveals that a frequent, individually modest threat is the dominant annual cost. Frequency matters as much as severity, and ALE is the only figure that accounts for both. Say this explicitly — it is the conclusion the question exists to elicit.

Exercise 6 — deriving EF and ARO from raw data · easy

(a) A server rack worth $300,000 suffered a power surge that caused $45,000 of damage. What is the exposure factor?

(b) Over the past four years, the company has recorded 3 such surges. What is the ARO?

(c) Using these figures, compute the ALE.

Show worked solution

  1. \( \text{EF} = \dfrac{\text{Loss Amount}}{\text{Total Asset Value}} = \dfrac{\$45{,}000}{\$300{,}000} \)

    Use the EF formula in reverse. When a question gives a loss amount rather than a percentage, this is the definition to reach for.

  2. \( \text{EF} = 0.15 = 15\% \)

    Divide. EF is dimensionless — the dollars cancel, which is a useful check that you divided the right way round. An EF above 1.0 is impossible.

  3. \( \text{ARO} = \dfrac{3 \text{ events}}{4 \text{ years}} = 0.75 \text{ per year} \)

    Events divided by the observation period. Historical incident counts must be normalised to a single year before they can be used as an ARO.

  4. \( \text{SLE} = \$300{,}000 \times 0.15 = \$45{,}000 \)

    SLE = AV × EF — which recovers the original loss amount, confirming the EF was derived correctly. Use this as a built-in check.

  5. \( \text{ALE} = \$45{,}000 \times 0.75 = \$33{,}750 \text{ per year} \)

    ALE = SLE × ARO. Three $45,000 events across four years is $135,000 total, or $33,750 per year — which matches.

Answers

(a) EF = 0.15 (15%) · (b) ARO = 0.75/year · (c) ALE = $33,750/year.

Sanity check: total observed loss = 3 × $45,000 = $135,000 over 4 years = $33,750/year. The ALE matches the direct average, as it must. ✓

Exercise 7 — the reverse question: maximum justifiable spend · medium

A manufacturer has calculated an ALE of $250,000 for supply chain compromise. A vendor claims their platform would reduce this risk by 80%.

(a) What is the maximum annual price at which this platform is still financially justified? (b) If the vendor quotes $150,000 per year, what are the net benefit and the return on investment?

Show worked solution

  1. \( \text{Annual Savings} = \text{ALE} \times \text{reduction} = \$250{,}000 \times 0.80 \)

    An 80% reduction saves 80% of the ALE, not all of it. The control's value is the exposure it removes.

  2. \( \text{Annual Savings} = \$200{,}000 \)

    Compute. Residual ALE is the other 20%: \(\$250{,}000 \times 0.20 = \$50{,}000\) still carried each year.

  3. \( \text{Net Benefit} = \text{Savings} - \text{TCO} \ge 0 \implies \text{TCO} \le \$200{,}000 \)

    Set the decision rule to break-even and solve for the unknown. The control is justified exactly while its cost does not exceed the saving it produces.

  4. \( \text{(b) Net Benefit} = \$200{,}000 - \$150{,}000 = +\$50{,}000 \text{/year} \)

    Substitute the quoted price into the same rule. Positive, so the quote is acceptable.

  5. \( \text{ROI} = \dfrac{\$50{,}000}{\$150{,}000} \times 100 = 33.33\% \)

    Net benefit ÷ cost. Expressing it as a return lets the board compare this control against other uses of the same $150,000.

Answers

(a) Maximum justifiable price = $200,000 per year. Above that the control costs more than the risk it removes. (b) At $150,000: net benefit +$50,000/year, ROI 33.33% — accept the quote.

Sanity check: the break-even price must be less than the full ALE, since the control does not remove all the risk. $200,000 < $250,000. ✓

Two qualifications worth a mark each. First, the residual risk of $50,000 per year remains after the control and must be formally accepted and documented. Second, the 80% reduction figure comes from the vendor, which is the party being paid — the analysis is only as reliable as that estimate, and a prudent buyer would seek independent evidence or negotiate contractual guarantees before treating it as fact.

Exercise 8 — choosing between two controls · hard

An e-commerce platform faces payment fraud with an ALE of $400,000. Two mutually exclusive controls are proposed:

  • Control X: costs $90,000/year, reduces the ALE by 50%.
  • Control Y: costs $250,000/year, reduces the ALE by 85%.

Evaluate both and recommend one. Would your recommendation change if the company's insurer offered to cover fraud losses above $100,000 for a premium of $40,000/year?

Show worked solution

  1. \( \text{Savings}_X = \$400{,}000 \times 0.50 = \$200{,}000 \)

    Savings = ALE × reduction fraction. Compute each option's saving before its net benefit — comparing costs alone is meaningless when the controls differ in effectiveness.

  2. \( \text{Net}_X = \$200{,}000 - \$90{,}000 = +\$110{,}000 \text{/year} \)

    Savings minus TCO. Residual ALE under X is the untouched half: \(\$200{,}000\).

  3. \( \text{Savings}_Y = \$400{,}000 \times 0.85 = \$340{,}000 \)

    Same rule applied to the stronger control. Y removes considerably more risk.

  4. \( \text{Net}_Y = \$340{,}000 - \$250{,}000 = +\$90{,}000 \text{/year} \)

    Both are net positive, but X wins on net benefit despite Y removing more risk — because Y's extra effectiveness costs more than it saves.

  5. \( \text{ROI}_X = \dfrac{110{,}000}{90{,}000} \times 100 = 122.2\%, \quad \text{ROI}_Y = \dfrac{90{,}000}{250{,}000} \times 100 = 36.0\% \)

    Return on investment confirms the ranking and sharpens it: X returns more than three times as much per dollar spent.

  6. \( \text{Marginal analysis: } \Delta\text{Savings} = \$340{,}000 - \$200{,}000 = \$140{,}000 \)

    Ask what the upgrade from X to Y buys. Moving from X to Y removes an extra $140,000 of annual exposure.

  7. \( \Delta\text{Cost} = \$250{,}000 - \$90{,}000 = \$160{,}000 > \$140{,}000 \)

    The upgrade costs $160,000 to save $140,000 — a marginal loss of $20,000. This is the decisive argument and it is more rigorous than comparing totals.

Answers

Control X: savings $200,000, net +$110,000, ROI 122.2%, residual ALE $200,000. Control Y: savings $340,000, net +$90,000, ROI 36.0%, residual ALE $60,000. Recommend Control X — higher net benefit and far higher return, and the marginal upgrade to Y destroys $20,000 of value.

Sanity check: both nets are positive, so both are better than doing nothing; the question is which is best, and marginal analysis answers it. ✓

Does the insurance offer change things? Yes, and this is the interesting half.

Insurance is the Transfer response — shifting financial liability to a third party. Under Control X the residual ALE is $200,000, and the policy covers losses above $100,000 for a $40,000 premium. Combining X with insurance caps the company's own exposure while costing \(\$90{,}000 + \$40{,}000 = \$130{,}000\) in total — still far below Control Y's $250,000, and with a lower worst-case exposure than X alone. The combined X-plus-transfer option is superior to Y.

But state the limits of transfer. Insurance reimburses money; it does not restore customer trust after a payment fraud incident, does not prevent regulatory action, and does not discharge the organisation's due diligence obligation — the cyber law lecture is explicit that liability persists even when an organisation is a victim. Insurers also price on the strength of existing controls, so the premium quoted here likely assumes a control such as X is in place. Transfer supplements mitigation; it never replaces it.

Exercise 9 — full quantitative report · hard · stretch

A Dhaka-based fintech generates $180,000 in monthly revenue through its mobile app. Three risks have been identified:

  • API abuse outage: each event halts the app for roughly a week, costing 25% of that month's revenue; observed 8 times in the last 2 years.
  • Credential stuffing: each successful campaign costs $60,000 in fraud and remediation; expected twice a year.
  • Regulatory penalty from a data breach: $5,000,000 exposure, EF 0.08, expected once in twenty years.

Compute each SLE and ALE and the total exposure. A unified security platform costing $28,000 per month would reduce API abuse by 90%, credential stuffing by 75%, and breach likelihood by 50%. Evaluate it.

Show worked solution

  1. \( \text{Risk 1: AV} = \$180{,}000 \text{/month}, \quad \text{EF} = 0.25 \)

    Match the AV period to the stated loss period. The question says "25% of that month's revenue", so monthly revenue is the AV — even though the outage itself lasts a week.

  2. \( \text{SLE}_1 = \$180{,}000 \times 0.25 = \$45{,}000 \)

    SLE = AV × EF.

  3. \( \text{ARO}_1 = \dfrac{8}{2} = 4 \text{ per year} \)

    Normalise the observed count to one year: 8 events over 2 years is 4 per year.

  4. \( \text{ALE}_1 = \$45{,}000 \times 4 = \$180{,}000 \text{/year} \)

    Annualise.

  5. \( \text{SLE}_2 = \$60{,}000, \quad \text{ARO}_2 = 2 \implies \text{ALE}_2 = \$120{,}000 \text{/year} \)

    The SLE is given directly here — no AV or EF is needed, because the question states the per-event cost. Do not manufacture an EF when the SLE is handed to you.

  6. \( \text{SLE}_3 = \$5{,}000{,}000 \times 0.08 = \$400{,}000, \quad \text{ARO}_3 = \dfrac{1}{20} = 0.05 \)

    Back to AV × EF, with a small fractional ARO for a rare event.

  7. \( \text{ALE}_3 = \$400{,}000 \times 0.05 = \$20{,}000 \text{/year} \)

    Annualise. The largest single loss produces the smallest annual figure — the same lesson as Exercise 5.

  8. \( \text{Total ALE} = \$180{,}000 + \$120{,}000 + \$20{,}000 = \$320{,}000 \text{/year} \)

    Sum across independent threats to get total annual risk exposure.

  9. \( \text{Savings} = (180{,}000)(0.90) + (120{,}000)(0.75) + (20{,}000)(0.50) \)

    Apply each reduction to its own ALE, then sum. The platform reduces the three risks by different amounts, so a single blended percentage would be wrong.

  10. \( = \$162{,}000 + \$90{,}000 + \$10{,}000 = \$262{,}000 \text{/year} \)

    Compute each term and add. Residual exposure is \(\$320{,}000 - \$262{,}000 = \$58{,}000\)/year.

  11. \( \text{TCO} = \$28{,}000 \times 12 = \$336{,}000 \text{/year} \)

    Annualise the monthly price before comparing against annual savings.

  12. \( \text{Net Benefit} = \$262{,}000 - \$336{,}000 = -\$74{,}000 \text{/year} \)

    Negative. The platform costs more than the risk it removes — and note it costs more than the entire $320,000 exposure, so no level of effectiveness could make it pay.

Answers

API abuse: SLE $45,000, ALE $180,000 · Credential stuffing: SLE $60,000, ALE $120,000 · Regulatory breach: SLE $400,000, ALE $20,000 · Total exposure $320,000/year.

Platform savings $262,000/year · TCO $336,000/year · Net benefit −$74,000/year. Not financially justified as priced.

Sanity check: the TCO ($336,000) exceeds the total ALE ($320,000), so the platform cannot break even even if it were 100% effective. That observation alone answers the question. ✓

Recommendation. Do not purchase the platform at $28,000/month. Instead:

  • Target the dominant risk separately. API abuse alone accounts for $180,000 — 56% of total exposure. A point solution (rate limiting, API gateway controls) would need to cost under $162,000/year to be justified at the same 90% effectiveness, which is well within reach of a dedicated product. Mitigate.
  • Credential stuffing at $120,000 ALE is largely addressed by MFA and credential-stuffing detection — inexpensive controls relative to the exposure. This is the Colonial Pipeline lesson: leaked credentials without MFA. Mitigate.
  • Regulatory breach at $20,000 ALE does not warrant expensive technical spend on this arithmetic. Accept the residual, formally documented, and consider Transfer via cyber insurance given the $400,000 single-event severity.
  • Negotiate. The platform becomes justified below $262,000/year, i.e. roughly $21,800/month. Quoting the break-even price gives procurement a concrete target.

The qualitative caveat. As always, the verdict holds on the quantified losses only. For a fintech, a regulatory breach may carry consequences the $20,000 ALE badly understates — licence suspension, irreversible loss of customer trust, and the due diligence liability that persists even when the firm is the victim. If the platform materially reduces those unpriced exposures, the board may reasonably approve it despite a negative arithmetic result. State the number, then state what the number omits.

Topic 07

Case studies — the what / when / who / how structure

Four landmark attacks. Learn each as five fixed fields.

The faculty presents every case in the same five fields, which is almost certainly what your syllabus's "what, when, who, how, …" refers to. Learn the grid and any case study question answers itself.

The five fields

What happened (the mechanism) · When (date) · Victims (who, and scale) · Incident response (how it was handled) · Root cause. The last field is the one that carries the marks, because it is what connects the story to the risk management lesson.

Case 1 — WannaCry ransomware (2017)

  • What: a worldwide ransomware outbreak that self-propagated by weaponising the EternalBlue exploit against an SMBv1 vulnerability. Infected endpoints were encrypted and Bitcoin ransoms demanded.
  • When: May 2017.
  • Who: over 200,000 systems across 150+ countries; the UK National Health Service suffered severe clinical and emergency service disruption.
  • How handled: Microsoft issued emergency out-of-band patches for legacy and modern Windows; researcher Marcus Hutchins found and registered a hardcoded kill-switch domain, curtailing global spread; organisations restored from immutable offline backups rather than paying.
  • Root cause: proliferation of unpatched, end-of-life Windows systems lacking critical security updates.

Case 2 — Yahoo data breach (2013–14, disclosed 2016)

  • What: state-sponsored adversaries exfiltrated full user account databases — names, emails, phone numbers, hashed passwords (MD5/bcrypt) and security questions.
  • When: breaches in 2013 and 2014; disclosed 2016.
  • Who: all 3 billion Yahoo accounts — the largest breach in digital history.
  • How handled: forced password resets, invalidation of unencrypted security questions; a $350 million reduction in the Verizon acquisition price.
  • Root cause: inadequate internal security controls, outdated hashing algorithms, and failure to detect multi-year APT presence.

Case 3 — Equifax data breach (2017)

  • What: attackers exploited an unpatched remote code execution flaw in Apache Struts (CVE-2017-5638) to exfiltrate sensitive PII.
  • When: July 2017.
  • Who: approximately 147 million US consumers — Social Security numbers, birth dates, driver's licence records.
  • How handled: free credit monitoring offered; CEO, CIO and CISO resigned and testified before Congress; substantial financial settlements and lasting brand damage.
  • Root cause: systemic failure in vulnerability management and patch deployment despite published vendor alerts.

Case 4 — Colonial Pipeline ransomware (2021)

  • What: the DarkSide group compromised billing and business networks, prompting a precautionary shutdown of the pipeline itself.
  • When: May 2021.
  • Who: Colonial Pipeline Company; fuel shortages, flight cancellations and consumer panic across the US East Coast.
  • How handled: paid roughly $4.4 million (75 Bitcoin), of which about $2.3 million was later recovered by the FBI; operations restored after several days.
  • Root cause: leaked legacy VPN credentials without multi-factor authentication.
The pattern across all four

Three of the four root causes are basic hygiene failures: unpatched end-of-life systems (WannaCry), unapplied vendor patches (Equifax), and credentials without MFA (Colonial). None involved an unknown zero-day. That is the single most useful sentence you can write about these cases, and it is exactly the argument the business-case model uses to justify security spending.

Question 4 · long answer

Select two of the landmark case studies covered in class. For each, state what happened, when, who was affected, how the incident was handled and its root cause. Then identify what the two cases have in common and what preventive controls would have stopped both.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from The landmark case studies (Risk Management & Governance §1), structured on the faculty's own five fields. The final part is the real question — "what do they have in common" pulls in the root cause analysis from §2 of the business case model (human vulnerability, legacy IT, misaligned budgeting, governance deficits, supply chain risk). Choose two cases with a shared root cause so the synthesis writes itself: WannaCry and Equifax are the natural pair, since both are patching failures.

Case 1: WannaCry (May 2017). What: a self-propagating ransomware worm weaponising the EternalBlue exploit against an SMBv1 vulnerability; infected endpoints were encrypted and Bitcoin ransoms demanded. Who: over 200,000 systems in more than 150 countries, with the UK NHS suffering severe disruption to clinical and emergency services. How handled: Microsoft released emergency out-of-band patches covering even end-of-life Windows versions; Marcus Hutchins discovered and registered the hardcoded kill-switch domain, halting further global propagation; affected organisations restored from immutable offline backups instead of paying. Root cause: proliferation of unpatched, end-of-life Windows systems lacking critical security updates.

Case 2: Equifax (July 2017). What: attackers exploited an unpatched remote code execution vulnerability in the Apache Struts web framework, CVE-2017-5638, to exfiltrate large volumes of PII. Who: approximately 147 million US consumers, exposing Social Security numbers, birth dates and driver's licence records. How handled: free credit monitoring offered to those affected; the CEO, CIO and CISO resigned and testified before Congress; substantial financial settlements, a severe stock price drop and lasting brand damage followed. Root cause: systemic failure in vulnerability management and patch deployment despite published vendor alerts.

What they have in common.

  1. Both root causes are patching failures, not novel attacks. In each case a fix existed and had been published before the incident — Microsoft's MS17-010 for SMBv1, and Apache's advisory for CVE-2017-5638. Neither was a zero-day. The organisations were breached through known flaws they had not remediated.
  2. Both are failures of process rather than technology. No new product was required to prevent either; what was missing was a vulnerability management lifecycle that reliably identified and deployed available patches. In the business-case vocabulary this is the governance deficit and legacy IT root cause, not a technical capability gap.
  3. Both show impact far exceeding the direct technical event. WannaCry's cost was measured in cancelled operations and diverted ambulances; Equifax's in resignations, congressional testimony, settlements and permanent brand damage. This supports the lecture's claim that breach recovery typically costs five to ten times more than proactive defence.

Controls that would have prevented both.

  • A formal patch and vulnerability management lifecycle — the single control addressing both root causes directly. Continuous vulnerability assessment scanning to identify missing patches, with defined SLAs for remediating critical CVEs. This is the VAPT topic's contribution.
  • Asset inventory and lifecycle management, so end-of-life systems are identified and either upgraded or isolated before support ends. WannaCry spread through machines the organisations often did not know they still had.
  • Network segmentation, which limits impact when prevention fails. WannaCry's worm behaviour depended on flat networks; segmentation would have contained it, reducing the impact term of \(\text{Risk} = \text{Likelihood} \times \text{Impact}\) even where likelihood could not be reduced.
  • Immutable offline backups with rehearsed restore procedures — the Contingency plan treatment response. The organisations that recovered from WannaCry without paying were precisely those who had this.
  • Continuous monitoring through a SOC, to detect exploitation and exfiltration in progress. Equifax's data was exfiltrated over an extended period without detection, which is a detection-rate failure in the SOC topic's terms.

Closing synthesis. Both incidents are, at root, the same failure: an organisation that knew a patch existed and did not deploy it in time. They are the strongest available argument for treating patch management as a governance obligation rather than an IT chore — and, in the Bangladeshi context, for the due diligence obligation the cyber law lecture describes, under which an organisation remains liable even when victimised if it failed to exercise reasonable cybersecurity diligence.

Topic 08

The business case model and governance frameworks

How to pitch security to decision makers, plus the compliance table.

The four-part executive appeal framework

  1. Issue — the problems. Rising threat landscape (ransomware-as-a- service, spear-phishing, DDoS, insider threats); direct business impact (downtime, revenue loss, data theft, brand erosion); compliance exposure (GDPR, HIPAA, PCI-DSS penalties); productivity loss to incident response.
  2. Root cause — why problems persist. Human vulnerability (weak awareness, weak passwords, social engineering); legacy IT (unsupported, unpatched, technical debt); misaligned budgeting (security seen as overhead rather than enabler); governance deficits (no documented IR, monitoring or access policy); supply chain risk (unvetted third parties as pivot points).
  3. Solution — the controls. Technical: NGFW, EDR, SIEM, zero-trust network access, encryption, MFA. Process: vulnerability scans, independent penetration tests, backup drills. People: mandatory awareness training and phishing simulation. Governance: formal adoption of ISO 27001 or NIST CSF.
  4. Effect — measurable benefits. Financial protection; operational resilience with faster RTO; competitive advantage from certifications (ISO 27001, SOC 2) that build client trust and shorten sales cycles; regulatory compliance.

The implementation roadmap is worth memorising as three horizons: short-term (weeks–months) — enforce MFA everywhere, patch critical CVEs, baseline awareness training; medium-term (6–12 months) — deploy SIEM/SOC monitoring, refine policies, conduct compliance audits; long-term (1–3 years) — build a resilient zero-trust architecture and a security culture.

The killer statistic

Breach recovery and remediation costs typically run 5× to 10× higher than proactive defence investment. That single line is the "risk of inaction" argument and belongs in any answer about justifying security budget to management.

Governance frameworks — the ones most likely to be asked

The full table covers fourteen frameworks. Rather than memorising all of them, learn what distinguishes the commonly-confused ones:

  • ISO 27001 — the global standard for establishing and operating an ISMS (Information Security Management System). Certifiable by an accredited third-party registrar; audit takes 1 week to 1 month; annual.
  • SOC 2 — governs customer data security, availability and privacy for SaaS and B2B vendors. Not a certification — it produces an attestation report from an independent CPA firm, over roughly a 6-month monitoring period.
  • NIST CSF — the framework for Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover. Not certifiable; self-assessment or third-party gap analysis; continuous.
  • PCI DSS — mandatory for any entity storing, processing or transmitting card data. Assessed by a QSA (Qualified Security Assessor) or via self-assessment questionnaire; annual.
  • GDPR — EU privacy regulation applying to any organisation processing EU residents' personal data. Enforced by Data Protection Authorities; ongoing and event-driven.
  • HIPAA — protects PHI (Protected Health Information) for US healthcare providers, plans, clearinghouses and business associates.
  • CIS Controls — a prioritised set of 18 prescriptive best practices, for organisations of any size wanting rapid threat reduction.
Watch out — the distinction examiners love

ISO 27001 gives a certification. SOC 2 gives an attestation report. NIST CSF gives neither — it is a self-assessed framework. If a question asks which framework an organisation should adopt, the answer usually turns on whether they need something certifiable to show clients, and on which regulator has jurisdiction over their data.

Last-minute revision — Risk Management

5 IA pillars

Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability, Authentication, Non-repudiation.

The 4 concepts

Asset (what you own) · Threat (external, uncontrollable) · Vulnerability (your flaw) · Risk (the consequence).

Risk equation

\( \text{Risk} = \text{Likelihood} \times \text{Impact} \). Multiplicative — either factor near zero kills the risk.

Matrix values

Likelihood 1.0 / 0.5 / 0.1 · Impact 100 / 50 / 10. Bands: Low 1–10, Medium >10–50, High >50–100.

THE CHAIN

\( \text{SLE}=\text{AV}\times\text{EF} \) then \( \text{ALE}=\text{SLE}\times\text{ARO} \). Compare ALE vs TCO.

EF

\( \text{EF}=\frac{\text{Loss Amount}}{\text{Asset Value}} \). Decimal 0–1. A stated % is the EF.

ARO

Events per year. Can be fractional: once in 10 yrs = 0.1; 3 events in 4 yrs = 0.75.

Decision rule

Net = Savings − TCO. Positive → Mitigate. Negative → Accept.

Partial mitigation

Savings = ALEbefore − ALEafter. Not the full ALE.

×12 trap

Monthly quote → always annualise before comparing to ALE. $10k/mo = $120k/yr.

AV period trap

If the event damages one week, AV = one week's value. Match the period.

6 responses

Avoid · Mitigate · Transfer · Accept · Contingency plan · Management action.

Transfer limit

Insurance moves money, not reputation, not regulatory duty, not due-diligence liability.

Lifecycle

Context → Identify → Analyse → Evaluate → Treat, with Communication and Monitoring running alongside.

Case root causes

WannaCry: unpatched EOL Windows. Equifax: unpatched Struts CVE-2017-5638. Colonial: leaked VPN creds, no MFA. Yahoo: weak controls + undetected APT.

Case scale

WannaCry 200k systems/150 countries · Yahoo 3 billion accounts · Equifax 147m · Colonial $4.4m ransom.

Business case

Issue → Root cause → Solution → Effect. Inaction costs 5×–10× proactive defence.

Frameworks

ISO 27001 = certification. SOC 2 = attestation report. NIST CSF = self-assessed. CIS = 18 controls.

Qualitative caveat

Always close a cost-benefit answer with what the arithmetic omitted: churn, fines, brand, contractual penalties.
