Topic 01

What VAPT is

The definition, and why the two halves are named together.

Start with the plain picture. Imagine you own a building and want to know how secure it is. You could hire someone to walk the perimeter with a clipboard and list every weakness — this window has a broken latch, that door's lock is an old model, the fire exit does not close properly. Thorough, fast, covers the whole building, and completely non-destructive. That is a vulnerability assessment.

Or you could hire someone to actually break in. They pick one weak point, get through it, and then see how far into the building they can travel — can they reach the server room from a broken ground-floor window? That is a penetration test. It covers far less ground, but what it tells you is real rather than theoretical.

The faculty's definition

VAPT provides an end-to-end evaluation of an organisation's defensive posture by combining automated vulnerability discovery with simulated adversarial exploitation. The two words in that sentence doing the work are combining — VAPT is deliberately a pairing, not one technique.

Your lecture summarises the split as two questions:

  • Vulnerability Assessment asks: "what is weak or unpatched?" Non-intrusive automated scans, broad environment-wide scope, identifies known CVEs and flaws. (A CVE — Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures — is the public catalogue ID given to a known, published security flaw.)
  • Penetration Testing asks: "can these weaknesses be exploited to gain deep access?" Intrusive, targeted simulations, demonstrates real-world risk.

▶ Vulnerability Assessments Vs. Penetration Testing: What's The Difference? — Blue Goat Cyber. Why it helps: a short, plain-language framing of the breadth-versus-depth trade-off, which is the axis your whole answer to Question 2 should turn on.

Topic 02 · priority

VA vs. PT — and how they complete each other

The comparison table, then the argument the faculty asks for.

Criteria Vulnerability Assessment Penetration Testing
Primary objectiveIdentify, catalogue and rank vulnerabilitiesExploit weaknesses to validate attack feasibility
Nature of testingNon-intrusive; passive/active scanningIntrusive; active exploitation and privilege escalation
Scope & breadthBroad across all network assetsNarrow and deep into specific target systems
FrequencyContinuous, daily or weekly scansQuarterly, annual or milestone-based
Execution methodPredominantly automated scannersAutomated scripts plus manual testing
Primary outputComprehensive vulnerability checklistRemediation report based on demonstrated exploits
Business valueBaseline vulnerability hygiene & complianceProof of exploitability and defence readiness
Compress the table to one axis

Every row is a variation on breadth versus depth. VA is wide, shallow, automated, frequent and cheap. PT is narrow, deep, manual, occasional and expensive. If you can only remember one thing, remember that — and then each row can be reconstructed from it.

Why neither alone is sufficient

What a vulnerability assessment cannot tell you. A scanner produces a list, typically a long one, of flaws ranked by a generic severity score. Four things it cannot do. It cannot tell you whether a given flaw is actually reachable in your specific configuration — a critical-rated CVE on a service that is firewalled off from every network an attacker can touch is not a real risk. It cannot chain flaws: three individually "medium" findings that combine into full domain compromise still read as three mediums on the report. It cannot tell you what an attacker would reach once through. And it produces false positives, because it infers from version banners rather than proving anything.

What a penetration test cannot tell you. A pen test is scoped, timed and narrow. It cannot give you coverage — a tester with two weeks and a defined scope examines a fraction of an enterprise estate. It cannot run continuously, so it is a snapshot that begins ageing the moment the report is signed; a server patched on Monday and a new vulnerability published on Tuesday are both invisible to a test that finished last month. And it is expensive per unit of ground covered.

The completion argument in one sentence

VA gives breadth without proof; PT gives proof without breadth. Run together, the assessment tells you everywhere you might be weak and the penetration test tells you which of those weaknesses actually matter — so the pair converts a long unprioritised list into a short evidence-backed one.

Question 2 · long answer · priority

Explain how vulnerability assessment and penetration testing complete each other in enterprise system testing. Your answer should make clear why an organisation performing only one of the two has an incomplete picture of its security posture.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from This is the flagship VAPT question and it draws on the whole lecture: the VA vs PT table (§2) for the contrasts, the scan types (§3) for what VA covers, the 7-stage methodology (§4) for what PT actually does, and the benefits (§7) for the business framing. The word "complete" is the instruction — the examiner does not want a comparison, they want an argument that the two are interdependent. Structure: what each provides → what each lacks → how the gaps interlock → the combined workflow → the business consequence.

1. What each provides. Vulnerability assessment provides coverage. It runs automated, non-intrusive scans across the entire estate — network-based, host-based, wireless, application and database scans — and returns a comprehensive, ranked catalogue of known CVEs and misconfigurations. Because it is cheap and automated it can run continuously, so it maintains a current picture of the whole environment.

Penetration testing provides proof. It takes specific targets and, through the seven-stage methodology — pre-engagement, reconnaissance, scanning and enumeration, gaining access, maintaining access, covering tracks, reporting — actively exploits weaknesses using a combination of automated scripts and manual technique, and demonstrates what an adversary could genuinely achieve.

2. What each lacks — and this is where the argument lives.

A vulnerability assessment produces findings, not risk. It cannot establish exploitability: a scanner infers a flaw from a version banner or a configuration value, so a "critical" finding on a service that is unreachable from any network an attacker can touch is not, in that environment, a critical risk. It cannot establish chaining: three separately medium-rated findings — a weak service account, an unpatched internal file server, and an over-permissive share — may combine into full domain compromise, but the report shows three mediums and no relationship between them. It cannot establish business impact, because it does not know what lies beyond the flaw. And it generates false positives, so a proportion of the remediation effort it triggers is wasted.

A penetration test produces proof, not coverage. Its scope is narrow and deep by design, so an enterprise estate of thousands of hosts is sampled, not surveyed — the tester examines specific systems within an agreed window. It is periodic, quarterly or annual, which makes it a snapshot: a system patched the week after the test, or a CVE published the day after the report, are both outside its knowledge. And its cost per host examined is high, so it cannot be scaled to the whole estate at any sensible budget.

3. How the gaps interlock. Read those two paragraphs against each other and they are complementary, not merely different. VA's weakness is precisely PT's strength — VA cannot prove exploitability, PT proves it. And PT's weakness is precisely VA's strength — PT cannot cover the estate continuously, VA does exactly that. Neither gap can be closed by doing more of the same discipline: scanning ten times more often still never proves an exploit, and pen testing every host is unaffordable.

4. The combined workflow in practice. The two are sequenced, and describing the sequence is what shows real understanding.

  1. VA runs continuously across the estate and produces the ranked inventory of weaknesses. This is the baseline hygiene layer and it also satisfies the continuous-monitoring requirements of ISO 27001, PCI-DSS and HIPAA.
  2. VA output feeds PT scoping. Rather than the tester beginning blind, the assessment identifies the highest-value targets, so the expensive manual effort is aimed where it will learn the most. This is why the pen test methodology's own stage 3 is "scanning and enumeration" — the disciplines are already nested.
  3. PT validates and prioritises. The tester attempts exploitation and returns three verdicts on the VA list: confirmed and exploitable, present but not exploitable in this configuration, and false positive. That converts a list of hundreds into a short, evidence-backed remediation queue — and it demonstrates chains the scanner could not see.
  4. PT also tests the defenders, not just the systems. Stage 6, covering tracks, assesses whether the SOC's logging captured the intrusion and whether forensic traces could be purged. A vulnerability scan can never do this, because nothing is attacked. This is the lecture's benefit "strengthen incident response — test blue-team detection capabilities against active simulations."
  5. PT findings feed back into VA. New attack paths discovered manually become new things to scan for continuously, so the baseline improves.

5. Why one alone leaves you blind — state both failure cases.

VA only: the organisation has a long list of theoretical weaknesses with no way to prioritise them, patches by generic severity score rather than by real exposure, remains vulnerable to chained attacks that no single finding predicts, and has never tested whether its SOC would notice an intrusion. It is measuring exposure and calling it risk.

PT only: the organisation has excellent evidence about a small slice of its estate at a single point in time, and no idea about the rest. Between annual tests the picture decays continuously as new CVEs are published and new systems are deployed. It is measuring risk on a sample and calling it coverage.

Conclusion. VAPT is named as one discipline because the two halves answer different questions that are only useful together: vulnerability assessment asks "where might we be weak?" and penetration testing asks "does it actually matter?" Breadth without proof produces noise; proof without breadth produces false confidence. Combined, they produce the end-to-end evaluation of defensive posture that the lecture's definition describes — and deliver the stated business benefits: real-time risk visibility quantified by demonstrated exploit paths, regulatory compliance, tested incident response, and security budgets justified by data rather than by scanner severity ratings.

Marking tip

The single highest-value sentence you can write is "VA measures exposure, PT measures risk." Open or close with it. Then make sure you have covered the four interlocking pairs — coverage/proof, continuous/periodic, cheap/expensive, automated/manual — and both failure cases. Candidates who merely re-state the comparison table lose the marks for "complete each other."

Topic 03

Vulnerability scanning — the five types

Plus host discovery methods and the SAST/DAST distinction.

  1. Network-based scans — discover open ports, unencrypted services and weak access control lists (ACLs). This is the view from the network: what is listening, and can it be reached?
  2. Host-based scans — inspect operating system configurations, missing patches and registry settings. This is the view from inside a machine, which sees things no network scan can.
  3. Wireless scans — identify rogue access points and WEP/WPA misconfigurations. (A rogue access point is an unauthorised wireless device attached to the network, whether malicious or simply someone's own router plugged in without approval.)
  4. Application scans — discover SQL injection, XSS, CSRF and API flaws in web applications.
  5. Database scans — test database accounts, default credentials and weak SQL configurations.

The five are not alternatives; they are different vantage points. A host with a critical missing patch may present no unusual open port at all, so a network-based scan sees nothing while a host-based scan sees the flaw immediately. That "different vantage point" framing is what to say if asked why all five are needed.

Host discovery methods

Before scanning anything you have to find out what exists. Two methods:

  • ARP requests for local subnet discovery. ARP (Address Resolution Protocol) operates within a single local network segment and is reliable there because hosts must answer it to communicate at all.
  • ICMP echo requests — ordinary pings — for wider network ranges, since ARP cannot cross a router.

SAST vs DAST

Two ways to test an application

SAST — Static Application Security Testing: scans source code in a non-running state. Reads the code as text, looking for dangerous patterns.

DAST — Dynamic Application Security Testing: scans running applications in real time, assessing behaviour by sending inputs and observing responses.

The memory hook: static = still, dynamic = running. SAST needs the source code and can be run before the application is ever deployed, which makes it cheap to fix findings — but it cannot see anything that only appears at runtime, such as a misconfigured server or a flaw in how two services interact. DAST needs no source code and tests the application as an attacker would meet it, but it can only reach what is exposed and reports symptoms rather than the offending line of code.

Watch out

SAST and DAST map onto the white-box / black-box distinction in Section 5. SAST requires full source access, so it is inherently a white-box technique; DAST works from outside with no internal knowledge, so it is black-box in character. Making that link explicitly is an easy extra mark.

Question 3 · medium · story-based

A university's IT team is asked to assess the security of its campus systems. During a walk-through they observe the following:

  • The student portal accepts a search term and, when a single quote is typed into it, returns a database error message.
  • Several lab PCs are three months behind on operating system updates.
  • An unfamiliar wireless network named "CampusWiFi_Free" is broadcasting from the library.
  • The results database still has an account with the vendor's default username and password.
  • A file server has port 21 open and running plain FTP.

For each observation, identify which type of vulnerability scan would have detected it, and state whether the finding is a vulnerability, a risk, or both. Then explain what a penetration test would add.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from This is the faculty's story format applied to the 5 types of vulnerability scanning (VAPT lecture §3). The second half — vulnerability versus risk — pulls in the definitions from the Risk Management lecture (vulnerability = a flaw or weakness; risk = the consequence when a threat exploits it), so the examiner is checking you can use the two vocabularies together. The final part is the VA/PT link from §2.

Mapping each observation to a scan type:

  • Portal returning a database error on a single quote → Application scan. This is the classic signature of SQL injection: unsanitised input reaching the database engine. Tools: Burp Suite or OWASP ZAP; Nikto for the server layer.
  • Lab PCs three months behind on patches → Host-based scan. Missing patches and OS configuration are visible from inside the machine, not from the network. Tool: Nessus or OpenVAS with credentialed host scanning.
  • Unfamiliar "CampusWiFi_Free" network → Wireless scan. This is a textbook rogue access point — either an attacker running an evil-twin network to harvest credentials, or a well-meaning staff member's own router. Either way it is an unmanaged path onto the network.
  • Default vendor credentials on the results database → Database scan, which specifically tests database accounts and default credentials.
  • Port 21 open running plain FTP → Network-based scan, which discovers open ports and unencrypted services. Tool: Nmap for discovery, Wireshark to demonstrate that FTP credentials cross the wire in cleartext.

Vulnerability or risk? Every one of the five is a vulnerability — a flaw, misconfiguration or weakness. None of them is yet a risk, because risk requires a threat actor and an impact: \(\text{Risk} = \text{Likelihood} \times \text{Impact}\). The default database credentials become a risk only when you add "an attacker who can reach that database" and "the student results it contains." Stating that distinction explicitly is the point of the question — a scanner outputs vulnerabilities, and turning them into risks requires judgement the scanner does not have.

That said, two of the five carry enough context to assess as high risk immediately: the default credentials on a system holding student records (high impact, trivial likelihood), and the rogue access point (it may already be an active attack rather than a latent weakness).

What a penetration test adds. Four things the scan cannot deliver.

  1. Proof of exploitability. The tester actually performs the SQL injection and shows what data it returns. "Returns a database error" is a symptom; "retrieves the full student results table" is a finding a university has to act on.
  2. Chaining. Individually these are five separate findings. A tester would try to link them: connect to the rogue access point to reach the internal network, sniff cleartext FTP credentials from the file server with Wireshark, and reuse those credentials elsewhere — or use the SQL injection to reach the database the default credentials also protect. The combination is far more serious than any single item, and only an attack simulation reveals it.
  3. Business impact. The pen test report says what was reached — student records, exam papers, staff payroll — which is what turns a technical list into a prioritised remediation queue.
  4. Testing the defenders. Did anything alert while all this was happening? Stage 6 of the methodology, covering tracks, assesses whether logging captured the intrusion. A scan tells you the door is unlocked; a pen test tells you nobody noticed someone walking through it.
Marking tip

The chaining paragraph is what separates a good answer from a list. Give at least one concrete chain — rogue AP → sniff FTP credentials → reuse elsewhere — because it demonstrates you understand why the disciplines are paired rather than merely that they differ.

Topic 04

The 7-stage penetration testing methodology

Learn the order. It is the most reliably examined sequence in this lecture.

  1. Pre-engagement. Define rules of engagement, target scope, testing windows and legal authorisations. Nothing else may begin until this is signed — without written authorisation the identical activity is a criminal offence under Sections 18 and 32 of the Bangladesh cyber law.
  2. Reconnaissance (OSINT and intelligence gathering). Collect domain records, DNS entries, mail server headers and network infrastructure data. OSINT means Open Source Intelligence — information gathered from publicly available sources, touching nothing belonging to the target.
  3. Scanning and enumeration. Port scanning, banner grabbing and service enumeration, using tools such as Nmap and Nessus. Banner grabbing means reading the identifying text a service returns on connection, which usually reveals its exact software version.
  4. Gaining access (exploitation). Weaponise the identified vulnerabilities — SQL injection, buffer overflows, session hijacking, password cracking — to breach the target perimeter.
  5. Maintaining access (persistence). Deploy reverse shells, scheduled jobs and backdoors, and establish command-and-control communication. This simulates what a real adversary does after the initial breach, and it tests whether the organisation would detect an attacker who is already inside.
  6. Covering tracks. Assess logging effectiveness and evaluate whether forensic traces can be modified or purged. Note the framing carefully: the tester is not hiding, they are measuring whether hiding is possible.
  7. Reporting. Deliver technical and executive reports documenting proof-of-concept exploits, business impact and remediation steps.
Watch out — the two stages people misread

Stage 6 is not the tester covering up wrongdoing. It exists to answer "would our SOC have caught this, and could an attacker have erased the evidence?" It is a test of the defenders. And stage 1 is not paperwork you can skip in an exam answer — authorisation is what separates penetration testing from crime, and examiners give it a mark.

The three testing approaches

  • Black box — the tester has zero prior knowledge of internal architecture or source code. Most realistically simulates an external attacker. Slowest, and may miss internal issues simply for lack of time.
  • Gray box — partial knowledge: low-privilege user credentials, network diagrams. Simulates an attacker who has already gained a foothold, or a malicious insider with ordinary access. Usually the best value.
  • White box — full knowledge, including architectural documentation and complete source code. Most thorough coverage in the time available; least realistic as an attack simulation.
How to choose

Black box when you want to know what an outsider could achieve. Gray box when you want to know what a compromised user account or a disgruntled employee could achieve. White box when you want maximum coverage and the deepest possible code review, and realism is not the goal.

Question 4 · medium · story-based

A penetration testing firm is engaged by a Dhaka-based fintech company. Over three weeks the following happens, in this order:

  1. The firm and the client sign a document defining which IP ranges may be tested and that testing will occur between 01:00 and 05:00 only.
  2. A tester searches public DNS records and LinkedIn and builds a list of employee email addresses and the company's mail server details.
  3. Nmap is run against the agreed IP range; a web server responds with a banner identifying an outdated version of its software.
  4. The tester exploits a known flaw in that version and obtains a shell on the server.
  5. A scheduled job is installed that reopens the connection every hour.
  6. The tester checks whether the client's SIEM logged any of it, and whether the relevant log entries could be deleted.
  7. A document is produced containing screenshots of the shell, an assessment of what customer data was reachable, and a patching plan.

Identify the methodology stage each numbered event corresponds to. State which testing approach appears to have been used and justify your answer. Explain why event 1 is legally indispensable.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from The 7-stage methodology (VAPT §4) presented as a story to be decoded — precisely the analytical format you flagged as the faculty's style. It also asks for the testing approaches (§4) and connects to Cyber Law Section 18/32 on unauthorised access. Three lecture points in one question, so allocate your time accordingly: the mapping is quick, the justification and the legal point need real sentences.

Stage mapping — one line each:

  1. Pre-engagement. Rules of engagement, target scope, testing window and legal authorisation are exactly this stage's deliverables.
  2. Reconnaissance (OSINT). DNS records, mail server details and employee information gathered from public sources — nothing belonging to the client has been touched yet, which is what makes it OSINT.
  3. Scanning and enumeration. Nmap port scanning plus banner grabbing, which is what reveals the outdated software version.
  4. Gaining access (exploitation). The known flaw is weaponised and a shell obtained — the perimeter is breached.
  5. Maintaining access (persistence). A scheduled job that reopens the connection is a persistence mechanism, exactly as the lecture lists (reverse shells, scheduled jobs, backdoors, C2).
  6. Covering tracks. Assessing logging effectiveness and whether forensic traces could be purged — note this is a measurement of the client's detection capability, not concealment by the tester.
  7. Reporting. Proof-of-concept evidence, business impact assessment and remediation steps — the stage's three stated outputs.

Testing approach: black box. Justification comes from event 2. The tester had to build a list of employee addresses and mail server details from public DNS records and LinkedIn — if they had been given network diagrams or credentials, that reconnaissance effort would have been unnecessary. Extensive OSINT is the signature of zero prior knowledge. Event 3 supports it: Nmap is being used to discover what is running, which a white-box tester with architecture documentation would already know. Nothing in the story indicates the client supplied credentials, which rules out gray box.

A complete answer adds the trade-off: black box gives the most realistic simulation of an external attacker, but it spends a large share of the three weeks on discovery rather than on depth. Gray box would have covered more ground; black box tells you more about what an outsider truly faces.

Why event 1 is legally indispensable. Every subsequent event in this story — scanning the IP range, exploiting the flaw, obtaining a shell, installing a persistence mechanism, attempting to alter logs — is an offence under Bangladeshi cyber law if performed without authorisation. Section 18 covers unauthorised access to a computer system; Section 32 covers hacking offences including unauthorised data deletion, modification and network intrusion, carrying up to 14 years' imprisonment or a fine up to 10,000,000 BDT; and Section 19 would apply to any damage caused to the systems in the process.

The signed rules-of-engagement document is what converts these acts from crimes into a contracted professional service. It matters in three specific ways. It provides consent from a party with authority to give it — which is why authorisation must come from the system owner, not merely from a friendly IT manager. It bounds the scope: testing outside the agreed IP ranges or outside the 01:00–05:00 window is unauthorised even though a contract exists, because consent was given only for what the document specifies. And it allocates liability if the testing causes an outage, which intrusive testing can.

The professional-ethics link, if you have time: the ACM/IEEE Software Engineering Code's Principle 2 (Client and Employer) requires engineers to use client property only with formal authorisation and consent — so pre-engagement is an ethical obligation as well as a legal shield.

Question 5 · medium

A small e-commerce company with no security budget wants to establish basic VAPT capability using free tools only. Recommend a toolchain, state which stage of testing each tool serves, and explain what capability the company will still be missing.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from The tools table (VAPT §5) mapped onto the 7-stage methodology (§4). The constraint "free tools only" is the examiner's way of testing whether you know which entries in the table are open-source. The final part returns to the VA/PT completion argument from §2 — automation is free, expertise is not.

Recommended toolchain:

  • Nmap — stages 2 and 3, reconnaissance and scanning/ enumeration. Network mapping, port scanning and OS/service detection to establish what exists and what is exposed. Free and the standard choice.
  • OpenVAS — vulnerability assessment across the infrastructure. The open-source equivalent of Nessus, suitable for scheduled internal vulnerability audits, so the company gets the continuous-coverage layer without licence cost.
  • Nikto — rapid web server configuration audits, checking for dangerous files and outdated server software. Fast, free, and directly relevant since the business is web-facing.
  • OWASP ZAP — application-layer testing. Automated DAST scanning and proxy interception for SQL injection, XSS and CSRF, which is where an e-commerce site's real exposure sits. This is the free counterpart to Burp Suite.
  • Wireshark — traffic analysis, to confirm that customer data and credentials are not travelling in cleartext anywhere in the checkout flow.

What this gives them. A genuine, continuous vulnerability assessment capability across network, host, web server and application layers, at zero licence cost. For a small company this is a real improvement — it delivers the baseline hygiene and compliance value the lecture attributes to VA, and it will catch the unpatched services, default credentials and obvious injection flaws that most opportunistic attacks rely on.

What is still missing — the important half of the answer.

  1. The penetration testing half of VAPT. Every tool listed is a scanner or an analyser. The company will have a list of findings and no proof of which are exploitable, cannot see chained attack paths, and will be prioritising by generic severity score rather than by real exposure. They are measuring exposure and will be tempted to call it risk.
  2. Manual expertise. The lecture is explicit that penetration testing is automated scripts plus manual testing. Business logic flaws — a checkout that lets you change the price in a request, or an order ID you can increment to read someone else's invoice — are invisible to every scanner because nothing is technically malformed. Only a human finds those, and on an e-commerce site they are among the most damaging.
  3. False positive triage. OpenVAS will report findings that are not real, and with no expertise to validate them the company will either waste effort or start ignoring the reports.
  4. Detection testing. No scanner assesses whether an intrusion would be noticed — that is stage 6 of the methodology and it requires an actual simulated attack.

Recommendation. Run the free toolchain continuously as the baseline, and budget for a scoped external penetration test — even a narrow, annual one limited to the checkout and authentication flows. That preserves the VA/PT pairing at the lowest viable cost: the free tools supply breadth, the paid engagement supplies proof, and the assessment output makes the paid engagement cheaper by scoping it precisely.

Topic 06

OWASP Top 10 smart contract vulnerabilities

The 2025 vs 2026 comparison. A likely table-recall question — note what changed.

OWASP is the Open Worldwide Application Security Project, which publishes ranked lists of the most critical security risks in various domains. This one covers smart contracts — the self-executing programs on a blockchain that Guide 1 introduced.

Learn the four changes, not the twenty rows

Unchanged at the top and middle: SC01 Access Control, SC06 Unchecked External Calls. Two new entries in 2026: Arithmetic Errors (SC07) and Proxy & Upgradeability Vulnerabilities (SC10). Two removed: Insecure Randomness and Denial of Service. Two elevated under new names: Business Logic Vulnerabilities to SC02 and Flash Loan-Facilitated Attacks to SC04. Everything else just shifted down to make room.

Two terms worth defining in case you need to write about them. Reentrancy is when a contract calls an external contract which calls back into the original before the first call finished, allowing the same funds to be withdrawn repeatedly. A flash loan is an uncollateralised loan that must be borrowed and repaid within a single transaction — attackers use them to temporarily command enormous capital, typically to manipulate a price oracle. Note that the 2026 elevation of both Business Logic and Flash Loan attacks tells a story: the industry's problems are shifting from low-level coding bugs toward higher-level design and economic flaws.

Topic 07

Business value and best practices

The five benefits. Short section, easy marks.

  1. Safeguard user data — protect sensitive customer records and PII from public exposure. (PII = Personally Identifiable Information.)
  2. Real-time risk visibility — quantify operational risk based on demonstrated exploit paths. Note "demonstrated" — this benefit comes specifically from the PT half.
  3. Regulatory compliance — maintain compliance with ISO 27001, PCI-DSS and HIPAA.
  4. Strengthen incident response — test blue-team detection capabilities against active simulations. (Blue team = the defenders, i.e. the SOC; red team = the simulated attackers.)
  5. Optimise security budgets — justify defensive investments using data-backed vulnerability metrics.
Cross-topic link worth making

Benefit 4 connects VAPT directly to the SOC topic: a penetration test is how you find out whether your SIEM correlation rules actually fire. And benefit 5 connects it to Risk Management: the demonstrated exploit paths supply the likelihood input to \(\text{Risk}=\text{Likelihood}\times\text{Impact}\), and the ALE calculation that follows from it. Examiners like answers that cross topics.

Question 6 · long answer · stretch

The board of a Bangladeshi commercial bank has approved budget for either a continuous vulnerability management programme or an annual penetration test, but not both. As the CISO, write the case you would put to the board. Argue for one, acknowledge what you lose, and propose how to mitigate that loss.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from A stretch question with no single correct answer — the marks are for the quality of the argument, not the choice. It draws on the whole VAPT lecture plus the business case model from the Risk Management lecture (issue → root cause → solution → effect). The instruction "acknowledge what you lose" is the examiner explicitly asking for the completion argument from §2, so a candidate who argues one side without naming the sacrificed capability cannot score well however good the case is.

Recommendation: the continuous vulnerability management programme — with a stated plan to recover the penetration testing capability by other means.

The argument. Three grounds.

Coverage against a changing estate. A bank's environment is not static: core banking systems, switching engines, customer portals and hundreds of endpoints change continuously, and new CVEs are published daily. An annual penetration test is a snapshot that begins ageing the day the report is signed. For eleven of twelve months the bank would be relying on evidence about a configuration that no longer exists. Continuous assessment maintains a current picture — and unpatched, end-of-life systems are precisely the root cause behind WannaCry and Equifax, both of which the risk management lecture treats as landmark cases.

Regulatory position. Banks operate under sustained compliance obligations. ISO 27001 and PCI-DSS expect ongoing vulnerability management, not a single annual exercise, and the cyber law lecture's due diligence obligation is relevant here: an organisation retains legal liability even when victimised, and failure to exercise reasonable cybersecurity diligence attracts civil regulatory penalties. A continuous programme is far easier to defend as reasonable diligence than one test a year.

Cost per unit of risk reduced. Automated scanning covers the entire estate at a fraction of the per-host cost of manual testing. With a single budget, breadth buys more risk reduction than depth — most successful attacks on banks exploit known, patchable weaknesses rather than novel zero-days.

What is lost — state it plainly, this is where the marks are. Choosing VA sacrifices four things.

  1. Proof of exploitability. The bank will hold a large ranked list and no evidence of which items an attacker could actually use. It will be prioritising by generic severity score rather than by real exposure, and some remediation effort will be spent on findings that are unreachable in practice.
  2. Chained attack paths. Scanners cannot see how several medium-rated findings combine. For a bank this is the most serious loss, because compromise of a core banking system typically comes from a chain rather than from one catastrophic flaw.
  3. Blue-team validation. No scan tests whether the SOC would detect an intrusion. The bank will not know whether its correlation rules fire, which is the benefit "strengthen incident response".
  4. Business logic flaws. Invisible to automation entirely — a transaction flow that can be manipulated is not technically malformed, so nothing flags it.

How to mitigate the loss without the second budget line.

  • Build limited internal testing capability. The SOC's Tier 3 analysts already have vulnerability assessment and penetration testing within their stated responsibilities. Using existing staff and free tools — Nmap, OWASP ZAP, Burp Suite Community — recovers some validation capability at no new licence cost.
  • Use purple-team exercises. Have Tier 3 run controlled attack simulations against the SOC's own detection rules. This addresses the blue-team validation gap directly and is a staffing decision rather than a procurement one.
  • Scope a very small external test. Rather than a full annual engagement, commission a narrow gray-box test of the two highest-value flows only — internet banking authentication and payment initiation. Because the vulnerability programme has already identified the targets, the scoping is precise and the engagement is a fraction of full cost.
  • Prioritise by exposure, not by score. Combine scanner output with the asset inventory and criticality ratings from the risk management lecture, so that a critical CVE on an internet-facing core banking component outranks the same CVE on an isolated internal host. This partially substitutes human judgement for the exploitability evidence a pen test would have supplied.

Closing statement to the board. "Continuous vulnerability management is the right first investment because it addresses the failure mode that actually breaches banks — known, unpatched weaknesses across a changing estate — and it satisfies our ongoing regulatory obligations. But I want to record that it measures our exposure, not our risk. Until we can validate exploitability, we will be prioritising on inference rather than evidence, and we will not know whether our SOC would detect a real intrusion. I am proposing internal testing and a tightly scoped external engagement to close that gap, and I will return to the board with the cost."

Marking tip

Arguing the other way is equally acceptable — a bank that already has mature patching but has never been tested might reasonably choose the pen test. What is not acceptable is choosing either without naming the sacrificed capability. The examiner is testing whether you internalised "VA measures exposure, PT measures risk", so make sure that idea appears explicitly.

Last-minute revision — VAPT

Definition

End-to-end evaluation of defensive posture combining automated vulnerability discovery with simulated adversarial exploitation.

The one axis

VA = broad, shallow, automated, continuous, cheap. PT = narrow, deep, manual, periodic, expensive.

The killer sentence

VA measures exposure. PT measures risk. Use it in any comparison question.

Two questions

VA: "what is weak or unpatched?" PT: "can it be exploited to gain deep access?"

5 scan types

Network · Host · Wireless · Application · Database. Different vantage points, not alternatives.

SAST vs DAST

Static = still (source code, not running). Dynamic = running (live app, real time).

7 PT stages

Pre-engagement → Recon (OSINT) → Scanning/Enumeration → Gaining Access → Maintaining Access → Covering Tracks → Reporting.

Stage 1 matters

Without written authorisation it is a crime under DSA s.18 and s.32. Always mention it.

Stage 6 misread

Covering tracks = testing whether the SOC logged it, not the tester hiding.

3 approaches

Black = zero knowledge (outsider). Gray = partial, low-priv creds (insider/foothold). White = full source + docs.

Host discovery

ARP for the local subnet · ICMP echo for wider ranges.

Tools by stage

Discovery: Nmap. VA: Nessus, OpenVAS, Nikto. Web PT: Burp Suite, OWASP ZAP. Packets: Wireshark.

Free equivalents

Nessus → OpenVAS. Burp Suite → OWASP ZAP.

OWASP 2026 changes

NEW: Arithmetic Errors (SC07), Proxy & Upgradeability (SC10). REMOVED: Insecure Randomness, DoS. SC01 Access Control unchanged.

5 benefits

Safeguard data · real-time risk visibility · compliance (ISO 27001, PCI-DSS, HIPAA) · strengthen IR · optimise budgets.

What VA can't do

Prove exploitability · chain findings · assess business impact · test the defenders · avoid false positives.
