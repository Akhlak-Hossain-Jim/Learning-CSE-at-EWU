Foundations · 10 min
Where does the power actually go?
Breaking down power consumption inside a computer
⏱ 10 minCO1
▼

Before we can minimize power, we need to know where it goes. In any computing device — from a smartphone to a data center server — power is consumed by several major components, each with very different behavior.

35%
CPU/SoC — the single largest consumer in most systems
20%
Memory (DRAM) — power scales with capacity & refresh rate
15%
Storage — varies hugely: SSD vs spinning HDD
15%
Display — dominant in laptops & phones, esp. backlit LCDs
10%
Network/radio — WiFi, cellular, Bluetooth modules
5%
Power conversion losses — AC/DC, voltage regulators
📘 From Velete Ch.3
Power consumption splits into two categories: static power (leakage current — consumed even when a circuit is idle, due to imperfect insulation in transistors) and dynamic power (consumed when transistors actively switch state). As chips have shrunk to nanometer scales, static/leakage power has grown to be a significant fraction of total consumption — which is why idle states matter so much.

A critical concept: power (Watts) is the rate of energy use, while energy (Joules, or kWh) is power × time. A device drawing 10W for 1 hour uses the same energy as one drawing 5W for 2 hours. This distinction matters because some power-saving techniques reduce instantaneous power but increase the time to complete a task — the net energy savings depend on which effect dominates.

⚡ Worked example
A laptop CPU at full performance (15W) completes a video encode in 10 minutes = 2.5 Wh of energy. The same task at half power (8W, but takes 22 minutes due to lower clock speed) = 2.93 Wh. The "slower" mode actually used more total energy — because static/leakage power was drawn for longer. This is the foundation of the "race to idle" principle we'll explore next.

Core Content · 15 min
CPU power management — DVFS & C-states
The two pillars of modern processor power management
⏱ 15 minCO1
▼

Modern CPUs use two complementary techniques to manage power: Dynamic Voltage and Frequency Scaling (DVFS) for when the CPU is actively working at varying intensity, and C-states (idle states) for when the CPU has nothing to do.

💡 Key insight — voltage matters MORE than frequency
Because power scales with voltage squared but only linearly with frequency, reducing voltage gives a quadratic saving while reducing frequency gives only a linear one. A 10% voltage cut saves ~19% power; a 10% frequency cut saves only ~10%. This is why modern CPUs aggressively pair lower frequencies with lower voltages (DVFS) rather than just throttling frequency alone.

When the CPU has nothing to do, DVFS isn't enough — even at minimum frequency, leakage current wastes power. This is where C-states (CPU idle states) come in, defined by the ACPI (Advanced Configuration and Power Interface) standard. Explore each state below:

⚡
C0 — Operating State
Power: 100% (reference)
The CPU is actively executing instructions. This is the only state where useful work happens. DVFS operates within C0 — the CPU can run at different voltage/frequency points (P-states: P0 = max performance, Pn = min performance) while still in C0.
😴
C1 — Halt
Power: ~70% · Wake latency: ~1ns
The CPU stops executing instructions but maintains full context (caches, registers stay powered). Extremely fast to wake — essentially free to enter/exit. Used constantly between instructions even during "active" workloads.
🛌
C2 — Stop Clock
Power: ~50% · Wake latency: ~50ns
Internal clocks are stopped, but caches remain coherent. Slightly deeper sleep than C1, with marginally more power saving and marginally higher wake latency. The exact behavior varies by CPU vendor.
💤
C3 — Sleep
Power: ~30% · Wake latency: ~100ns
All internal clocks are stopped and the CPU flushes its caches to maintain coherency externally. Significant power savings, but waking up requires cache reload — affecting performance if entered/exited too frequently.
🥶
C6 — Deep Power Down
Power: ~5–10% · Wake latency: ~100µs
The CPU core's voltage is reduced to near-zero (or fully removed) — core state is saved to a retention register or last-level cache before power-down. Mobile and laptop CPUs spend most idle time here. Wake latency is high enough that the OS scheduler must predict idle duration accurately.
❄️
C7 / C8–C10 — Package Sleep States
Power: <5% · Wake latency: ~1ms+
Deepest states — power down shared resources across the entire CPU package (all cores, last-level cache, memory controller, I/O). Used in mobile SoCs during screen-off "Doze" periods. Highest power savings but highest wake latency, so the OS only enters these for extended idle periods.

Concept Deep-Dive · 15 min
Energy-proportional computing
The gap between "ideal" and "real" servers — and why it's the central challenge of Green IT
⏱ 15 minCO1
▼

An energy-proportional system consumes power exactly proportional to the amount of useful work it does: 0% utilization = 0% power, 100% utilization = 100% power, 50% utilization = 50% power. No real system achieves this perfectly — but understanding the gap is essential.

📗 From Lamb Ch.2
The formula used above approximates real servers as: Power = 50% (idle floor) + 50% × utilization. This reflects the fact that fans, memory, motherboard, power supplies, and baseline CPU circuits all draw power regardless of load. Google's research (Barroso & Hölzle, 2007) first popularized this concept and showed most data center servers operate at 10-50% utilization — squarely in the most wasteful zone of this curve.

Two strategies address this gap:

📦 Strategy 1 — Consolidation

Instead of running many servers at 10-20% utilization (each paying the "idle floor" cost), consolidate workloads via virtualization onto fewer servers running at 60-80% utilization. Fewer servers paying the idle floor = less total waste. This is the single most impactful Green IT lever in most data centers.

🔁 Strategy 2 — Race to idle

For battery-powered devices, complete tasks as fast as possible (high power, short time) then enter deep sleep (C6+), rather than running slowly for longer (moderate power, long time). Because idle states have near-zero power, finishing fast and sleeping long usually wins — unless the task is so short that wake/sleep transition overhead dominates.

Applied · 10 min
Power management beyond the CPU
Displays, storage, networking, and mobile-specific techniques
⏱ 10 minCO1
▼

The CPU isn't the whole story. In laptops and phones, the display often dominates; in storage systems, the difference between technologies is enormous; and in mobile devices, software-level wake management is critical.

🖥️ Displays
💾 Storage
📡 Networking
📱 Mobile OS
Displays
Backlit vs. self-emissive screens
Often 15-40% of total device power
LCD (backlit)
A backlight illuminates the entire panel uniformly — power draw is roughly constant regardless of content. Brightness is the dominant control: 50% brightness ≈ 50% backlight power.
OLED/AMOLED
Each pixel emits its own light — power scales with content. A black background can use 3-5× less power than white. "Dark mode" is a genuine, measurable power-saving feature on OLED screens (not on LCDs).
Refresh rate
Higher refresh rates (90Hz, 120Hz) increase power draw roughly proportionally. Adaptive refresh rate (dropping to 1Hz for static content) is now standard in efficient devices.
Engineering takeaway
For OLED devices, UI/UX designers choosing dark themes have a direct, measurable impact on battery life — a rare case where design aesthetics and energy efficiency align perfectly.
Storage
HDD vs. SSD power profiles
10-15% of system power, but highly variable
HDD (spinning disk)
Mechanical platters spin continuously (~5-10W active). Spin-down saves power but adds 2-5 second wake latency — frequent spin-down/up cycles can shorten drive lifespan and sometimes use more energy than staying spun up.
SSD (flash)
No moving parts. Active power ~2-5W, idle power can drop to <0.1W almost instantly with no wake latency penalty. SSDs are dramatically more energy-proportional than HDDs.
Storage tiering
In data centers: hot data on SSD (frequently accessed, fast, power-hungry), cold/archival data on HDD or tape (rarely accessed, can be spun down or powered off entirely).
Engineering takeaway
SSD migration is one of the simplest "free" efficiency wins in both laptops and servers — improving performance AND reducing power simultaneously.
Networking
Radio & network interface power
WiFi, Bluetooth, cellular radios
Radio power states
Radios have their own idle/active states. Maintaining a WiFi/cellular connection (even idle) consumes far more power than the radio being fully off — this is why airplane mode dramatically extends battery life.
Polling vs push
An app that polls a server every 30 seconds keeps the radio awake constantly. Push notifications let the radio sleep until a message arrives — potentially 10× more efficient for the same "freshness".
Wake-on-LAN
In data centers and offices, idle machines can fully power down while a low-power network interface listens for a "wake" packet — enabling near-zero idle power with instant-on capability.
Energy-proportional networking
Network switches and links can scale their speed (and power) with traffic load — putting unused ports/links to sleep during low-traffic periods.
Mobile OS
Software-managed power on Android/iOS
Where engineering choices meet user experience
Wake locks
An app can request a "wake lock" to prevent the CPU from sleeping (e.g., during a download). Poorly released wake locks are one of the most common causes of battery drain in badly-written apps.
Doze mode (Android)
When the screen is off and the device is stationary, Android defers background network access and CPU-intensive jobs to periodic "maintenance windows" — batching work to allow longer sleep periods.
App Standby Buckets
Android categorizes apps by usage pattern (active, working set, frequent, rare) and restricts background activity accordingly — rarely-used apps get severely limited background access.
Engineering takeaway
Green mobile development isn't just about algorithms — it's about respecting OS power management APIs, batching network calls, and avoiding unnecessary background services.

Practical · 10 min
The engineer's power-minimization toolkit
Concrete techniques you can apply at every layer of the stack
⏱ 10 minCO1
▼

Today's concepts translate into concrete techniques. Here's a checklist organized by where you sit in the stack:

  • 💻
    Application developers — Avoid busy-wait loops (use event-driven/interrupt-based design). Batch network requests instead of frequent polling. Release wake locks promptly. Use dark UI themes for OLED devices. Profile and eliminate CPU-hot code paths.
  • ⚙️
    Systems/OS engineers — Enable and tune CPU governors (e.g., Linux "powersave", "ondemand", "schedutil"). Configure ACPI C-state limits appropriately — too aggressive can hurt latency-sensitive workloads. Use SSDs for OS and frequently-accessed data.
  • 🏗️
    Infrastructure/data center engineers — Consolidate workloads via virtualization to push utilization toward the efficient zone (60-80%). Implement workload scheduling that "packs" jobs onto fewer servers, allowing others to fully power down.
  • 📡
    IoT/embedded engineers — Design firmware around deep sleep states (C6+/equivalent). Use interrupt-driven wake rather than polling sensors continuously. Choose radio protocols (BLE, LoRa) suited to the duty cycle required.
  • 📊
    Everyone — Measure before and after. Use tools like Intel Power Gadget, powertop (Linux), or battery historian (Android) to verify that "optimizations" actually reduce energy — remember the race-to-idle example where slower wasn't greener.
Code review scenario
You review code with: while(true) { checkStatus(); sleep(100ms); } running continuously. What's the green computing concern, and what's a better pattern?
Tap to see answer
Answer
This polling loop prevents the CPU from entering deep C-states every 100ms — keeping it effectively always "awake". Better: use an event/callback or interrupt-driven mechanism (e.g., file system watchers, message queues, or OS-level notification APIs) so the CPU can sleep until something actually changes.
Architecture scenario
A company runs 200 servers at 15% average utilization. Leadership wants to "go green" by buying more energy-efficient (Energy Star) servers to replace them 1-for-1. Good idea?
Tap to see answer
Answer
Buying new efficient hardware helps marginally, but the bigger problem is utilization — 200 servers at 15% means ~85% of capacity (and the "idle floor" power) is wasted regardless of hardware efficiency. Consolidating via virtualization to ~50 servers at 60% utilization would save far more energy than a 1-for-1 hardware refresh, at a fraction of the capital cost.
Mobile dev scenario
A weather app updates location every 10 seconds "for accuracy," even when the screen is off. Users report poor battery life. What's the fix?
Tap to see answer
Answer
This is a wake-lock / Doze-mode violation — 10-second GPS polling with the screen off keeps the radio and GPS chip continuously active, preventing deep sleep. Fix: use significant-location-change APIs (which use cell tower/WiFi triangulation, far lower power) and respect Doze mode by deferring to maintenance windows when the screen is off.
