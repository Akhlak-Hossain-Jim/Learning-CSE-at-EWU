Foundation · 12 min
What is Business Process Reengineering?
From Hammer & Champy to the sustainability lens
⏱ 12 minCO2
▼

Business Process Reengineering (BPR) is the fundamental rethinking and radical redesign of business processes to achieve dramatic improvements in critical measures of performance — cost, quality, service, and speed. The concept was popularized by Michael Hammer and James Champy in their 1993 book "Reengineering the Corporation."

📘 From Velete Ch.5 — BPR vs. incremental improvement
Traditional process improvement asks "how can we do this faster?" BPR asks "why are we doing this at all?" It questions the existence of a step entirely, not just its efficiency. This distinction matters enormously for sustainability: optimizing a wasteful process (e.g., faster paper approval routing) still leaves the waste in place. BPR asks whether the paper-based approval should exist at all.

When BPR is applied with a sustainability lens, the guiding questions expand beyond cost and speed to include environmental impact:

🏢 Traditional BPR questions
  • Can this step be eliminated entirely?
  • Can steps be combined or run in parallel?
  • Can technology replace manual work?
  • Can decision-making be pushed closer to the point of work?
🌿 Sustainability-lens BPR questions
  • Does this step consume physical resources (paper, travel, energy) unnecessarily?
  • Can digital alternatives eliminate material/energy waste?
  • Does this process generate waste that could be designed out?
  • What is the carbon/resource footprint of each process step?

Core Method · 18 min
The AS-IS / TO-BE methodology
Mapping current reality, then designing the sustainable future state
⏱ 18 minCO2
▼

Every BPR project follows the same fundamental structure: document the AS-IS (current state) process in detail, identify waste and inefficiency, then design the TO-BE (future state) process that eliminates that waste. This is also exactly the structure of your upcoming Case Study 1 assignment.

💡 Key insight
Notice that the TO-BE redesign didn't just digitize the AS-IS steps one-for-one — it eliminated the physical walking, the manual re-entry, and the long-term physical storage entirely. This is the difference between digitization (same process, digital tools) and true reengineering (redesigned process). True BPR usually removes 1-2 entire steps, not just speeds up existing ones.

Tool Skills · 12 min
BPMN 2.0 notation basics
The standard visual language for documenting business processes
⏱ 12 minCO2
▼

To map AS-IS and TO-BE processes professionally — as you'll need to for Case Study 1 — you need a standard notation. BPMN (Business Process Model and Notation) 2.0 is the most widely used standard globally, maintained by the Object Management Group (OMG). Tap each symbol below to learn what it means.

Start Event
Task / Activity
Gateway (Decision)
End Event
Swimlane (Role)
Data Object
Start Event (thin circle). Marks where a process begins — e.g., "Expense incurred" or "Customer places order." Every BPMN diagram needs exactly one (or more) start events to show entry points into the process.
Task / Activity (rounded rectangle). Represents a single unit of work performed by a person or system — e.g., "Fill expense form" or "Approve request." The most common element in any process map. In sustainability BPR, each task should be annotated with its resource footprint (paper, energy, travel).
Gateway / Decision (diamond). Represents a branch point where the process splits based on a condition — e.g., "Amount > $500?" leading to different approval paths. Critical for identifying where unnecessary complexity or redundant approval layers exist.
End Event (thick circle). Marks where a process concludes — e.g., "Reimbursement paid" or "Order fulfilled." A process can have multiple end events for different outcomes (approved/rejected).
Swimlane (horizontal/vertical band). Groups process steps by the role or department responsible — e.g., "Employee," "Department Head," "Finance." Swimlanes immediately reveal handoffs between people/systems — and every handoff is a potential point of delay, error, or waste in a sustainability audit.
Data Object (document icon). Represents information or a physical artifact flowing through the process — e.g., "Paper expense form" or "Digital approval record." In green BPR, distinguishing physical data objects (paper) from digital ones is the whole point — every physical data object is a sustainability target for elimination.
📝 For your Case Study 1
You will be required to map AS-IS and TO-BE models using BPMN 2.0, ArchiMate, or TOGAF (your choice). Free tools: Bizagi Modeler, Camunda Modeler, or even draw.io / Lucidchart with a BPMN shape library all work well for student projects. Annotate each task with its estimated resource footprint — this is what makes it a sustainability-focused BPR rather than a generic process map.

Applied Method · 15 min
Auditing processes for sustainability waste
A practical checklist — and a calculator for paper vs. digital impact
⏱ 15 minCO2
▼

Lean manufacturing identifies "7 wastes" (TIMWOOD). Sustainability-focused BPR adapts this lens specifically to environmental waste in business processes. Use this framework when analyzing any process:

📄 Material waste

Paper, printed forms, physical signatures, photocopies, physical mail/courier. Each represents both a direct material cost AND embodied carbon (paper production, ink, transport).

🚗 Transport/travel waste

Physical movement of documents or people between locations when digital transfer would suffice — internal mail runs, in-person meetings replaceable by video calls, physical document delivery.

⏳ Waiting/storage waste

Documents sitting in physical inboxes or filing cabinets, energy-consuming idle equipment waiting for the next process step, physical archive storage requiring climate control.

🔁 Redundancy waste

Re-entering the same data multiple times across systems, duplicate approvals, printing a document to read it then digitizing it again — energy and material spent on work that adds no value.

📗 From Lamb Ch.3
One sheet of A4 paper has an embodied footprint of roughly 4.7g CO₂e (production + transport), and approximately 8,333 sheets come from a single mature tree's pulp equivalent. These numbers seem small per-sheet, but at organizational scale — thousands of forms monthly — the cumulative impact becomes significant, and entirely avoidable through process redesign.

Real-world BPR sustainability case examples
Flip each card to see the redesign and its measured impact
⏱ 10 minCO2
▼

These examples illustrate sustainability-focused BPR in different sectors — to give you a sense of the range of processes you might select for your own Case Study 1 assignment.

Banking sector
A bank's loan approval process required 12 physical signatures across 4 departments, taking 3 weeks. What was the sustainability-focused redesign?
Tap to see answer
Redesign & impact
Digital signature workflow with parallel (not sequential) approval routing. Result: 3 weeks → 3 days, paper use down 95%, courier trips between branches eliminated entirely (reducing transport emissions). The parallel routing alone — independent of digitization — cut cycle time in half.
University admin
A university's course registration required students to physically queue at 5 different offices to collect signatures. What changed?
Tap to see answer
Redesign & impact
Centralized online portal with automated eligibility checks replacing manual signature verification. Eliminated thousands of student-hours of physical queuing (and associated campus energy use for waiting areas), removed ~15,000 sheets of paper per semester, and cut registration time from 2 days to 20 minutes.
Manufacturing supply chain
A garment factory's quality inspection process required physical samples shipped to a central lab 200km away for every batch. What was the redesign?
Tap to see answer
Redesign & impact
Remote visual inspection via high-resolution imaging and IoT sensors, with physical sampling only for statistically-flagged anomalies. Reduced courier trips by 80%, cutting transport emissions and turnaround time from 5 days to same-day — directly relevant to Bangladesh's large garment export sector.
