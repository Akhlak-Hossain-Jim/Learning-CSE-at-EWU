Welcome to CSE407 Green Computing. This is not just another elective — it may be one of the most practically relevant courses in your degree. The systems you will build as engineers will consume real energy, produce real carbon emissions, and generate real e-waste. This course teaches you how to think about, measure, and minimize that impact.

Today's Learning Goals
After this class you should be able to: (1) explain why IT energy consumption is a global problem, (2) describe the two-track structure of green computing, (3) define key terms like PUE and carbon footprint, and (4) identify concrete actions engineers can take.

The course runs across 22 classes and is structured in two complementary tracks. We use two main textbooks — Velete's Green IT and Lamb's The Greening of IT. Today we cover Velete Chapter 1 and Lamb Chapters 1 and 2 (pages 15–30).

IT's growing energy problem
From idle laptops to hyperscale data centers — where the energy goes
⏱ 20 min CO1

Computing has become one of the world's largest energy consumers — yet most of that energy is wasted. Not because computers are necessarily inefficient by design, but because of how they are deployed, configured, and managed.

📖 From Velete Ch.1
Computing power doubles roughly every two years (Moore's Law) — but energy efficiency has not kept the same pace. The result: each generation of hardware is faster, but data centers collectively consume more energy than ever because we deploy so many more of them.

Let's look at where energy is consumed across the IT stack:

End-user devices
Smartphones, laptops, desktops, TVs, routers. Billions of units each consuming 5W–400W. Mostly idle but rarely fully powered down. Collectively enormous.
Network infrastructure
Routers, switches, fiber repeaters, mobile base stations. Always-on, globally distributed. The internet's backbone uses roughly 250 TWh/year.
Data centers
Servers, storage, cooling, UPS, lighting. PUE averages 1.58 globally — meaning for every 1W of compute, 0.58W is wasted on overhead. Best-in-class (Google, Microsoft) achieve 1.1–1.2.
Emerging: AI & crypto
Training a single large AI model (like GPT-4) can emit as much CO₂ as 5 cars over their full lifetime. Bitcoin mining uses ~150 TWh/year — more than Argentina.

Course Structure · 15 min
The two tracks of green computing
How this course is organized — and why both tracks matter equally
⏱ 15 min CO1–CO3
▼

Green computing isn't a single discipline — it splits into two complementary approaches. Understanding both is essential because the biggest sustainability gains come from combining them.

⚡ Track 1 — Energy-efficient computing
🌍 Track 2 — Computing for sustainability

Making computers themselves use less energy. This is about the hardware, software, and infrastructure of IT systems — reducing the energy they consume while maintaining or improving performance.

💻
CPU power management
Dynamic voltage scaling, sleep states, turbo boost management
🏗️
Data center cooling
Hot/cold aisle containment, liquid cooling, free cooling
☁️
Virtualization
Consolidating workloads onto fewer physical servers
📡
Network efficiency
Energy-proportional networking, sleep on idle links
💾
Storage tiering
Moving cold data to low-power storage automatically
🔋
Mobile optimization
Reducing app battery drain, efficient wake locks

Using computers to help the world use less energy. This is about leveraging IT as a tool — building systems that monitor, model, and reduce energy consumption in buildings, cities, industry, and everyday life.

🏢
Smart buildings
Automated lighting, HVAC, and occupancy-based control
⚡
Smart grids
Demand-response systems, renewable integration
📄
Paperless workflows
Eliminating print, physical mail, paper-based processes
🏭
Industrial IoT
Real-time monitoring of factory energy consumption
🚗
Smart transport
Traffic optimization, EV charging coordination
🌾
Precision agriculture
Sensor-driven irrigation, reducing water and fertilizer waste
Key insight: These tracks compound. A Track 1 improvement (more efficient server) deployed in a Track 2 application (smart building management) multiplies the impact. The most impactful engineers understand both.

PUE — Power Usage Effectiveness. The ratio of total data center energy to energy used by IT equipment only. Formula: PUE = Total facility power ÷ IT equipment power. PUE = 1.0 is theoretically perfect (zero overhead). Industry average is ~1.58. World-class is 1.1–1.2. Used to benchmark and compare data center efficiency.
Carbon Footprint. The total greenhouse gas emissions caused directly or indirectly by an individual, organization, event, or product — expressed in CO₂-equivalent (CO₂e). For IT systems this includes manufacturing emissions (embodied carbon), operational electricity, and end-of-life disposal.
E-waste (Electronic waste). Discarded electrical or electronic devices — including phones, computers, servers, batteries, and peripherals. One of the world's fastest-growing waste streams (~57 million tonnes/year). Contains toxic materials (lead, mercury, cadmium) but also valuable recoverable metals (gold, copper, rare earths).
Virtualization. Technology that allows multiple virtual machines (VMs) to run on a single physical server simultaneously. Increases utilization from typical 10–20% to 60–80%, drastically cutting the number of physical servers needed — and therefore the energy consumed by idle hardware.
ESG — Environmental, Social, and Governance. A framework investors and regulators use to evaluate how companies manage sustainability risks and opportunities. The "E" in ESG covers carbon emissions, energy efficiency, e-waste, and resource use. Companies with poor ESG scores increasingly face higher capital costs and reputational risk.
Lifecycle Assessment (LCA). A methodology for assessing the total environmental impact of a product "from cradle to grave" — including raw material extraction, manufacturing, use, and disposal. For IT equipment, manufacturing often accounts for 70–80% of the total carbon footprint, not the use phase.
Renewable Energy. Energy from naturally replenishing sources — solar, wind, hydro, geothermal. Major data center operators (Google, Microsoft, Amazon) have committed to 100% renewable energy. In Bangladesh, renewables are expanding via SREDA (Sustainable and Renewable Energy Development Authority).
Scope 1/2/3 Emissions. Scope 1: direct emissions (fuel burned on-site). Scope 2: indirect from purchased electricity. Scope 3: all other indirect emissions (supply chain, product use, employee travel). For a tech company, Scope 3 — including hardware manufacturing and user devices — often dwarfs Scope 1+2 combined.

Context · 10 min
Why organizations adopt Green IT
The four forces making sustainability a business priority
⏱ 10 min CO2
▼

Green IT adoption is rarely driven by altruism alone. Four converging forces are making it economically and strategically unavoidable for organizations:

💰 Cost savings

Energy is a top-3 operational cost for any data center. A 10% reduction in PUE for a 10 MW facility saves ~$800K/year at $0.10/kWh. Green IT pays for itself.

⚖️ Regulation

EU Energy Efficiency Directive, EPA Energy Star, ISO 14001, and local regulations mandate reporting and improvement. Non-compliance means fines and reputational damage.

📈 ESG pressure

Institutional investors (BlackRock, Vanguard) now screen on ESG metrics. Companies with poor sustainability scores face higher cost of capital and exclusion from indices.

🔌 Energy scarcity

Grid instability and energy shortages in many markets make efficient systems more resilient. A system needing half the power can stay online when others can't.

👥 Talent & brand

Engineers increasingly choose employers based on sustainability commitments. Companies like Patagonia and Apple attract top talent partly through genuine green commitments.

⚠️ Risk management

Climate change creates physical risks (flooding data centers, heat waves disrupting cooling). Sustainable infrastructure is lower-risk infrastructure over a 20-year horizon.

Cost scenario
A bank runs 500 servers at 8% average utilization. What's the most impactful green IT action?
Tap to see answer
Answer
Consolidate via virtualization. Running 500 servers at 8% = enormous waste. Consolidating to 80 servers at 50% utilization cuts power, cooling, and floor space costs by ~84% — saving millions annually.
Regulation scenario
A Dhaka startup's EU client demands a sustainability report. They've never measured their carbon footprint. Where do they start?
Tap to see answer
Answer
Start with Scope 2 — measure electricity consumption and convert using Bangladesh's grid emission factor (~0.6 kg CO₂/kWh). Then Scope 3 for hardware manufacturing. Tools: GHG Protocol, AWS Carbon Footprint Tool.
Engineering scenario
A mobile app's background sync is draining user batteries 30% faster. What green computing principle applies?
Tap to see answer
Answer
Energy-proportional computing — a device should consume energy proportional to its useful work. Batching syncs, using push instead of polling, and respecting Doze mode are all Track 1 green engineering practices.

Local Relevance · 5 min
Green IT in the Bangladesh context
Why this matters especially for engineers here
⏱ 5 min CO2
▼

Green computing is not just a concern for Silicon Valley hyperscalers. For Bangladesh — and for you as engineers working here — it is directly and immediately relevant.

🔌 Grid constraints

Bangladesh's power grid faces load shedding, generation shortfalls, and distribution losses. Efficient systems reduce demand on a constrained grid — keeping your services running when others go down.

🌡️ Climate vulnerability

Bangladesh is among the world's most climate-vulnerable nations. Flooding, heat, and cyclones already threaten infrastructure. Engineers here have extra motivation to reduce IT's contribution to climate change.

📱 Digital Bangladesh

The government's Digital Bangladesh initiative is rapidly expanding IT infrastructure. Designing it green from the start — rather than retrofitting later — is vastly more cost-effective.

🏭 IT export sector

Bangladesh's IT/ITES export sector serves EU and US clients who increasingly require suppliers to meet sustainability standards. Green IT is becoming a market-entry requirement.

💡 Opportunity
SREDA (Sustainable and Renewable Energy Development Authority) is actively building Bangladesh's renewable energy capacity. Engineers with green IT knowledge are positioned to work at the intersection of the digital and energy transitions — one of the most in-demand skill combinations of the next decade.

Practical Application · 10 min
The engineer's role in Green IT
Every specialization has a green lever — what's yours?
⏱ 10 min CO1
▼

Engineers are the ones who design, build, and operate the systems consuming all this energy. Every architectural decision has an energy consequence — and every engineer has levers to pull.

  • 💻
    Software engineer — Write efficient algorithms (O(n) vs O(n²) at scale can mean megawatts). Avoid polling; use event-driven architecture. Reduce dependency bloat. Profile and fix CPU-hot paths. Green code is often faster code.
  • 🖥️
    Systems / infrastructure engineer — Right-size hardware. Enable power management (C-states, P-states). Use IPMI/DCIM tools to monitor real consumption. Consolidate underutilized servers before buying new ones.
  • 🏗️
    Data center engineer — Target PUE ≤ 1.3. Use hot/cold aisle containment. Consider free cooling when outside air is ≤18°C. Optimize UPS efficiency curves. Choose high-efficiency PDUs.
  • 📡
    IoT / embedded engineer — Design for microwatt budgets. Use energy harvesting (solar, RF, vibration) where possible. Duty-cycle radios aggressively. Choose low-power MCUs (ARM Cortex-M0+, RISC-V).
  • ☁️
    Cloud architect — Schedule batch workloads in low-carbon grid regions. Use spot/preemptible instances. Autoscale to zero. Choose regions powered by renewables (AWS eu-north-1, GCP europe-north1).
  • 📊
    Business analyst / IT manager — Build the business case for Green IT investments. Track energy KPIs alongside performance KPIs. Procure Energy Star equipment. Implement device lifecycle policies.
📗 From Velete Ch.1 — Top-down AND bottom-up
Sustainable IT transformation requires leadership commitment (top-down) AND engineering practice (bottom-up). A CEO can mandate carbon neutrality, but it's the engineers who actually achieve it. Neither alone is sufficient — which is why this course bridges both perspectives.
