⚡ The problem

IT consumes ~2–4% of global electricity. Data centers, networks, and devices are all growing faster than the grid can supply cleanly.

🗺️ Two tracks

Track 1: make IT use less energy. Track 2: use IT to make the world more sustainable. Both needed, both complement each other.

📐 PUE reminder

PUE = Total facility power ÷ IT equipment power. Perfect = 1.0. Industry average = 1.58. World-class = 1.1–1.2.

Foundation · 10 min
Why governance & regulation matter
The case for external rules when market incentives fall short
⏱ 10 minCO1
▼

In Class 1 we saw that Green IT saves money — so why don't organizations do it automatically? The answer reveals why governance and regulation exist in the first place.

📗 Lamb Ch.3 — The market failure argument
Energy costs are often shared (building-wide electricity bills) rather than charged to the team that wastes it. When the person making the decision doesn't bear the cost, they under-invest in efficiency. Regulation corrects this market failure by making the cost explicit and mandatory.

There are three fundamental reasons why voluntary action alone is insufficient:

  • 🏦
    Short-termism — Organizations optimize for quarterly results. A $500K energy efficiency investment with a 3-year payback loses to a $500K marketing campaign with a 3-month payback — unless rules force a longer horizon.
  • 🎮
    Free-rider problem — If competitors don't invest in green IT, you're at a cost disadvantage if you do. Regulation levels the playing field so nobody gains by cutting corners on sustainability.
  • 🌍
    Externalities — CO₂ emissions harm everyone, not just the emitter. A factory or data center doesn't pay for climate damage unless forced to. Carbon pricing and efficiency standards internalize these costs.
  • 📊
    Information asymmetry — Consumers and investors can't assess a company's true environmental performance without standardized reporting. Governance mandates disclosure, enabling better market decisions.
💡 Key distinction
Governance = the internal systems, policies, and processes an organization uses to manage its environmental impact (voluntary). Regulation = externally imposed legal requirements with penalties for non-compliance (mandatory). Both matter — governance shapes culture, regulation enforces minimums.

ISO 14001
Environmental Management Systems
International Organization for Standardization (ISO) · Voluntary certification

The world's most widely adopted environmental management standard. It doesn't specify environmental performance targets — instead it provides a framework for organizations to build systematic processes for identifying, managing, and improving their environmental impact. Over 300,000 organizations in 171 countries are certified.

What it requires
Identify environmental aspects, set objectives, implement controls, monitor, audit, and continuously improve. PDCA (Plan-Do-Check-Act) cycle.
IT relevance
Data centers, e-waste disposal, energy monitoring, supplier assessment. Often required for ISO 14001 certification of the parent organization.
Who needs it
Any organization wanting to demonstrate environmental commitment to clients, investors, or government. Often required in EU/UK public sector procurement.
Penalty for non-compliance
None (voluntary) — but loss of certification can mean loss of contracts. Many global clients require it of suppliers.
Energy Star
Energy Efficiency Labeling Program
US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) · Voluntary certification

A labeling program that identifies energy-efficient products. Equipment earns the Energy Star label by meeting EPA performance standards — typically the top 25% of energy efficiency in its category. It covers computers, monitors, servers, storage, network equipment, and data centers.

For hardware
Processors, laptops, desktops, monitors, servers. Energy Star computers use 30–65% less energy than standard models.
For data centers
Energy Star for Data Centers requires PUE reporting and achieving top 25% efficiency. EPA provides a benchmarking tool.
Procurement impact
US federal agencies are legally required to purchase Energy Star products. Many corporations and universities follow the same policy.
Bangladesh relevance
Not legally binding locally, but EU/US clients may require it of their suppliers. Increasingly referenced in government ICT procurement policies.
GHG Protocol
Greenhouse Gas Protocol
WRI & WBCSD · Most widely used emissions accounting standard

The GHG Protocol provides the accounting framework for nearly every corporate and government carbon footprint measurement. It defines Scope 1, 2, and 3 emissions — a classification system every engineer working in sustainability needs to understand deeply.

Scope 1 (direct)
Emissions from sources owned or controlled by the company. E.g., diesel generators in your data center, company vehicles.
Scope 2 (indirect — energy)
Emissions from purchased electricity, heat, or steam. For most IT organizations, this is the largest scope. Measured by electricity consumption × grid emission factor.
Scope 3 (value chain)
All other indirect emissions: manufacturing of hardware, employee commuting, business travel, use of sold products. Often 70–85% of a tech company's total footprint.
Why engineers need this
Scope 3 includes your users' devices and your hardware suppliers. Engineers who specify hardware or design for long device lifespans directly impact Scope 3.
TCFD
Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures
Financial Stability Board · Now mandatory in UK, EU, NZ; adopted globally

TCFD requires companies to disclose how climate-related risks and opportunities affect their business — covering governance, strategy, risk management, and metrics. Originally voluntary, it is now mandatory for large companies in the UK, EU (as CSRD), New Zealand, and others.

Four pillars
Governance (board oversight), Strategy (climate scenario analysis), Risk Management (identification and response), Metrics & Targets (GHG data, energy intensity).
IT system risk
Physical risk: flooding/heat threatening data centers. Transition risk: carbon taxes making energy-intensive operations expensive. Both must be disclosed.
Engineering implication
IT infrastructure resilience assessments, energy intensity metrics, and climate scenario testing are now C-suite and board-level concerns — engineers provide the data.
Bangladesh watch
Not yet mandatory locally, but multinational clients operating in Bangladesh are subject to it. Expect trickle-down requirements in supply chain by 2026–2028.
EU EED
EU Energy Efficiency Directive
European Union · Legally binding for EU member states

The EU Energy Efficiency Directive (revised 2023) sets binding targets for reducing energy consumption across the EU economy, with specific provisions for data centers. From September 2024, data centers above 500 kW must register and report energy performance indicators to the EU Commission.

Data center requirements
PUE reporting, water usage effectiveness (WUE), renewable energy share, server utilization rate, and e-waste data — all mandatory for large data centers.
2030 target
EU overall energy efficiency improvement target of 32.5%. Data centers must be carbon-neutral by 2030 under the European Green Deal.
Penalties
Member states set penalties, but non-compliance risks significant fines and public naming — reputationally damaging for large operators like AWS, Google, Microsoft EU.
Bangladesh relevance
Direct EU market access requires compliance. Bangladeshi IT/ITES companies serving EU clients increasingly face contractual EED-aligned requirements.
WEEE
Waste Electrical & Electronic Equipment Directive
European Union · Legally binding; adopted in many other nations

WEEE makes manufacturers, importers, and distributors of electronics responsible for the end-of-life collection, recycling, and recovery of their products. It bans e-waste from landfill and sets recovery targets. The RoHS Directive (Restriction of Hazardous Substances) complements it by restricting toxic materials in electronics from the outset.

Who is affected
Anyone who manufactures, imports, or sells electrical/electronic equipment in the EU. Includes software companies that sell hardware bundled with services.
Collection targets
85% of e-waste generated must be collected for recycling. Recovery rates set by equipment category (IT/telecom equipment: 75% recovery rate).
Engineer's role
Design for disassembly, use of recyclable materials, avoiding hazardous substances, and extending product lifespans all directly reduce WEEE compliance burden.
Bangladesh context
Bangladesh has the E-Waste Management Rules 2021, modeled partly on WEEE. WEEE is directly applicable to Bangladeshi exporters selling into the EU market.

Landmark e-waste and hazardous substances rules in electronics — set the global template for producer responsibility.

Local Focus · 10 min
Bangladesh regulatory landscape & SREDA
What rules apply here — and what's coming
⏱ 10 minCO1
▼

Bangladesh's Green IT regulatory environment is young but evolving fast. Engineers starting their careers now will see these rules tighten significantly over the next 5–10 years. Understanding the current framework — and where it's heading — is a professional necessity.

📋 SREDA Act 2012

Sustainable and Renewable Energy Development Authority — Bangladesh's primary body for renewable energy policy. Targets: 10% renewable by 2021 (missed), 40% by 2041. Oversees solar, wind, and energy efficiency programs. Engineers can engage SREDA for project approvals and incentives.

♻️ E-Waste Management Rules 2021

Requires manufacturers, importers, and dealers of EEE (Electrical & Electronic Equipment) to register, collect, and ensure proper recycling of e-waste. Sets up Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) — the same model as EU WEEE. Enforcement is currently limited but growing.

🏛️ ICT Policy 2018

Bangladesh's national ICT policy includes provisions for green ICT, energy efficiency in government data centers, and promotion of renewable-powered ICT infrastructure under the "Digital Bangladesh" vision. Provides a framework for public procurement of green IT.

🔌 Bangladesh Energy Efficiency Master Plan

Sets economy-wide efficiency targets including the industrial and commercial ICT sector. The Bangladesh Energy Regulatory Commission (BERC) sets electricity tariffs that influence data center operating economics.

⚠️ The compliance gap
Many of Bangladesh's Green IT rules exist on paper but have weak enforcement mechanisms. This creates a compliance window — companies that build green IT practices now will be ahead of the curve when enforcement tightens, rather than scrambling to retrofit later. The cost of proactive compliance is a fraction of the cost of reactive compliance.

Applied · 10 min
Compliance in practice
How organizations actually implement and prove compliance
⏱ 10 minCO1
▼

Knowing a regulation exists is not enough. Engineers need to understand how compliance actually works — what evidence is required, who audits it, and what happens when things go wrong.

📘 Velete Ch.2 — The compliance cycle
Compliance is not a one-time event — it's an ongoing process: Assess → Plan → Implement → Monitor → Report → Audit → Improve. Organizations that treat compliance as a project (one-time) consistently fail audits. Those that embed it into operations (continuous) consistently pass.
🧩 Scenario: Is this organization compliant?
A medium-sized Dhaka IT company (200 employees, 50 servers, exports software to Germany). Answer each question:
1. They buy new servers every 3 years and throw old ones in general waste bins.
2. They measure their electricity consumption monthly but don't track carbon emissions or report to any framework.
3. They source 20% of their office electricity from a solar rooftop system and have an informal recycling bin for old equipment.
Audit scenario
An ISO 14001 auditor visits your data center. What is the first document they will ask for?
Tap to see answer
Answer
The Environmental Management System (EMS) manual and your environmental aspects register — listing every activity that has an environmental impact, its significance, and your controls. Without this, the audit fails immediately.
Procurement scenario
Your company bids for a UK government IT contract. They require ISO 14001 and TCFD disclosure. You have neither. How long would it realistically take?
Tap to see answer
Answer
ISO 14001 certification typically takes 6–18 months to implement and certify from scratch. TCFD-aligned reporting takes 3–6 months to develop. Realistically 12–18 months minimum. This is why regulatory awareness must be built into business development strategy — not handled after winning the bid.
Design scenario
You're specifying servers for a new Dhaka data center that will host EU clients. Which standards should influence your hardware selection?
Tap to see answer
Answer
Energy Star (efficiency labeling), EU Ecodesign Regulation compliance (product efficiency), WEEE-compliant end-of-life disposal plan, and GHG Protocol Scope 2 calculation for electricity. Choosing EU-compliant hardware from day one avoids costly retrofitting when the client requests an ESG audit.

Practical · 10 min
The engineer's regulatory obligations
What governance and compliance actually means for your day-to-day work
⏱ 10 minCO1
▼

Regulations aren't just management's problem. Engineers are the ones who implement the technical controls that make compliance possible — or impossible. Here's what governance means at the engineering level.

  • 📊
    Metering & monitoring — Compliance starts with measurement. Installing smart meters, DCIM (Data Center Infrastructure Management) software, and energy dashboards is engineering work. You can't report what you don't measure.
  • 📋
    Asset inventory & lifecycle tracking — E-waste rules require knowing what equipment you have, when it was deployed, and when it is retired. Engineers who build CMDB (Configuration Management Database) systems directly enable compliance.
  • 🔒
    Evidence generation — Auditors require documented evidence: energy readings, disposal certificates, vendor certifications. Automated reporting pipelines, not spreadsheets, are the professional standard.
  • 🏗️
    Design for compliance — Choosing WEEE-compliant hardware, designing systems that enable renewable energy integration, and specifying energy-efficient configurations are engineering decisions with regulatory consequences.
  • ⚠️
    Flagging non-compliance risk — Engineers who discover practices that create regulatory risk (e.g., improper e-waste disposal, fraudulent energy reporting) have a professional obligation to escalate. Engineering codes of conduct worldwide require acting in the public interest.
⚠️ Professional responsibility
From EP5 (Complex Engineering Activities): When a design involves diverse stakeholders with varying needs — regulators, clients, communities, future generations — engineers must identify and account for all their interests. Green IT governance is precisely this kind of complex multi-stakeholder engineering challenge.
