Last class we went inside the machine — DVFS, C-states, energy proportionality, and the race-to-idle principle. Today we zoom out to the data center level: once all those chips generate heat, how do we remove it efficiently?

📐 P = C·V²·f

Dynamic CPU power scales with voltage squared. Reducing voltage by 10% saves ~19% power — quadratic, not linear.

💤 C-states

CPU idle states (C0–C7+) allow near-zero power consumption when the processor has nothing to do. The OS balances depth vs. wake latency.

📊 Energy proportionality

Real servers draw ~50% power at 0% load. Consolidation via virtualization is the primary fix — pushing utilization into the efficient 60–80% zone.

Foundation · 10 min
Why cooling matters — heat in data centers
How electrical energy becomes heat, and why it must be removed
⏱ 10 minCO1
▼

Every watt of electrical power consumed by a computer is ultimately converted to heat. This is a fundamental consequence of thermodynamics — useful work (computation) and waste heat are inseparable. In a data center, removing that heat reliably and efficiently is as critical as supplying the electricity in the first place.

🔥 The first law — energy in = energy out
A 1 MW data center (IT load) generates 1 MW of heat continuously, 24/7. That heat must be removed or the servers overheat within minutes. The cooling system that removes it consumes additional electricity on top — this overhead is what PUE measures. A PUE of 1.5 means 0.5 MW of cooling energy for every 1 MW of computing.
85°C
Typical CPU junction temperature limit — exceeded temps cause throttling and hardware damage
27°C
ASHRAE recommended server inlet air temperature (raised from 20°C in 2011 to save cooling energy)
1kW/m²
Typical floor-level heat density in traditional data centers — high-density racks can exceed 30kW/rack
3,500W
Heat output of a typical fully loaded 1U server — equivalent to 35 incandescent light bulbs

Heat is removed from data centers through a cascade: chip → heatsink → air (or liquid) → room → exterior environment. Each transfer step has efficiency losses. The goal of cooling engineering is to move heat from hot chips to the outside environment using as little additional energy as possible.

🌡️ ASHRAE thermal envelopes

ASHRAE (American Society of Heating, Refrigerating and AC Engineers) defines server inlet temperature classes. Class A1: 15–32°C. Class A2: 10–35°C. Class A3: 5–40°C. Class A4: 5–45°C. Higher class = more flexible cooling design = cheaper/greener cooling systems. Most modern servers support A2 or A3.

⚠️ Heat density challenge

Traditional data centers were designed for ~3–5 kW/rack. Modern GPU clusters for AI training can exceed 100 kW/rack — an order of magnitude denser. Air cooling struggles above ~25–30 kW/rack, which is why liquid cooling is becoming mainstream rather than niche.

Core Concept · 15 min
Hot aisle / cold aisle containment
The single most impactful layout change for air-cooled data centers
⏱ 15 minCO1
▼

Before hot/cold aisle layouts, server rooms were simply filled with cool air everywhere. Servers could exhaust hot air that was immediately re-ingested by neighboring servers — mixing hot and cold air and dramatically reducing efficiency. Hot/cold aisle containment eliminates this mixing.

💡 The core principle
Servers take in cool air from the front face (cold aisle) and exhaust hot air from the rear face (hot aisle). By alternating rack orientations so all fronts face one aisle and all rears face the adjacent aisle, cool and hot air streams are separated. Containment panels then physically seal the aisles to prevent mixing.
🗺️ Hot Aisle / Cold Aisle Layout — Interactive Diagram
✅ With containment — what improves
  • Cool air reaches servers at design temperature (27°C) reliably
  • Hot exhaust goes straight to cooling unit without mixing
  • Can raise cooling supply temperature → less refrigeration energy
  • Enables "free cooling" at lower ambient temps
  • Typical PUE improvement: 1.8 → 1.3 (significant)
❌ Without containment — the problems
  • Hot exhaust recirculates into neighboring server intakes
  • Servers run hotter → throttle more → do less useful work
  • Must over-cool entire room to ensure hottest spots stay safe
  • Cold air short-circuits directly to hot aisle (bypasses servers)
  • Wasteful over-cooling of already-cool areas
📗 From Lamb Ch.8
Hot/cold aisle containment is one of the highest ROI investments in data center energy efficiency — often costing tens of thousands of dollars but saving hundreds of thousands per year in electricity. For existing data centers, it's the first physical modification to implement before considering any other cooling technology.

CRAC / CRAH
Computer Room Air Conditioning / Air Handler
Traditional air cooling — most widely deployed globally

Large air conditioning units placed around the perimeter (or under raised floors) of the data center push cool air through the room. CRAC units use refrigeration compressors (less efficient). CRAH units use chilled water from external chillers (more efficient at scale). Both are air-based and suitable up to ~10–15 kW/rack.

Max heat density
~10–15 kW/rack comfortably. Above this, hot spots form even with good aisle containment.
Typical PUE contribution
Cooling overhead of 0.4–0.8 PUE points. CRAH + chiller is more efficient than CRAC standalone.
Green credential
Poor to moderate. Refrigerant-based cooling uses significant energy. CRAH with economizer/free-cooling mode is better.
Best for
Legacy data centers, mixed-density environments, where capital investment for newer tech is constrained.
In-Row Cooling
Rack-Row-Level Air Cooling Units
Targeted air delivery — reduces distribution losses

Cooling units placed directly within server rows — between or beside racks — deliver cool air to the exact point of heat generation rather than relying on room-level air distribution. Dramatically reduces the distance (and energy) needed to move cool air to hot equipment.

Max heat density
~20–30 kW/rack — a significant step up from perimeter CRAC/CRAH units.
PUE improvement
Typically 0.1–0.2 PUE improvement over perimeter cooling in the same facility, due to shorter air paths and better targeting.
Green credential
Moderate to good. Still air-based but far more targeted. Often paired with hot-aisle containment for best results.
Best for
High-density zones within mixed data centers, colocation facilities adding dense GPU racks to existing air-cooled spaces.
Rear-Door HX
Rear-Door Heat Exchanger
Rack-level passive liquid cooling — no fans added

A heat exchanger replaces the rear door of a server rack. Chilled water circulates through the door panel, capturing the hot exhaust air directly as it leaves the rack — before it can reach the hot aisle. The air entering the hot aisle is effectively already cooled. This is a retrofit-friendly solution that works with existing server fan infrastructure.

Max heat density
~25–30 kW/rack in passive mode (using server fans), up to ~40 kW with supplemental fans.
Key advantage
Retrofit: installs in hours on existing racks without reconfiguring servers or networking. No server modifications needed.
Green credential
Good — uses server's existing fans (no extra fan energy), chilled water loop is efficient, can capture >100% of rack heat.
Best for
Existing data centers with pockets of high-density equipment (GPU servers, HPC) that can't be fully re-fitted for liquid cooling.
Direct Liquid Cooling
Cold Plates on CPU/GPU
Chip-level liquid cooling — mainstream in hyperscale & HPC

Liquid (water or dielectric fluid) circulates through metal cold plates attached directly to CPUs and GPUs — the primary heat sources. Because water has ~1,000× the heat capacity of air, cold plates can remove vastly more heat from a small area. This is now the standard approach for AI training clusters (NVIDIA H100/H200, AMD MI300) which can exceed 700W per chip.

Max heat density
~50–100 kW/rack and beyond — orders of magnitude above what air cooling can handle.
Efficiency
Can achieve PUE of 1.03–1.10 with warm water (up to 45°C supply) enabling economizer-only cooling in moderate climates.
Green credential
Excellent — waste heat can be warm enough (40–60°C) for heat recovery (district heating, process heat), turning a cost into a resource.
Best for
AI/ML training clusters, HPC supercomputers, hyperscale cloud (Google, Microsoft, Meta all deploying extensively).
Immersion Cooling
Single & Two-Phase Immersion
Servers submerged in dielectric fluid — the frontier of cooling tech

Servers are fully submerged in thermally conductive, electrically non-conductive (dielectric) fluid. In single-phase systems, the fluid circulates to an external heat exchanger. In two-phase systems, the fluid boils at low temperatures, rising as vapor to condensers that cool it back to liquid. Eliminates server fans entirely.

Max heat density
Effectively unlimited at rack scale — currently deployed at 200+ kW/rack. Bitcoin mining farms were early adopters due to extreme density needs.
PUE achievable
1.02–1.05 — world-class. Server fans (10–15% of server power) are eliminated entirely, adding another efficiency layer.
Challenges
High upfront cost, vendor lock-in (specialized tanks), two-phase fluid phase-out concerns (some fluids are potent greenhouse gases), maintenance complexity.
Green credential
Excellent efficiency, but some two-phase fluids (e.g., 3M Novec) have high GWP — a sustainability concern under EU F-Gas Regulation. Single-phase mineral oil or engineered fluids are more benign.

Key Technique · 15 min
Free cooling — nature does the work
Using ambient air or water to cool data centers without mechanical refrigeration
⏱ 15 minCO1
▼

Free cooling (also called an economizer mode) uses ambient outdoor air or outdoor water bodies to cool the data center — eliminating or reducing the energy-intensive refrigeration compressor cycle. When the outdoor temperature is low enough, nature provides the cooling for free; the data center only runs pumps and fans, not compressors.

💡 Economic impact
A chiller compressor can consume 0.3–0.5 kW per kW of cooling delivered. Free cooling (pumps + fans only) consumes 0.03–0.08 kW per kW of cooling — roughly a 10× improvement. For a 10 MW data center, every additional hour of free cooling per year saves approximately $800 in electricity (at $0.10/kWh). At 3,000 hours/year of free cooling, that's $2.4M saved annually.

Three types of free cooling are used in practice:

  • 💨
    Air-side economizer — Outdoor air is filtered and drawn directly into the data center when it's cool and dry enough. Used by Facebook (now Meta) in their Luleå, Sweden data center where sub-zero winters provide nearly year-round free cooling. Requires careful humidity and particulate management.
  • 💧
    Water-side economizer — A cooling tower rejects heat to the atmosphere via evaporative cooling, with the cooled water looped through the data center. Works at higher ambient temps than air-side but uses water (WUE — Water Usage Effectiveness becomes an important metric).
  • 🌊
    Natural water body cooling — Using nearby rivers, lakes, or seawater as a heat sink. Google's Hamina, Finland data center uses seawater from the Gulf of Finland. Microsoft's Project Natick tested underwater data centers. Extremely efficient but geographically limited.
⚠️ Free cooling and climate — a design constraint
Free cooling availability depends heavily on local climate. A data center in Dhaka, Bangladesh (average temp 26°C, high humidity) has far fewer hours of free cooling available than one in Helsinki, Finland (average temp 6°C). This is a key reason why hyperscale data centers cluster in Nordic countries, the Pacific Northwest US, and other cool, dry climates — geography is a cooling strategy.

⚠️ PUE limitations — what it doesn't measure
PUE only measures facility overhead, not IT efficiency. A data center with a PUE of 1.0 where all servers run at 5% utilization is massively inefficient — but PUE won't reveal that. PUE also ignores: water consumption (needs WUE), carbon intensity of electricity source, server hardware embodied carbon, or whether useful work is actually being done. This is why frameworks like the European Commission's data center KPIs add utilization rate, renewable energy fraction, and WUE alongside PUE.

Local Context · 10 min
Cooling challenges in Bangladesh
Hot, humid, unreliable grid — designing cooling for a difficult climate
⏱ 10 minCO1
▼

The cooling strategies discussed above were largely developed for temperate climates. Bangladesh's climate presents unique engineering challenges — and constraints — that change which techniques are practical and which are not.

🌡️ Climate challenge

Dhaka averages 26°C annually, reaching 35–40°C in April–May. High year-round humidity (80%+) makes air-side economizers impractical — moisture control would consume more energy than it saves. Free cooling hours per year: very limited (maybe 200–400h vs. 6,000+ hours in Nordic countries).

🔌 Grid reliability

Load shedding and voltage fluctuations mean data centers must maintain large diesel generator capacity — adding to both capital cost and carbon footprint (Scope 1 emissions). Cooling systems must be designed to restart gracefully after generator switchover without temperature spikes.

💧 Water-side cooling tradeoff

Water-side economizers and cooling towers are viable and widely used. However, water scarcity is a growing concern in Dhaka — WUE (Water Usage Effectiveness) matters alongside PUE. Closed-loop water systems minimize consumption versus evaporative open towers.

✅ What works here

Hot/cold aisle containment (immediate ROI regardless of climate), in-row cooling for density, rear-door HX for hybrid zones, and high-efficiency CRAH units with chilled water plants. Geothermal cooling has unexplored potential in Bangladesh's alluvial geography.

🔧 Engineering opportunity
Bangladesh's National Data Center (Kaliakoir Hi-Tech Park) and growing private data center market represent a significant design opportunity. Engineers who can specify and implement efficient cooling for tropical climates — where most solutions were designed for temperate ones — are solving a genuinely hard and underserved problem with global applicability (entire tropical belt faces the same challenge).
Design scenario
A new 2 MW data center is planned in Dhaka. The architect proposes an air-side economizer "to save cooling energy." Should you approve?
Tap to see answer
Answer
No — air-side economizers are impractical in Dhaka's hot, humid climate. The outdoor air is too warm and too humid for most of the year. Recommend instead: hot/cold aisle containment + high-efficiency CRAH units + chilled water plant + water-side economizer for the limited cool-season hours.
Upgrade scenario
An existing Dhaka data center has a PUE of 2.1. Management approves a BDT 50 lakh budget. What's the single highest-ROI investment?
Tap to see answer
Answer
Hot/cold aisle containment — lowest cost, fastest ROI, no server downtime required. Physical blanking panels for empty rack spaces (stops bypass airflow) cost almost nothing. Combined, these typically cut PUE from 2.1 toward 1.6–1.7 for a fraction of the budget.
GPU cluster scenario
A Bangladeshi AI company imports 40 NVIDIA H100 GPUs (700W each, 28 kW/rack). Their existing air-cooled racks max at 10 kW. What do they do?
Tap to see answer
Answer
28 kW/rack far exceeds air-cooling limits. Options: (1) rear-door heat exchangers retrofitted to existing racks — easiest, (2) direct liquid cooling cold plates on the H100s if server vendor supports it, (3) co-locate in a facility already equipped for high-density liquid cooling. Option 1 is fastest; Option 2 is most efficient long-term.
