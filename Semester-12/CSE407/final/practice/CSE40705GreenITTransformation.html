How to use this guide

Class 13 — Managing & Maintaining Green IT Transformation. Velete Ch.13 & 14, Lamb Ch.12 & Appendix A. Course outcome CO3.

Every previous class gave you a technology. This one gives you the organisational machinery that makes technology stick. The faculty's framing in the opening was blunt: after Class 12's consolidation work, "technology alone won't keep an organisation green. Without policy, governance, and active change management, virtual sprawl quickly cancels out efficiency gains."

The one-sentence version of Class 13

Technology accounts for only 20% of transformation failure and human factors for the other 80%, so a Green IT programme succeeds on change management and governance — and it only gets funded if you can present it to a CFO as CapEx, payback period and ROI rather than as environmental goodwill.

The numerical work here is the most business-oriented of the six topics, and it is also the most formulaic — four formulas cover essentially everything. The faculty even put them in a sidebar quick-reference, which is a strong hint. Learn those four and most of the calculation marks are secured.

The faculty's own four-formula quick reference
\[ \text{① Annual \$ saved} = \text{kWh saved} \times \text{Tariff} \] \[ \text{② Simple payback (years)} = \frac{\text{CapEx}}{\text{Annual \$ saved}} \] \[ \text{③ Tonnes CO}_2\text{e} = \frac{\text{kWh} \times \text{Emission factor}}{1{,}000} \] \[ \text{④ ROI \%} = \frac{\text{Total savings} - \text{CapEx}}{\text{CapEx}} \times 100\% \]
Topic 01

The four-phase transformation lifecycle

Learn the four phase names in order, and know what each one contains.

The faculty's central structural claim is that a Green IT transformation is not a single project but an iterative lifecycle. The standard enterprise framework has four phases, designed to move an organisation from reactive compliance to proactive leadership, and the whole enterprise typically takes 3–5 years to reach maturity.

PhaseNameWhat happens
Phase 1Assess & Plan Baselining the current footprint and gap analysis. Establish Scope 1, 2 and 3 energy baselines; perform a Maturity Model assessment (e.g. CMMI-aligned Green IT models).
Phase 2Architect & Implement Deploying quick wins and core infrastructure. Identify low-hanging fruit — consolidation, PC power management. Execute green procurement requirements for hardware.
Phase 3Measure & Optimize Continuous KPI tracking and refinement. Implement automated DCIM and IoT monitoring dashboards.
Phase 4Institutionalize Embedding policies into culture. Link Green IT KPIs to executive and department performance reviews; establish formal e-waste and recycling vendor audits; publish annual ESG and sustainability reports.
Watch out — the "set and forget" risk

The faculty called this out specifically, and it is the reason Phase 4 exists as a separate phase rather than as an afterthought. Without institutionalisation, efficiency gains erode over time. Three named mechanisms: staff turn over and the knowledge leaves with them, legacy hardware creeps back in, and virtualisation ratios degrade without active enforcement. A transformation that stops at Phase 3 will quietly unwind. If a question asks why initiatives stall or regress, this is the answer.

Note the shorthand the wrap-up slide used for the same four phases: Plan, Implement, Measure, Sustain. Either naming is acceptable, but use one consistently.

Topic 02

Change management and Kotter's 8 steps

The 80/20 claim, and the eight steps mapped onto sustainability.

Start with the number that justifies the whole section: 70% of digital transformations fail due to poor change management and culture, and the faculty's split is that technology accounts for only 20% of transformation failure while human factors account for the other 80%. That is why a green computing course spends fifteen minutes on organisational behaviour.

The framework applied is Kotter's 8-Step Model, which the lecture presented in four pairs. Learn the pairs — it halves the memorisation and matches how the faculty grouped them.

Steps 1 & 2 — Create urgency & build a guiding coalition

  • Urgency drivers: rising energy tariffs, regulatory compliance penalties, corporate reputation risk. Note these are all business risks — urgency is manufactured from commercial pressure, not from environmental appeal.
  • Guiding coalition: form a Green Steering Committee combining IT, Facilities, Finance and HR leadership. The cross-functional composition matters — an IT-only committee cannot change procurement policy or performance reviews.

Steps 3 & 4 — Develop the vision & communicate buy-in

  • Green vision: set tangible targets. The lecture's model phrasing: "30% energy reduction in 24 months with zero performance compromise." Note the structure — a number, a deadline, and an explicit reassurance about the thing people fear losing.
  • Communication: departmental dashboards, town halls, and internal green champion networks.

Steps 5 & 6 — Empower action & generate quick wins

  • Empowerment: remove the friction. Specifically, remove policies that force paper printing or require always-on desktop hardware — you cannot ask staff to behave sustainably while a policy compels the opposite.
  • Quick wins: execute rapid server virtualisation or PC sleep-policy rollouts to demonstrate immediate dollar savings. The purpose of a quick win is political, not environmental — it buys credibility for the harder phases.

Steps 7 & 8 — Sustain acceleration & anchor in culture

  • Sustain gains: reinvest energy cost savings into next-generation sustainable technologies — creating a self-funding cycle rather than competing for fresh budget each year.
  • Cultural anchor: embed green criteria into hiring, employee onboarding, and annual performance reviews. This is the same mechanism as Phase 4 of the lifecycle, viewed from the people side.
Key idea — why initiatives stall after the easy wins

The faculty opened the class with this as a discussion question, which makes it very likely to appear. The answer combines both frameworks: easy wins are technical and require no behavioural change — switching off unused servers costs nobody anything. The next tier of savings requires people to work differently, which is where the 80% human factor bites. Without Kotter steps 7 and 8 and lifecycle Phase 4 — reinvestment, performance-review linkage, cultural anchoring — there is no mechanism to carry a programme past the point where it starts asking something of people.

Topic 03

Metrics, ROI and TCO — building the CFO-ready case

The four formulas, and what the resulting numbers mean.

The faculty's framing here is the sentence to quote in any business-case question: "CFOs do not approve projects based on environmental goodwill." Every Green IT initiative must present a rigorous business case comparing upfront Capital Expenditure (CapEx) against long-term Operational Expenditure (OpEx) savings.

Simple payback period

The most-used metric, and the one most likely to be asked for. It answers: how long until the investment has paid for itself out of the savings it generates?

Simple payback period
\[ \text{Payback (years)} = \frac{\text{CapEx}}{\text{Annual savings}} \]

"Simple" means future cash flows are not discounted — a dollar saved in year 5 is counted the same as a dollar saved in year 1. That is the method's known limitation and worth one sentence in an exam answer.

The faculty's interpretation bands, taken from their interactive calculator: under 2 years = excellent, over 4 years = poor. They also noted specifically that a 2-year payback is considered excellent for software-driven Green IT projects.

Return on investment

Net ROI over a period
\[ \text{ROI \%} = \frac{\text{Total savings} - \text{CapEx}}{\text{CapEx}} \times 100\% \]

The numerator is the net return — savings after the investment has been recovered. Compute total savings across the full period first, then subtract.

Carbon accounting

Energy to emissions
\[ \text{Tonnes CO}_2\text{e} = \frac{\text{kWh saved} \times \text{Emission factor (kg CO}_2\text{e/kWh)}}{1{,}000} \]

The faculty's stated figure for Bangladesh's grid carbon intensity is 0.6 kg CO₂e per kWh. Use it unless a question supplies a different one. The ÷1,000 converts kilograms to metric tonnes.

The lecture's note on why this conversion matters: it is crucial for mandatory corporate Scope 2 carbon reporting. Electricity purchased is Scope 2, so any energy saving converts directly into a reportable Scope 2 reduction.

Watch out — payback and ROI are not the same question

Payback asks how long until you break even, and is measured in years. ROI asks how much you gained over a stated period, and is a percentage. A project can have an attractive payback and an unimpressive ROI if the horizon is short, and vice versa. Read which one is being asked for, and always state the period for an ROI figure — "+75%" is meaningless without "over 5 years".

Topic 04

Governance, compliance and policy

Four instruments. Know what each is for, since they are easily confused.

Governance provides the legal and procedural guardrails for transformation. The faculty's framing: compliance frameworks help organisations avoid greenwashing while meeting regulatory auditing requirements — note that both halves matter, internal discipline and external proof.

  • ISO 14001 — the framework for an Environmental Management System (EMS), used to continuously measure and improve environmental impacts. It is a management system standard: it certifies that you have a process, not that you have achieved a particular outcome.
  • GHG Protocol (Scope 1, 2, 3) — the standard accounting framework for emissions. Scope 1 = direct emissions (your own generators, vehicles). Scope 2 = purchased electricity. Scope 3 = supply chain and vendor footprint, including manufacturing of equipment you buy and end-of-life disposal. This is required for regulatory ESG reporting.
  • Sustainable Procurement Policy — mandating Energy Star, EPEAT Gold or TCO Certified ratings for all new hardware acquisitions. This is the Class 8 procurement multiplier expressed as organisational policy rather than as an individual decision.
  • E-Waste Governance (WEEE) — certified chain-of-custody recycling to ensure zero hazardous electronic waste reaches landfill. This is Class 7's EPR material appearing as an internal control.
Key idea — the three scopes, with course examples

Scope 1 — the diesel generators a Dhaka data centre needs because of grid unreliability (Class 11). Scope 2 — every kWh a data centre draws, which is what PUE improvement and consolidation reduce (Classes 11 and 12). Scope 3 — the 70–80% embodied carbon in every device procured, and the e-waste leaving at end of life (Classes 7 and 8). Being able to place each of the other five topics into the right scope is a strong answer to any question about emissions boundaries.

Topic 05

The Bangladesh context

Three specific local drivers, all of which make good scenario material.

Transformation drivers in Bangladesh are heavily influenced by commercial electricity price rises, national energy shortages, and green banking guidelines from Bangladesh Bank.

  • Rising electricity tariffs. Commercial power tariffs have risen steadily, so server room energy efficiency directly affects operating margins for tech firms and commercial banks in Dhaka. This is the urgency driver a local Green Steering Committee would lead with.
  • Bangladesh Bank Green Guidelines. Central bank regulations require commercial banks to allocate specific funds to green finance, implement in-house green office policies, and report annual Scope 1 & 2 footprints. For a bank, Green IT is therefore a compliance obligation, not a discretionary programme.
  • SREDA — the Sustainable & Renewable Energy Development Authority. Its role, per the lecture: providing energy audit standards, guidelines for rooftop solar integration for data centres, and certification for energy-efficient commercial buildings.
Watch out — the thin client figure

The faculty's answer to the branch-bank scenario contains a number worth memorising: centralised automated PC power management combined with thin-client deployment cuts per-terminal draw from 150 W to 15 W — a factor of ten. Paired with enforced off-hours shutdown, this is the standard "most effective policy-first initiative" answer for a branch-network scenario.

Practice A

Numerical practice — worked line by line

All four formulas, individually and combined. These extend the faculty's own five practice cards.

Question 1 · easy · warm-up

An enterprise invests $60,000 in dynamic power management software. It cuts annual electricity usage by 200,000 kWh. Electricity costs $0.15/kWh. (a) Annual saving? (b) Simple payback period? (c) Is this a good result?

Show worked solution

  1. \( E = 200{,}000\ \text{kWh/yr},\quad r = \$0.15/\text{kWh} \)

    Annual energy saved and the tariff — the two inputs to formula ①.

  2. \( S = E \times r = 200{,}000 \times 0.15 \)

    Formula ①: Annual $ = kWh × tariff. Units: kWh × $/kWh = $, so kWh cancels.

  3. \( S = \$30{,}000/\text{yr} \)

    Multiply.

  4. \( \text{Payback} = \dfrac{\text{CapEx}}{S} = \dfrac{60{,}000}{30{,}000} \)

    Formula ②: payback = CapEx ÷ annual savings. Units: $ ÷ ($/yr) = yr, which confirms the answer is a time.

  5. \( = 2.0\ \text{years} \)

    Divide.

Answer

(a) $30,000/year · (b) 2.0 years · (c) Yes — the faculty's own note on this problem is that a 2-year payback is considered excellent for software-driven Green IT projects, and their calculator bands put under 2 years at "excellent" and over 4 at "poor".

Worth adding: software-driven projects like DPM typically pay back faster than hardware projects because the CapEx is licensing rather than equipment, and there is no embodied carbon in the investment itself. Sanity check: the CapEx is exactly twice the annual saving, so a 2-year payback is arithmetically obvious — a useful pattern to spot quickly under time pressure.

Question 2 · easy · warm-up

Bangladesh's grid carbon intensity is 0.6 kg CO₂e per kWh. How many metric tonnes of CO₂e are offset by saving 150,000 kWh? Which GHG Protocol scope does this reduction fall under?

Show worked solution

  1. \( E = 150{,}000\ \text{kWh},\quad f = 0.6\ \text{kg CO}_2\text{e/kWh} \)

    Energy saved and the grid emission factor. The factor converts electricity into emissions.

  2. \( m = E \times f = 150{,}000 \times 0.6 = 90{,}000\ \text{kg} \)

    Energy × emission factor. Units: kWh × kg/kWh = kg, so kWh cancels — confirming the factor was applied the right way round.

  3. \( m = \dfrac{90{,}000}{1{,}000} = 90\ \text{metric tonnes} \)

    Formula ③'s ÷1,000 step: convert kilograms to metric tonnes, since 1 t = 1,000 kg and corporate reporting uses tonnes.

Answer

90 metric tonnes CO₂e. This is a Scope 2 reduction, because Scope 2 covers emissions from purchased electricity.

The lecture's note on why this conversion matters: it is crucial for mandatory corporate Scope 2 carbon reporting. In the Bangladeshi banking context this connects directly to Bangladesh Bank's requirement that commercial banks report annual Scope 1 and 2 footprints. Sanity check: 0.6 is roughly half, so the tonnage should be a bit over half the kWh figure divided by a thousand — 150 → 90 fits.

Question 3 · medium · core

A company replaces 100 legacy servers (400 W each) with 10 virtualised hosts (600 W each), running 24/7 for a year (8,760 hrs). (a) How many kWh are saved annually? (b) At $0.15/kWh, what is the annual monetary saving? (c) At 0.6 kg CO₂e/kWh, how many tonnes of CO₂e are avoided?

Show worked solution

  1. \( P_{\text{legacy}} = 100 \times 0.4\ \text{kW} = 40\ \text{kW} \)

    Count × per-unit power. Convert 400 W to 0.4 kW immediately, because energy will be wanted in kWh and mixing W with kWh is where marks are lost.

  2. \( P_{\text{new}} = 10 \times 0.6\ \text{kW} = 6\ \text{kW} \)

    Same pattern after consolidation. Note the per-host figure rose from 0.4 to 0.6 kW — the non-linear power curve from Class 12, since the surviving hosts work harder.

  3. \( \Delta P = 40 - 6 = 34\ \text{kW} \)

    Difference of the two loads is the continuous power saved.

  4. \( E = \Delta P \times t = 34 \times 8{,}760 = 297{,}840\ \text{kWh/yr} \)

    Energy = power × time. 8,760 h is one continuous year, which the question specifies as 24/7 operation.

  5. \( S = 297{,}840 \times \$0.15 = \$44{,}676/\text{yr} \)

    Formula ①. kWh × $/kWh = $.

  6. \( m = \dfrac{297{,}840 \times 0.6}{1{,}000} = 178.7\ \text{tonnes} \)

    Formula ③. Energy × emission factor, then ÷1,000 for tonnes.

Answer

(a) 297,840 kWh/year — the faculty's note calls this "nearly 300 MWh of annual energy reduction". (b) ≈ $44,676/year. (c) ≈ 178.7 tonnes CO₂e/year.

Add for marks: this counts only the direct IT electricity. Applying the facility PUE from Class 12's multiplier point would increase all three figures by 30–60% depending on the facility. Sanity check: 34 kW continuous is a big saving, and 34 × 8,760 ≈ 300,000 kWh is the right order of magnitude — if you had got 3,000 or 3,000,000 a factor of 8,760 has gone wrong.

Question 4 · medium · core

A legacy PC fleet draws 50 kW during non-working hours because sleep policies are disabled. Enforcing night sleep cuts this off-hours draw to 5 kW across 12 off-peak hours per day. (a) Energy saved per day? (b) Over a 250-day working year? (c) What is the CapEx, and what does that imply for payback?

Show worked solution

  1. \( P_{\text{before}} = 50\ \text{kW},\quad P_{\text{after}} = 5\ \text{kW} \)

    The off-hours draw before and after the policy. Note both are off-peak figures — the working-hours draw is unaffected and is irrelevant to this calculation.

  2. \( \Delta P = 50 - 5 = 45\ \text{kW} \)

    Difference in power. The residual 5 kW represents machines that must stay awake — servers, always-on terminals — so it cannot be driven to zero.

  3. \( E_{\text{day}} = \Delta P \times h = 45 \times 12 = 540\ \text{kWh/day} \)

    Energy = power × time, using only the 12 off-peak hours because the saving only exists during those hours.

  4. \( E_{\text{yr}} = 540 \times 250 = 135{,}000\ \text{kWh/yr} \)

    Daily saving × working days. Use 250, not 365 — the question specifies a working year, and the fleet is presumably already off at weekends.

Answer

(a) 540 kWh/day · (b) 135,000 kWh/year · (c) The CapEx is effectively $0 — this is a Group Policy configuration change, not a purchase. With zero investment, the payback period is immediate, and formula ② is undefined (division by zero), which is the correct thing to say rather than forcing a number.

The faculty's own note: "over a 250-day working year, that simple policy tweak saves 135,000 kWh with $0 CapEx!" This is the archetypal quick win from Kotter steps 5 and 6 — rapid, visible, no capital request, and it demonstrates ROI fast enough to buy credibility for the harder phases. At $0.15/kWh it is worth about $20,250/year.

Note the connection to Kotter step 5 (empowerment): the barrier here was a policy that required always-on desktop hardware, not a technical limitation. The saving was available all along and was blocked by an organisational rule — which is exactly the faculty's point that human factors dominate.

Question 5 · medium · core

An enterprise spends $100,000 on a green data centre retrofit. Over 5 years it saves $35,000 per year in electricity. (a) What is the 5-year Net ROI percentage? (b) What is the simple payback period? (c) Would a CFO approve this?

Show worked solution

  1. \( \text{CapEx} = \$100{,}000,\quad S_{\text{yr}} = \$35{,}000/\text{yr},\quad n = 5 \)

    The investment, the annual saving, and the evaluation period. ROI is meaningless without stating the period.

  2. \( S_{\text{total}} = 35{,}000 \times 5 = \$175{,}000 \)

    Total savings = annual × years. Compute the gross figure before subtracting anything — this is the step most often skipped.

  3. \( \text{Net return} = 175{,}000 - 100{,}000 = \$75{,}000 \)

    Subtract the CapEx to get the net return. The investment must be recovered before anything counts as gain.

  4. \( \text{ROI \%} = \dfrac{75{,}000}{100{,}000} \times 100\% \)

    Formula ④: net return ÷ CapEx, expressed as a percentage. Note the denominator is CapEx, not total savings.

  5. \( = +75\% \)

    Divide and scale. The plus sign matters — a negative ROI would mean the project never recovered its cost.

  6. \( \text{Payback} = \dfrac{100{,}000}{35{,}000} \approx 2.86\ \text{years} \)

    Formula ②. Consistent with the ROI: breaking even at 2.86 years leaves about 2.14 years of pure saving inside the 5-year window, and 2.14 × 35,000 ≈ 75,000. The two answers cross-check.

Answer

(a) +75% over 5 years · (b) ≈ 2.9 years payback · (c) The faculty's assessment: "a +75% net return over 5 years easily secures CFO project authorisation."

Nuance worth a mark: the payback of 2.9 years sits between the "excellent" band (under 2) and the "poor" band (over 4), so on payback alone it is merely acceptable. It is the 5-year ROI that makes the case, which illustrates why you present both metrics — a project can look ordinary on one and strong on the other. Note also that this is simple payback, undiscounted; a CFO applying a discount rate would see a somewhat lower real return, and acknowledging that in an answer shows you understand the method's limits.

Question 6 · hard · stretch

A Dhaka bank runs 1,200 branch desktops at 150 W each. It proposes replacing them with thin clients at 15 W each, plus centralised power management enforcing shutdown outside a 10-hour working day. Terminals currently run 24/7. Thin clients cost $180 each; the supporting server infrastructure costs $95,000. Electricity is $0.16/kWh; grid intensity 0.6 kg CO₂e/kWh.

(a) Current annual energy. (b) New annual energy. (c) Annual saving in kWh and dollars. (d) Total CapEx and simple payback. (e) 5-year ROI. (f) Annual CO₂e avoided, and which Bangladesh Bank obligation this supports.

Show worked solution

  1. \( P_{\text{now}} = 1{,}200 \times 0.15\ \text{kW} = 180\ \text{kW} \)

    Count × per-unit power, converting 150 W to 0.15 kW up front so all later steps stay in kW and kWh.

  2. \( E_{\text{now}} = 180 \times 8{,}760 = 1{,}576{,}800\ \text{kWh/yr} \)

    Energy = power × time. The current terminals run 24/7, so the full 8,760 hours applies.

  3. \( P_{\text{new}} = 1{,}200 \times 0.015\ \text{kW} = 18\ \text{kW} \)

    Same count × per-unit step at the thin-client draw of 15 W. Note this is exactly one tenth of the current load — the 150 W → 15 W factor the lecture quotes.

  4. \( t_{\text{new}} = 10\ \text{h/day} \times 365 = 3{,}650\ \text{h/yr} \)

    The hours change too. Power management enforces shutdown outside a 10-hour working day, so the new operating time is not 8,760 — this is the step the question is really testing.

  5. \( E_{\text{new}} = 18 \times 3{,}650 = 65{,}700\ \text{kWh/yr} \)

    Energy = power × time, using the reduced load and the reduced hours. Both factors compound.

  6. \( \Delta E = 1{,}576{,}800 - 65{,}700 = 1{,}511{,}100\ \text{kWh/yr} \)

    Difference of the two annual energies.

  7. \( S = 1{,}511{,}100 \times \$0.16 = \$241{,}776/\text{yr} \)

    Formula ①: kWh × tariff.

  8. \( \text{CapEx} = 1{,}200 \times 180 + 95{,}000 = \$311{,}000 \)

    Sum both capital components — the terminals and the supporting infrastructure. Forgetting the server cost is the obvious trap.

  9. \( \text{Payback} = \dfrac{311{,}000}{241{,}776} \approx 1.29\ \text{years} \)

    Formula ②: CapEx ÷ annual saving.

  10. \( S_{5} = 241{,}776 \times 5 = \$1{,}208{,}880 \)

    Total savings over the 5-year horizon, needed before the ROI subtraction.

  11. \( \text{ROI} = \dfrac{1{,}208{,}880 - 311{,}000}{311{,}000} \times 100\% \approx +289\% \)

    Formula ④: (total savings − CapEx) ÷ CapEx × 100.

  12. \( m = \dfrac{1{,}511{,}100 \times 0.6}{1{,}000} = 906.7\ \text{tonnes} \)

    Formula ③: energy × emission factor ÷ 1,000 for metric tonnes.

Answer

(a) 1,576,800 kWh/yr · (b) 65,700 kWh/yr · (c) 1,511,100 kWh saved ≈ $241,776/yr · (d) CapEx $311,000, payback ≈ 1.3 years · (e) ≈ +289% over 5 years · (f) ≈ 907 tonnes CO₂e/year avoided.

Why it is so dramatic: two multipliers compound. The hardware change cuts draw by a factor of ten (150 W → 15 W), and the policy change cuts operating hours by a factor of 2.4 (8,760 → 3,650). Together that is roughly a 96% energy reduction. This is the faculty's point that the most effective initiative is policy-first — the power management policy is free and delivers a large share of the benefit on its own.

On the Bangladesh Bank obligation (f): this supports the requirement that commercial banks report annual Scope 1 and 2 footprints, since purchased electricity is Scope 2. It also serves the in-house green office policy requirement, and the capital spend could potentially be framed within the bank's green finance allocation. For a bank, this is a compliance deliverable as well as a cost saving — which is the strongest possible framing for a CFO.

Two caveats to state for full marks. First, the thin clients depend on server infrastructure that itself draws power continuously — a rigorous analysis would add that load, which the $95,000 figure implies but does not quantify. Second, this ignores the embodied carbon of 1,200 new thin clients (Class 8): if the existing desktops are serviceable, the circular hierarchy would ask whether they can be repurposed rather than replaced. Payback in energy terms is 1.3 years; payback in carbon terms would be longer once manufacturing is counted.

Practice B

Analytical questions

Question 7 · short answer

Why do most corporate sustainability initiatives stall after the initial easy wins, such as turning off unused servers or printing less? Explain with reference to the transformation lifecycle and change management.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from This is the faculty's own warm-up discussion question from Section 1, which makes it a strong exam candidate. It requires you to combine Topic 01 (the four-phase lifecycle, particularly the "set and forget" risk) with Topic 02 (Kotter and the 80% human factor). A good answer explains what changes between the easy wins and the next tier of savings.

The core reason: the easy wins are technical, and everything after them is behavioural. Switching off unused servers or enabling a sleep policy costs nobody anything and asks nobody to work differently. It is a configuration change with an immediate, visible dollar saving. The next tier of savings — changing procurement standards, altering refresh cycles, enforcing virtualisation ratios, redesigning workflows to eliminate printing — requires people to change established habits and accept constraints on their own decisions. That is where resistance appears, and the faculty's split is that technology accounts for only 20% of transformation failure while human factors account for the other 80%. More broadly, 70% of digital transformations fail due to poor change management and culture.

The lifecycle explanation. Easy wins sit in Phase 2 (Architect & Implement), which the lecture describes as identifying "low-hanging fruit". Organisations that stop there never reach Phase 3 (Measure & Optimize), which requires investment in DCIM and IoT monitoring dashboards and ongoing KPI tracking, or Phase 4 (Institutionalize). Phase 4 is where the gains are made permanent, and skipping it produces the "set and forget" risk: efficiency gains erode over time because staff turn over and take the knowledge with them, legacy hardware creeps back in, and virtualisation ratios degrade without active enforcement. So a programme that stalls after the easy wins does not merely plateau — it regresses.

The change management explanation. In Kotter's terms, easy wins are step 6 — generate quick wins to demonstrate ROI fast. But step 6 is a means to an end, and organisations frequently treat it as the destination. What is missing is step 7 (sustain acceleration) — reinvesting the energy cost savings into next-generation sustainable technologies, creating a self-funding cycle rather than an annual budget fight — and step 8 (anchor in culture) — embedding green criteria into hiring, onboarding and annual performance reviews. Without step 8 in particular, nobody's job depends on the programme continuing, so when attention moves elsewhere it simply stops.

Two supporting factors worth naming. First, the guiding coalition (step 2) is often too narrow — an IT-only initiative cannot change procurement policy, facilities practice or HR performance criteria, so it exhausts the savings available within IT's own authority and halts. The lecture specifies a Green Steering Committee spanning IT, Facilities, Finance and HR precisely to avoid this. Second, the easy wins are cheap and highly visible, so they create an impression of success that reduces the perceived urgency (step 1) needed to justify the harder work.

Question 8 · short answer

Explain the GHG Protocol's three scopes, and classify the following into the correct scope: a data centre's diesel generators, purchased grid electricity, the embodied carbon of newly procured laptops, and e-waste sent to a recycler.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from Topic 04 (governance), but it is really a cross-course question — the examiner is using the scopes as a framework to test whether you can place material from Classes 7, 8, 11 and 12 into the right emissions boundary. Define the three scopes crisply first, then classify with a one-line justification each.

The three scopes. The GHG Protocol is the standard accounting framework for corporate emissions, and it divides them by who controls the source:

  • Scope 1 — direct emissions from sources the organisation owns or controls. Fuel burned on site, company vehicles, on-site generation.
  • Scope 2 — indirect emissions from purchased energy, principally electricity. The emission happens at the power station, but the organisation caused it by buying the power.
  • Scope 3 — all other indirect emissions across the value chain: the supply chain and vendor footprint, including the manufacture of purchased equipment and its end-of-life disposal. It is the broadest and hardest to measure, and for IT hardware it is usually the largest.

Classification:

  • Diesel generators — Scope 1. The fuel is burned on the organisation's own site under its own control. This is directly relevant in Bangladesh, where grid unreliability makes N+1 generator capacity close to mandatory (Class 11), so local facilities carry Scope 1 emissions that better-supplied grids avoid.
  • Purchased grid electricity — Scope 2. This is what PUE improvement (Class 11) and server consolidation (Class 12) reduce, and it is what the kWh × emission factor conversion quantifies.
  • Embodied carbon of newly procured laptops — Scope 3. The emissions occurred in someone else's factory, but they were caused by the purchase. This is the 70–80% of a device's lifetime footprint from Class 8 — which is why green procurement is a Scope 3 lever and why specification is the highest-leverage moment.
  • E-waste sent to a recycler — Scope 3. End-of-life treatment is a value-chain emission. This is why Class 7 stressed documenting the disposal route as part of Scope 3 e-waste reporting, and why certified chain-of-custody recycling is listed as a governance control.

Why the distinction matters practically. Regulatory ESG reporting frequently mandates Scope 1 and 2 while treating Scope 3 as voluntary — Bangladesh Bank's green guidelines, for instance, require commercial banks to report annual Scope 1 and 2 footprints. That creates a real risk: an organisation can show falling reported emissions while its Scope 3 footprint grows, simply by refreshing hardware more often. It also explains why the Class 8 greenwashing test asks whether a "carbon neutral" pledge includes Scope 3 — a neutrality claim covering only Scopes 1 and 2 omits most of a hardware company's actual footprint.

Question 9 · short answer

A project has a simple payback of 3.5 years and a 5-year ROI of +43%. A second project has a payback of 1.8 years and a 5-year ROI of +178%. Explain what each metric tells you, why both are presented, and what neither captures.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from Topic 03. The examiner is checking that you understand these are answers to different questions, not two versions of the same number. The final part — what neither captures — invites you to show the limits of the method, which is where the top marks sit.

What payback tells you. Simple payback answers how long until the investment is recovered, measured in years: CapEx ÷ annual savings. It is fundamentally a risk metric. A short payback means capital is at risk for less time, which matters if the technology may be superseded, the organisation's circumstances may change, or the electricity tariff assumption may not hold. Using the faculty's bands, project 2 at 1.8 years is excellent (under 2), while project 1 at 3.5 years is acceptable but approaching the poor threshold of 4 years.

What ROI tells you. ROI answers how much total value the investment generates over a stated period, as a percentage: (total savings − CapEx) ÷ CapEx × 100. It is a magnitude metric. Project 2's +178% means it returns nearly three times its cost over five years; project 1's +43% means it returns less than half its cost again.

Why both are presented. They can diverge, and each alone can mislead. A project with a very short payback but a short useful life may show a modest ROI. A project with a long payback but a long life may show a strong ROI while tying up capital for years. Presenting both lets a CFO see the trade-off between speed of recovery and total value. Here they happen to agree — project 2 dominates on both — so the choice is straightforward, and it is worth saying so rather than manufacturing complexity.

What neither captures. Four things, and naming them shows command of the method:

  • The time value of money. Both are simple and undiscounted — a dollar saved in year 5 is counted equally with one saved in year 1. A CFO applying a discount rate or computing NPV would see lower real returns, and the longer-payback project would be penalised more.
  • Risk and certainty of the savings. Savings dependent on a projected tariff, or on sustained behavioural compliance, are less certain than savings from decommissioned hardware.
  • Strategic and compliance value. A project may be required regardless of return — for a Bangladeshi bank, Bangladesh Bank's green guidelines make certain measures a compliance obligation, which no ROI figure reflects.
  • Carbon and embodied impact. Neither metric counts tonnes of CO₂e, and neither accounts for the embodied carbon of hardware the project itself purchases (Class 8). A project can have a strong financial ROI and a weak carbon case if it involves replacing serviceable equipment.

Conclusion. The correct practice is to present payback, ROI, and the carbon figure together — the first two for the CFO, the third for the ESG report — since the lecture's point is that a Green IT case must satisfy both audiences at once.

Question 10 · long answer

A commercial bank in Dhaka wants to reduce branch IT energy costs by 25%. Recommend the most effective policy-first initiative and justify it, including how you would build the case for the CFO and the board.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from The faculty's own corporate scenario from Section 7. The phrase "policy-first" is the key instruction — the examiner wants an answer that leads with governance and behaviour rather than hardware, drawing on Topic 02 (Kotter), Topic 03 (the business case) and Topic 05 (the Bangladesh drivers).

Recommendation. The faculty's answer, and the right one: centralised automated PC power management combined with thin-client deployment in branches. This cuts per-terminal draw from 150 W to 15 W while enforcing off-hours shutdown across the branch network.

Why this is the strongest option. Two independent multipliers compound. The thin clients cut the load by a factor of ten. The enforced shutdown policy cuts the operating hours — typically from 24/7 to a ten-hour working day, a further factor of about 2.4. Together these far exceed the 25% target, which means the programme can be phased and still hit its number. Crucially, the power management policy alone is free: it is a Group Policy configuration with essentially zero CapEx, delivering a large share of the benefit before any hardware is purchased. That makes it the ideal Kotter quick win (step 6) — rapid, visible, and requiring no capital request to demonstrate ROI.

Why "policy-first" is the right framing. The barrier to off-hours shutdown is almost never technical; it is organisational — a policy or habit that keeps machines always on, perhaps for overnight updates or remote access. Kotter's step 5 (empowerment) is precisely about removing policies that require always-on desktop hardware. The saving is already available and is being blocked by a rule, which is the clearest possible illustration of the lecture's claim that human factors account for 80% of the outcome.

Building the CFO case. Present it in the CFO's own terms, since CFOs do not approve projects on environmental goodwill:

  • Annual savings = kWh saved × tariff, with the tariff assumption stated explicitly — Bangladesh's commercial tariffs have risen steadily, so a sensitivity showing the saving improving if tariffs rise further strengthens the case.
  • Simple payback = CapEx ÷ annual savings. Present the policy component separately from the hardware component, since the policy element has effectively zero CapEx and immediate payback.
  • 5-year net ROI = (total savings − CapEx) ÷ CapEx × 100.
  • Carbon offset = (kWh × 0.6) ÷ 1,000 tonnes CO₂e.

The regulatory argument — decisive for a bank. This is not merely a cost programme. Bangladesh Bank green guidelines require commercial banks to implement in-house green office policies and to report annual Scope 1 and 2 footprints. Branch electricity is Scope 2, so the carbon figure is a reportable compliance deliverable, and the power management policy is itself an in-house green office policy. The banks are also required to allocate funds to green finance, which may create framing options for the capital component. A programme that satisfies a regulator and reduces cost is far easier to approve than one that only does the latter.

Making it stick — the part most answers omit. Without Phase 4 (Institutionalize) the gains will erode: staff will request exemptions, new machines will arrive with default settings, and the fleet will drift back. So the recommendation must include centralised enforcement rather than local configuration, Green IT KPIs linked to branch manager performance reviews (Kotter step 8), and periodic compliance reporting through the Green Steering Committee spanning IT, Facilities, Finance and HR. Savings should be reinvested in the next phase of the programme (step 7) rather than absorbed into general budget, so the initiative becomes self-funding.

One caution to include. Replacing 1,200 serviceable desktops carries substantial embodied carbon (Class 8, where manufacturing is 70–80% of a device's lifetime footprint). The circular hierarchy from Class 7 would ask first whether existing desktops can be repurposed as thin clients via software, or redeployed elsewhere, before new units are bought. Phasing the hardware replacement to coincide with the natural refresh cycle captures the operational saving without triggering an early manufacturing event — and the free policy change delivers benefit in the meantime.

Question 11 · long answer

You have been appointed to lead a Green IT transformation at a mid-sized Bangladeshi IT services company. Set out your programme across the transformation lifecycle, explaining what happens in each phase and how you would manage the human side.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from The synthesis question for Class 13 — it requires Topic 01 (the four phases), Topic 02 (Kotter), Topic 03 (metrics), Topic 04 (governance) and Topic 05 (local drivers) woven together. The best structure is to run the four phases in order and fold the Kotter steps into them where they naturally fall, rather than presenting two separate lists.

Framing. A Green IT transformation is an iterative lifecycle, not a project, and enterprise-wide maturity typically takes 3–5 years. I would plan on that horizon and say so up front, because a programme sold as a quick fix will be judged a failure at month eighteen.

Phase 1 — Assess & Plan

Establish Scope 1, 2 and 3 energy baselines — generator fuel, purchased electricity, and the embodied footprint of procured hardware plus e-waste. Nothing can be claimed later without a baseline now. Run a Maturity Model assessment (CMMI-aligned Green IT models) to establish where the organisation actually sits, and perform a gap analysis against target.

In parallel, Kotter steps 1 and 2. Create urgency using business risk, not environmental appeal: rising commercial electricity tariffs in Bangladesh directly eroding operating margins, regulatory compliance exposure, and reputational risk with EU/US clients who increasingly require supplier sustainability data. Then build the guiding coalition — a Green Steering Committee spanning IT, Facilities, Finance and HR. The cross-functional composition is essential: an IT-only committee cannot change procurement standards or performance review criteria, and will stall once it exhausts savings within its own authority.

Phase 2 — Architect & Implement

Deploy the low-hanging fruit first, both for the savings and for the political capital: server virtualisation and consolidation (Class 12 — targeting 65–80% utilisation on Type 1 hypervisors, then physically decommissioning surplus hosts) and PC power management policies, which cost essentially nothing. Simultaneously execute green procurement requirements — writing Energy Star, EPEAT Gold or TCO Certified and 80 PLUS Gold into hardware specifications (Class 8), which is the highest-leverage single act available given the procurement multiplier.

This is Kotter steps 3 to 6. Set a tangible vision on the lecture's model — "30% energy reduction in 24 months with zero performance compromise", where the second clause pre-empts the objection that sustainability costs performance. Communicate through departmental dashboards, town halls and a green champion network. Empower action by removing the policies that force always-on hardware or mandatory printing. And publicise the quick wins in dollars, because a visible saving buys permission for the harder phases.

Phase 3 — Measure & Optimize

Implement automated DCIM and IoT monitoring dashboards for continuous KPI tracking — PUE, utilisation ratios, kWh per unit of service delivered. Without instrumentation the programme is running blind and any later claim is unverifiable, which is itself a greenwashing risk. Refine continuously: revisit consolidation ratios, tune DRS and DPM thresholds, and check that procurement standards are actually being applied rather than waived under deadline pressure.

Engage SREDA at this stage for energy audit standards, and evaluate its rooftop solar guidance for the data centre, which addresses Scope 2 at source rather than by reduction alone.

Phase 4 — Institutionalize

This is the phase that determines whether any of the preceding work survives. Link Green IT KPIs to executive and departmental performance reviews, so the programme's continuation does not depend on my personal attention. Establish formal e-waste and recycling vendor audits with certified chain of custody (Class 7). Adopt ISO 14001 as the EMS framework, and publish annual ESG and sustainability reports using GHG Protocol scopes — which also positions the company well with European institutional clients.

Kotter steps 7 and 8 live here. Reinvest the energy cost savings into the next generation of sustainable technology rather than letting them disappear into general budget — this converts the programme into a self-funding cycle. And anchor it culturally by embedding green criteria into hiring, onboarding and annual reviews.

Managing the human side throughout

The single most important design principle: technology is only 20% of the risk; human factors are 80%, and 70% of digital transformations fail on change management and culture. Practically this means every technical intervention needs a matching organisational one — a virtualisation ratio needs an enforcement owner, a procurement standard needs someone authorised to reject a non-compliant bid, a sleep policy needs an exemption process that is deliberately slightly inconvenient. And I would guard explicitly against the "set and forget" risk: efficiency erodes because staff turn over, legacy hardware creeps back, and virtualisation ratios degrade. Scheduled re-audits and KPI ownership are the countermeasure.

Reporting to the board

Every proposal goes forward with CapEx, annual OpEx savings, simple payback period, 5-year net ROI, and tonnes of CO₂e avoided. The first four are for the CFO; the last is for the ESG report and, for clients who ask, for the supplier questionnaire. Presenting both at once is the practical expression of the lecture's core claim — sustainability must map directly to cost reduction, risk mitigation and ESG compliance if it is to survive a budget cycle.

Question 12 · long answer · stretch

Two years after a successful virtualisation programme, an organisation finds its consolidation ratio has fallen from 12:1 to 6:1, and its server count is climbing again. Diagnose what has happened and set out how to fix it.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from This is the "set and forget" risk (Topic 01) made concrete, combined with the virtual sprawl warning the faculty gave in the Class 13 opening. It tests whether you understand that Phase 4 exists to prevent a specific, predictable failure — and it rewards a diagnosis before a prescription.

Diagnosis: this is virtual sprawl, and it is the predicted consequence of stopping at Phase 3. The faculty warned of it explicitly when bridging from Class 12: "high consolidation ratios maximise hardware yield, but unmanaged VM sprawl creates phantom power waste." The programme achieved its technical goal and then failed to institutionalise it.

The mechanism. Virtualisation makes provisioning almost frictionless — the lecture contrasts legacy provisioning measured in weeks with VM provisioning in minutes. That is a genuine benefit, but it removes the natural brake that used to constrain server growth. When creating a VM required a purchase order, a procurement cycle and rack space, the cost was visible and someone had to approve it. When it takes a few clicks, nobody sees a cost at all. The result is predictable:

  • Zombie VMs — machines created for a project, test or migration that ended, never decommissioned because no one owns the decision to delete them.
  • Over-provisioning — VMs allocated far more vCPU and RAM than they use, because requesting generously is free to the requester and protects them against complaint.
  • Ratio degradation — as allocated resources rise, fewer VMs fit per host, so new hosts are purchased. The ratio halving from 12:1 to 6:1 is precisely this.

The consequence is that the organisation is paying twice: the idle-power baseline of the additional hosts (Class 12 — an idle server still draws 50–70% of peak), multiplied by the facility PUE, plus the embodied carbon and capital of hardware it did not need (Class 8).

Which phase failed. This is a clean Phase 4 (Institutionalize) failure. The lecture names three erosion mechanisms and two are visibly present: legacy hardware creeps back in and virtualisation ratios degrade without active enforcement. The third — staff turn over — is likely also a factor two years on, with the original programme knowledge having left. In Kotter terms, step 8 (anchor in culture) was never completed: nobody's performance review depends on the ratio, so nobody defends it.

The fix, in order:

  1. Measure before acting. Reinstate Phase 3 instrumentation — DCIM and monitoring dashboards tracking consolidation ratio, per-host utilisation, VM count and allocated-versus-consumed resources. You cannot govern what is not visible, and the current situation exists because visibility lapsed.
  2. Reclaim. Run a VM audit to identify zombie and over-provisioned VMs. Right-size allocations to actual consumption, decommission the orphans, and re-consolidate onto fewer hosts. Then physically decommission the surplus hardware — powering it off is not enough, since it will be quietly re-energised.
  3. Introduce lifecycle governance for VMs. Every VM gets a named owner, a business justification and an expiry date requiring active renewal. Default-expire rather than default-persist: the burden of proof should sit with keeping a VM, not with deleting it.
  4. Reintroduce friction deliberately. A lightweight approval or internal chargeback for VM provisioning restores the cost signal that virtualisation removed. Chargeback is particularly effective because it makes the consuming department feel the expense.
  5. Anchor it (Kotter step 8). Add consolidation ratio and VM hygiene to the IT department's KPIs and performance reviews, with periodic reporting to the Green Steering Committee. This is the step that prevents recurrence.
  6. Enable DPM with hysteresis so off-peak demand automatically consolidates further and idle hosts enter deep standby — an automated backstop that partially compensates for lapses in human discipline.

The general lesson to close on. This case demonstrates why the lifecycle has four phases rather than three. Phases 1 to 3 deliver the gain; Phase 4 is what makes it permanent. An organisation that treats institutionalisation as optional does not merely plateau — it regresses, and it pays for the same savings twice. Governance is not overhead on top of the technical work; it is the component that makes the technical work durable.
