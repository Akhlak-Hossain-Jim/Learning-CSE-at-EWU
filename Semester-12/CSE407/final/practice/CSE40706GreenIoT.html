How to use this guide

Class 16 — Green IoT Technologies (Part 1). Lecture slides — Green IoT. Course outcome CO3.

This class turns away from the data centre and toward the edge — billions of small devices, each drawing almost nothing. The faculty's framing was that at planetary scale, "almost nothing" adds up, and that the term "Green IoT" is used in two different senses that are routinely confused.

The one-sentence version of Class 16

A battery-powered sensor spends over 99% of its life asleep, so its energy budget is dominated by sleep current rather than active current — and every design decision, from the microcontroller chosen to the radio protocol used, follows from that single fact.

The maths here is the simplest of the six topics — the faculty said so directly: "duty cycle, battery life, coverage area and harvested energy are all short, direct calculations once you know the formula." The risk is therefore not difficulty but unit handling. Almost every mistake in this topic is a milliamp/microamp or seconds/minutes conversion error, so the worked solutions below flag every conversion explicitly.

Topic 01

The Green IoT paradox — two meanings of one term

Getting this distinction right is worth easy marks, and mixing them up is a common failure.

"Green IoT" is used in two related but distinct senses, and the faculty's opening point was that mixing them up causes real confusion in research and industry alike. Both matter.

Greening of IoTGreening by IoT
Making the IoT devices and networks themselves energy-efficient: low-power hardware, efficient protocols, energy harvesting, and responsible end-of-life recycling for the devices. Using IoT sensing and control to reduce energy use elsewhere: smart thermostats, smart grids, precision irrigation, leak detection, traffic optimisation — IoT as a sustainability tool for other systems.
The paradox in one line (the faculty's own phrasing)

IoT can be an enormous tool for sustainability while simultaneously being an enormous source of energy and e-waste burden if devices, networks and data pipelines are not designed efficiently. Both statements are true at once — that is what makes it a paradox rather than simply a trade-off.

The four supporting figures

  • 1.5% — estimated share of global electricity consumption attributable to ICT devices and networks including IoT (the faculty noted this varies by study).
  • 30%+ — potential energy savings in a building from smart sensing and automated HVAC/lighting control. This is the "greening by" side of the ledger.
  • 1000s — sensor nodes deployable for the embodied-carbon cost of a single server. The faculty called this "a real trade-off to weigh".
  • 2–5 years — typical replacement cycle for poorly designed IoT devices, which is a major e-waste driver at scale. This is the "greening of" side, and it links directly back to Class 7.

Also worth memorising from the title slide: 29 billion connected IoT devices projected worldwide by 2030, under 1 W typical power draw of a low-power sensor node, 10+ years of battery life achievable with proper low-power design, and 15 km+ typical LoRaWAN range in open rural environments.

Watch out — the trade-off hidden in "1000s of nodes per server"

That figure cuts both ways and a good answer says so. It means IoT monitoring is cheap in embodied-carbon terms relative to the compute infrastructure it feeds — a strong argument for deploying sensing. But multiply thousands of nodes by a 2–5 year replacement cycle from poor design and you have generated a substantial e-waste stream that did not exist before. The resolution is exactly what the rest of the lecture teaches: design for 10+ year life so the "greening by" benefit is not cancelled by the "greening of" cost.

Topic 02

Low-power hardware design and the 1% rule

The power-state table and three design techniques. This is the conceptual core of the class.

Start with the design problem. A battery-powered sensor that must run for years cannot simply stay on. The entire design philosophy revolves around spending as much time as possible in the deepest sleep state the application allows, waking briefly only to sense, compute and transmit.

Key idea — the 1% rule

Well-designed IoT sensor nodes are often active for well under 1% of their lifetime. The faculty's worked example: a soil-moisture sensor that wakes for 100 milliseconds every 10 minutes is awake roughly 0.017% of the time. The consequence is the one to remember — nearly all of its energy budget is spent on sleep current, not active current.

The five power states

Power stateTypical currentWhat is running
Active / transmit10–120 mA CPU at full clock, radio transmitting — the most power-hungry state, kept as brief as possible
Active / compute only1–15 mA CPU processing sensor readings, radio off
Idle0.5–2 mA CPU clocked down, peripherals mostly off, waiting for a timer or interrupt
Sleep / standby1–50 µA Only a real-time clock and wake logic powered; RAM often retained
Deep sleep / shutdown0.02–1 µA Nearly everything off; wake only via external reset or long timer
Watch out — the unit change between rows

Look carefully at the units. The top three states are in milliamps (mA); the bottom two are in microamps (µA). That is a factor of 1,000, and it is where the arithmetic goes wrong. Transmitting at 120 mA versus deep sleep at 1 µA is a ratio of 120,000:1 — which is exactly why duty cycling works so dramatically, and why a single missed conversion turns a 20-year answer into a 20-day one.

The four design techniques

  • Duty cycling — sensing and radio hardware are powered on only for short, scheduled bursts, then returned to sleep. The faculty called this the single biggest lever for battery life.
  • Event-driven wake — instead of polling on a timer, hardware interrupts (motion, a threshold crossing) wake the device only when something actually changes. Strictly better than timer polling when events are rare.
  • Radio efficiency — radio transmission is typically the single most expensive operation per event, so minimising payload size and transmission frequency matters more than CPU efficiency in most designs. This is a counter-intuitive point and a likely exam question: optimise the radio before the processor.
  • Right-sized compute — ultra-low-power microcontrollers, not general-purpose CPUs, chosen specifically for their sleep-current specifications, sometimes trading raw speed for standby efficiency.
Topic 03

LPWAN protocols — LoRaWAN, NB-IoT, Zigbee, BLE

A four-way comparison table. Learn the table, and learn which application each one is for.

No single wireless protocol is best for every IoT application — each trades off range, power, bandwidth and cost differently. The faculty was direct about the stakes: choosing the wrong one is one of the most common (and expensive) green-IoT design mistakes.

ProtocolTypical rangePower Data rateSpectrum
LoRaWAN2–15+ km Very low0.3–50 kbpsUnlicensed
NB-IoT1–10 km Very lowUp to ~250 kbpsLicensed cellular
Zigbee10–100 m per hop LowUp to 250 kbpsUnlicensed (mesh)
BLE10–50 m Very lowUp to ~2 MbpsUnlicensed

What each one is actually for

LoRaWAN — Long Range, Wide Area Network. Unlicensed spectrum, so it is operator-independent: you own the gateways and pay no carrier. Range is 2–5 km urban and 15+ km rural line-of-sight. Power is extremely low — years of battery life on a coin cell for infrequent, small messages. Designed for small, infrequent payloads, not streaming. Best for: agricultural sensors, rural utility metering, environmental monitoring across large unpowered areas.

NB-IoT — Narrowband IoT. Licensed cellular spectrum, running on existing telecom towers, so it benefits from dense coverage with no new gateways to build or maintain. Power is very low, though somewhat higher than LoRaWAN. Best for: smart metering and asset tracking where telecom coverage already exists.

Zigbee — a mesh networking protocol with a self-healing topology. Range is only 10–100 m per hop, but devices relay for each other, so a mesh can span a whole building. Note the power nuance the faculty gave: relay nodes (routers) draw more than simple end devices, which stay in deep sleep. Best for: dense indoor deployments — smart lighting, home automation, building sensor networks.

BLE — Bluetooth Low Energy. Short range and ubiquitous smartphone compatibility. The fastest data rate of the four at up to ~2 Mbps, useful for occasional bulk syncs. Very low power for advertisement and beacon use cases. Best for: wearables, proximity beacons, and any device that syncs directly with a nearby smartphone.

Watch out — the selection logic to apply

Do not memorise the table alone; memorise the decision. Ask three questions in order. (1) How far apart are the nodes? Kilometres → LoRaWAN or NB-IoT. Metres → Zigbee or BLE. (2) Is there existing cellular coverage, and do you want to avoid running gateways? Yes → NB-IoT. No, or you want operator independence → LoRaWAN. (3) Indoors and dense, or paired to a phone? Mesh through a building → Zigbee. Talks to a phone, or needs the higher data rate → BLE. Answering in that order gets you the right protocol nearly every time.

▶ What is LoRaWAN Technology? Why it helps: LoRaWAN is the protocol the Bangladesh scenarios keep landing on, and its architecture — end device to gateway to network server — is much easier to hold in your head once you have seen it drawn. It also makes the gateway-coverage calculation in Practice A question 3 feel concrete rather than abstract.

Topic 04

Energy harvesting

Four sources, and why this matters more at fleet scale than per device.

The faculty's opening line for this section is the one to quote: "the greenest battery is the one you never have to replace or dispose of." When paired with the ultra-low power budgets from Topic 02, small amounts of harvested ambient energy can keep a sensor running indefinitely.

  • Photovoltaic (solar) — small solar cells, even indoors under artificial light, can generate 10–200 mW outdoors — often more than enough to trickle-charge a supercapacitor or small battery for a duty-cycled sensor.
  • Piezoelectric (vibration) — converts mechanical vibration or strain into electricity. Used on industrial machinery, bridges, and railway sensors where vibration is constant and free.
  • Thermoelectric — harvests energy from temperature differentials, for example a pipe's surface against ambient air. Useful in industrial monitoring near heat sources.
  • RF harvesting — captures ambient radio-frequency energy from Wi-Fi or cellular signals. Powers only extremely low-draw devices, but requires zero dedicated infrastructure.
Key idea — why this matters at scale

The faculty's fleet-scale argument is the answer to any "why does this matter" question. A sensor needing a battery swap every 18 months versus one lasting 10 years is not a convenience difference. Across a 10,000-node deployment, it is the difference between roughly 6,700 truck-rolls and battery replacements per year versus almost none. Design choices at the single-device level compound enormously at fleet scale — in maintenance cost, in transport emissions, and in battery waste.

Watch out — harvesting only works with the power budget already solved

Harvesting is not an alternative to low-power design; it is a complement to it. The lecture is explicit that harvesting works "when paired with the ultra-low power budgets from the previous section". A solar panel generating 300 mWh/day cannot sustain a device that draws continuously at 30 mA — that would need roughly 100 mWh per hour. Get the duty cycle right first, then harvest to cover what remains. An exam answer proposing harvesting without mentioning duty cycling has the logic backwards.

Topic 05

The Bangladesh context

The faculty's argument that Bangladesh is an ideal proving ground for Green IoT.

Bangladesh is, in the faculty's framing, an ideal proving ground for Green IoT — a large agricultural sector, real flood risk to monitor, and a rural population not always well served by continuous grid power. The key line: low-power design here is not a nice-to-have; it is often the only thing that makes deployment feasible at all.

  • Smart agriculture. Soil moisture and irrigation sensors across dispersed farmland benefit directly from LoRaWAN's long range and multi-year battery life — no mains power and no frequent site visits needed.
  • Flood and water-level sensing. Remote river and embankment sensors in flood-prone regions must survive years unattended, often solar-powered, reporting infrequently to conserve energy — exactly the low-duty-cycle design pattern from Topic 02.
  • Cellular coverage advantage. Bangladesh's relatively dense mobile network coverage makes NB-IoT attractive for metering and tracking — no private gateway infrastructure to build.
  • Off-grid power reality. Rural and even some urban deployment sites cannot rely on continuous mains power, so solar or battery-only operation is not optional — it is the default design constraint from day one.
The protocol choice in the Bangladesh context

Note that the local section points at both long-range protocols for different reasons, and a good answer distinguishes them. LoRaWAN for dispersed agricultural and flood-monitoring deployments where there may be no reliable cellular coverage and you want operator independence and maximum battery life. NB-IoT for metering and asset tracking in areas already covered by the dense mobile network, where avoiding the cost and maintenance of private gateways is the deciding factor.

Practice A

Numerical practice — worked line by line

These extend the faculty's own five math cards. Watch the unit conversions — that is where marks are lost here.

Question 1 · easy · warm-up

A sensor wakes up and is active for 2 seconds every 10 minutes. What is its duty cycle as a percentage, and what fraction of its life is spent asleep?

Show worked solution

  1. \( t_{\text{active}} = 2\ \text{s},\quad T_{\text{period}} = 10\ \text{min} \)

    Active time and the repeat period. Note they are in different units — that must be fixed before dividing.

  2. \( T = 10 \times 60 = 600\ \text{s} \)

    Convert minutes to seconds (×60) so both quantities share a unit. Dividing 2 by 10 would give 20%, which is wrong by a factor of 60.

  3. \( \text{Duty cycle} = \dfrac{t_{\text{active}}}{T} \times 100\% \)

    Definition of duty cycle: the fraction of each period spent active, expressed as a percentage.

  4. \( = \dfrac{2}{600} \times 100\% \approx 0.33\% \)

    Divide and scale. The seconds cancel, leaving a dimensionless percentage.

  5. \( \text{asleep} = 100\% - 0.33\% = 99.67\% \)

    Complement: the device is either active or asleep, so the two shares sum to 100%.

Answer

Duty cycle ≈ 0.33%; the sensor spends 99.67% of its life asleep.

The faculty's interpretation is the part that earns the analytical mark: this is exactly why sleep current, not active current, dominates the total energy budget. This duty cycle also satisfies the 1% rule — well-designed nodes are active for well under 1% of their lifetime. Sanity check: 2 seconds out of 600 is roughly 1 part in 300, and 1/300 ≈ 0.33%.

Question 2 · easy · warm-up

A sensor draws a steady 5 µA in deep sleep (ignore brief wake spikes). Its battery is rated 1000 mAh. Roughly how many years will it last?

Show worked solution

  1. \( C = 1000\ \text{mAh},\quad I = 5\ \mu\text{A} \)

    Battery capacity and steady current. Again the units differ — mAh against µA — so a conversion is required first.

  2. \( I = 5\ \mu\text{A} = 0.005\ \text{mA} \)

    Convert µA to mA by dividing by 1,000, because capacity is in milliamp-hours. This single step is the most common source of error in the whole topic.

  3. \( t = \dfrac{C}{I} \)

    Definition: capacity divided by current gives time. Units: mAh ÷ mA = h, which confirms the conversion was done correctly.

  4. \( t = \dfrac{1000}{0.005} = 200{,}000\ \text{h} \)

    Divide. Dividing by a small number gives a large answer — expected here, since 5 µA is a tiny draw.

  5. \( t = \dfrac{200{,}000}{8{,}760} \approx 22.8\ \text{years} \)

    Convert hours to years by dividing by 8,760 (the hours in a year) — the same constant used throughout the data centre topics.

Answer

≈ 22.8 years.

The faculty's own caveat, which belongs in your answer: in practice self-discharge and wake spikes shorten this — a real coin cell loses charge on its own even with nothing connected. But the figure demonstrates the point that a well-designed sleep current can make the battery outlast the product itself, which connects straight to the fleet-scale argument in Topic 04. Sanity check on units: if you had forgotten the µA→mA conversion you would get 200 hours, or about 8 days — an answer so obviously wrong for a deep-sleep sensor that it should catch itself.

Question 3 · easy · warm-up

A LoRaWAN gateway has a usable range of 15 km in every direction. (a) Approximately how large an area, in km², can one gateway cover? (b) How many gateways would be needed for a 3,000 km² rural district, ignoring overlap?

Show worked solution

  1. \( r = 15\ \text{km} \)

    The range in every direction is the radius of a circular coverage area, not the diameter — reading it as diameter would quarter the answer.

  2. \( A = \pi r^2 \)

    Area of a circle. Coverage radiating equally in all directions from a point is a disc.

  3. \( A = \pi \times 15^2 = \pi \times 225 \)

    Square the radius first, then multiply by π — order of operations.

  4. \( A \approx 706.9\ \text{km}^2 \)

    Evaluate. Units: km × km = km², as required for an area.

  5. \( n = \dfrac{3000}{706.9} \approx 4.24 \Rightarrow 5\ \text{gateways} \)

    Total area ÷ coverage per gateway, then round up — four gateways would leave a gap, and you cannot install a fraction of one.

Answer

(a) ≈ 707 km² per gateway · (b) 5 gateways (rounding up from 4.24).

The faculty's note: this is why a single LoRaWAN gateway can economically cover a large rural district with a sparse network of sensors. Add a caveat for full marks — real deployments need more than the ideal figure, because circles do not tile without overlap, terrain and vegetation reduce effective range below the line-of-sight maximum, and redundant coverage is usually wanted for reliability. Treat 5 as a lower bound. Sanity check: 707 km² is roughly a 26 km × 26 km square, which is plausible for a 15 km radius.

Question 4 · medium · core

A network has 500 sensors, each sending a 20-byte message every 10 minutes. (a) How many messages does the network send per day? (b) How much total data per day? (c) Comment on the result.

Show worked solution

  1. \( 1\ \text{day} = 24 \times 60 = 1{,}440\ \text{min} \)

    Convert the day into the same unit as the message interval, so the two can be divided.

  2. \( \text{msgs per sensor} = \dfrac{1{,}440}{10} = 144 \)

    Period ÷ interval gives the number of transmissions each sensor makes in a day.

  3. \( \text{total msgs} = 144 \times 500 = 72{,}000 \)

    Per-sensor count × number of sensors scales to the whole network.

  4. \( D = 72{,}000 \times 20\ \text{B} = 1{,}440{,}000\ \text{B} \)

    Message count × payload size gives total bytes. Units: messages × B/message = B.

  5. \( D = \dfrac{1{,}440{,}000}{1{,}000{,}000} = 1.44\ \text{MB/day} \)

    Convert bytes to megabytes. Using the decimal convention (10⁶ B per MB) as the faculty's own solution does; the binary convention would give 1.37 MiB — state which you used.

Answer

(a) 72,000 messages/day · (b) ≈ 1.44 MB/day.

(c) The faculty's comment is the point of the question: despite 500 devices running continuously, the entire network's daily traffic is smaller than a single typical photo — a hallmark of well-designed IoT traffic. This is also why LoRaWAN's modest 0.3–50 kbps data rate is not a limitation for this class of application: the constraint that matters is range and power, not bandwidth. It further explains why minimising payload size is one of the four low-power design techniques — every byte transmitted costs radio energy, which is the most expensive operation per event.

Question 5 · medium · core

A small solar panel produces an average of 50 mW over 6 hours of usable sunlight per day. (a) How much energy in mWh does it harvest daily? (b) A coin cell holds about 1000 mAh at 3 V — how does one day's harvest compare with the cell's total stored energy?

Show worked solution

  1. \( P = 50\ \text{mW},\quad t = 6\ \text{h} \)

    Average harvested power and the usable window. "Average over 6 hours" means the variation within that window is already accounted for.

  2. \( E = P \times t = 50 \times 6 = 300\ \text{mWh/day} \)

    Energy = power × time. Units: mW × h = mWh, exactly what was asked for.

  3. \( E_{\text{cell}} = C \times V = 1000\ \text{mAh} \times 3\ \text{V} \)

    Energy = charge × voltage. A capacity in mAh is a charge, not an energy — it must be multiplied by the cell voltage to be comparable with mWh.

  4. \( E_{\text{cell}} = 3{,}000\ \text{mWh} \)

    Multiply. Units: mAh × V = mWh, which confirms the two quantities are now directly comparable.

  5. \( \dfrac{3{,}000}{300} = 10\ \text{days} \)

    Ratio of the two energies tells you how many days of harvesting equal the cell's entire stored energy.

Answer

(a) 300 mWh/day. (b) The coin cell holds about 3,000 mWh, so ten days of harvesting equals the cell's entire lifetime stored energy.

The faculty's framing: one good day of harvesting can rival an entire battery's stored energy — and over a device's life, harvesting delivers many multiples of what any single cell could store. That is the argument for "the greenest battery is the one you never have to replace". Note the step that catches people: mAh is a charge, not an energy. You cannot compare 1000 mAh directly with 300 mWh without multiplying by voltage. Practical caveat worth adding: the harvest must be buffered in a supercapacitor or rechargeable cell to cover nights and cloudy days, which is exactly the design the flood-sensor scenario calls for.

Question 6 · hard · stretch

A soil-moisture node wakes for 4 seconds every 15 minutes. During the wake it draws 28 mA; in deep sleep it draws 6 µA. Battery capacity is 1200 mAh.

(a) Duty cycle. (b) Average current. (c) Estimated battery life in years. (d) What proportion of the energy budget goes to active versus sleep? (e) If the wake time were halved to 2 s, what is the new battery life — and what does the comparison tell you?

Show worked solution

  1. \( t_a = 4\ \text{s},\quad T = 15 \times 60 = 900\ \text{s} \)

    Convert the period to seconds first so both times share a unit.

  2. \( d = \dfrac{4}{900} = 0.00444 = 0.444\% \)

    Duty cycle = active ÷ period. Comfortably under the 1% rule threshold, as a well-designed node should be.

  3. \( I_{\text{sleep}} = 6\ \mu\text{A} = 0.006\ \text{mA} \)

    Convert µA to mA so both currents are in the same unit as the mAh capacity.

  4. \( I_{\text{avg}} = d \times I_a + (1-d) \times I_s \)

    Time-weighted average: the device spends fraction \(d\) drawing the active current and fraction \(1-d\) drawing the sleep current. This is the correct way to combine two currents over time.

  5. \( = 0.00444 \times 28 + 0.99556 \times 0.006 \)

    Substitute the duty cycle and the two currents, both now in mA.

  6. \( = 0.1244 + 0.00597 = 0.1304\ \text{mA} \)

    Evaluate each term separately before adding — keeping them apart is what makes part (d) immediate.

  7. \( t = \dfrac{1200}{0.1304} = 9{,}202\ \text{h} \)

    Capacity ÷ average current = time. Units: mAh ÷ mA = h.

  8. \( t = \dfrac{9{,}202}{8{,}760} \approx 1.05\ \text{years} \)

    Convert hours to years (÷8,760).

  9. \( \dfrac{0.1244}{0.1304} = 95.4\%,\quad \dfrac{0.00597}{0.1304} = 4.6\% \)

    Each term as a share of the total average current. Because both are averaged over the same period, the current shares equal the energy shares.

  10. \( d' = \dfrac{2}{900} = 0.00222 \)

    Recompute the duty cycle with the halved wake time.

  11. \( I'_{\text{avg}} = 0.00222 \times 28 + 0.99778 \times 0.006 = 0.0682\ \text{mA} \)

    Same time-weighted average with the new duty cycle. The active term halves; the sleep term barely moves.

  12. \( t' = \dfrac{1200}{0.0682 \times 8{,}760} \approx 2.01\ \text{years} \)

    Capacity ÷ current, converted to years in one step. Life has almost exactly doubled.

Answer

(a) 0.444% · (b) ≈ 0.130 mA average · (c) ≈ 1.05 years · (d) ≈ 95.4% active, 4.6% sleep · (e) ≈ 2.01 years — halving the wake time nearly doubles battery life.

The interesting result, and the reason this is a stretch question: here the active current dominates the budget at 95%, which appears to contradict the lecture's claim that sleep current dominates. It does not — it shows the claim is conditional on the design. In question 2, sleep was the entire budget because the sensor essentially never woke. Here the node wakes for 4 s every 15 min at a relatively hungry 28 mA, so the active term dominates.

The design lesson: always check which term dominates before optimising, because it determines where effort should go. Here, cutting wake time is highly effective (part e proves it), while switching to a lower-sleep-current microcontroller would buy almost nothing — even driving sleep current to zero would only extend life by about 4.6%. Had sleep dominated, the priorities would reverse. This is also why radio efficiency is listed as a design technique: shortening the transmit burst is usually the highest-leverage change available.

Sanity check: 1 year is short for an IoT node — a real deployment would extend it by reducing wake time, reporting less often, adding a solar harvester, or all three. That is precisely the design conversation the flood-monitoring scenario asks for.

Practice B

Analytical questions

Question 7 · short answer

Explain the Green IoT paradox, distinguishing clearly between the two senses of the term, and give an example of each.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from Topic 01, the foundation section. This is a definition question with a twist — the examiner wants the tension between the two senses, not just two definitions side by side. Name both, give examples, then state why holding both at once is a paradox rather than a simple trade-off.

The two senses. "Green IoT" is used in two related but distinct ways, and the lecture was explicit that confusing them causes real problems in research and industry.

Greening of IoT means making the IoT devices and networks themselves energy-efficient: low-power hardware design, efficient wireless protocols, energy harvesting, and responsible end-of-life recycling for the devices. Example: designing a soil-moisture node that duty-cycles at under 1%, uses an ultra-low-power microcontroller selected for its sleep current, transmits small payloads over LoRaWAN, and runs on a solar harvester so its battery never needs replacing.

Greening by IoT means using IoT sensing and control to reduce energy use elsewhere in other systems. Example: smart building sensing with automated HVAC and lighting control, which the lecture puts at 30%+ potential energy savings for a building. Other examples given: smart grids, precision irrigation, leak detection, traffic optimisation.

The paradox itself. The faculty's own formulation is that IoT can be an enormous tool for sustainability while simultaneously being an enormous source of energy and e-waste burden if devices, networks and data pipelines are not designed efficiently. Both propositions are true at the same time, about the same technology — which is what makes it a paradox rather than a straightforward cost-benefit calculation.

Why it is a live tension and not an academic one. Two figures from the lecture make it concrete. First, thousands of sensor nodes can be deployed for the embodied-carbon cost of a single server, which argues strongly for deploying sensing widely. Second, the typical replacement cycle for poorly designed IoT devices is 2–5 years, which the lecture calls a major e-waste driver at scale. Multiply a large fleet by a short replacement cycle and the e-waste burden can erase the savings the deployment was justified on. Note also that ICT devices and networks including IoT already account for an estimated 1.5% of global electricity consumption.

The resolution. The two senses are not actually in opposition — they are sequential. Greening of IoT is the precondition for greening by IoT to deliver a net benefit. A deployment designed for 10+ year life with harvested power delivers its sustainability benefit without generating a compensating waste stream; one designed carelessly does not. Which is exactly why the rest of Class 16 is about power states, duty cycling, protocol selection and harvesting.

Question 8 · short answer

A designer proposes optimising their sensor firmware's computation to run 40% faster, in order to extend battery life. Evaluate this proposal.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from Topic 02, specifically the radio efficiency design technique and the power-state table. The proposal sounds sensible and is the intuitive move for a software engineer — the examiner wants to know whether you have absorbed the counter-intuitive point that the radio, not the CPU, usually dominates.

The proposal is not wrong, but it is very likely optimising the wrong thing.

Why. The lecture's design guidance is explicit: radio transmission is typically the single most expensive operation per event, so minimising payload size and transmission frequency matters more than CPU efficiency in most designs. The power-state table shows why. Active/transmit — CPU at full clock with the radio transmitting — draws 10–120 mA. Active/compute only — CPU processing with the radio off — draws just 1–15 mA. So the compute-only state can be an order of magnitude cheaper than the transmit state. Making the compute phase 40% faster shortens the cheaper of the two active phases, leaving the expensive one untouched.

What to check first. Before any optimisation, compute the time-weighted average current and determine which term actually dominates the budget: sleep, compute, or transmit. That single calculation determines where effort should go, and without it any optimisation is guesswork. If the node has a very low duty cycle, sleep current may dominate and neither active optimisation will matter much. If it transmits often, the radio will dominate.

Higher-leverage alternatives, roughly in order:

  • Reduce transmission frequency — report every 30 minutes instead of every 10, or transmit only when a reading changes meaningfully. This attacks the largest term directly.
  • Reduce payload size — send deltas rather than absolute values, pack fields tightly, drop redundant headers. Every byte costs radio-on time.
  • Aggregate locally. This is where the firmware work genuinely pays off — using the cheap compute state to average or filter several readings and send one message instead of several trades expensive radio time for cheap CPU time. That is a much better use of the developer's effort than making the computation faster.
  • Switch to event-driven wake — hardware interrupts on a threshold crossing rather than timer polling, so the device wakes only when something actually changes.
  • Deepen the sleep state — moving from sleep/standby (1–50 µA) to deep sleep/shutdown (0.02–1 µA) is up to a 50× reduction in the baseline, if the application can tolerate losing RAM retention.

One genuine benefit of the proposal. Faster computation does shorten the active window, and if the radio is idling powered-on during that computation, the saving is real and larger than the compute-only figures suggest. So the answer is not "don't do it" — it is measure first, and expect the radio to be where the budget actually goes.

Question 9 · short answer

Compare LoRaWAN, NB-IoT, Zigbee and BLE. For each, state the deployment scenario it suits and explain what makes it suitable.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from Topic 03. A four-way comparison, which means structure matters more than volume. Give the discriminating characteristics rather than reciting every cell of the table, and make sure each protocol's reason for suiting its use case is explicit — the lecture's warning is that choosing wrongly is one of the most common and expensive green-IoT mistakes.

The overarching point: no single protocol is best for every application. Each trades off range, power, bandwidth and cost differently, so selection is an engineering decision made against the deployment, not a preference.

LoRaWAN — sparse, wide-area, unpowered deployments. Range of 2–5 km urban and 15+ km rural line-of-sight, with extremely low power giving years of battery life on a coin cell. It runs on unlicensed spectrum, making it operator-independent — you own the gateways and pay no carrier fees. Data rate is only 0.3–50 kbps, designed for small infrequent payloads rather than streaming. Suits: agricultural sensors, rural utility metering, environmental monitoring across large unpowered areas. Why: a single gateway covers roughly 700 km², so one or two gateways can serve an entire district, and the low power budget pairs naturally with solar or multi-year batteries where no mains supply exists.

NB-IoT — metering and tracking where cellular already reaches. Range 1–10 km, benefiting from existing dense tower coverage, with no new gateways to build or maintain. Runs on licensed cellular spectrum, so quality of service is managed but a carrier subscription is required. Power is very low, though somewhat higher than LoRaWAN; data rate up to ~250 kbps gives more headroom. Suits: smart metering and asset tracking. Why: where coverage exists, the infrastructure burden disappears entirely — the trade is carrier dependence and slightly higher power in exchange for zero gateway maintenance.

Zigbee — dense indoor networks. Only 10–100 m per hop, but it is a self-healing mesh in which devices relay for each other, so a mesh can span a whole building. Up to 250 kbps. Note the power nuance: relay nodes (routers) draw more than simple end devices, which stay in deep sleep — so a mesh is not uniformly low-power, and routers usually need mains power. Suits: smart lighting, home automation, building sensor networks. Why: indoor environments defeat long-range radio through walls and structure, and mesh relaying solves that by hopping rather than by raw transmit power.

BLE — anything that talks to a phone. 10–50 m, personal-area rather than wide-area, with ubiquitous smartphone compatibility and the highest data rate of the four at up to ~2 Mbps, useful for occasional bulk syncs. Very low power for advertisement and beacon use. Suits: wearables, proximity beacons, devices syncing directly with a nearby phone. Why: the receiver infrastructure already exists in everyone's pocket, so there is no network to deploy at all.

The selection logic in three questions: How far apart are the nodes — kilometres or metres? If kilometres, is there cellular coverage you can exploit (NB-IoT) or do you want operator independence and maximum battery life (LoRaWAN)? If metres, does it need to mesh through a building (Zigbee) or pair with a phone (BLE)?

Question 10 · long answer

A team wants to deploy 200 soil-moisture sensors across a large rice field in Bangladesh with no mains power nearby. Recommend a protocol and a power strategy, and justify both.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from The faculty's own deployment scenario from Section 7. It requires Topic 03 (protocol selection) and Topic 02 (low-power design) applied to the Bangladesh constraints in Topic 05. The examiner wants both halves of the question answered — protocol and power strategy — with the reasoning shown, not just the conclusion.

Recommended protocol: LoRaWAN.

Justification. Apply the selection logic. The nodes are dispersed across a large field, so this is a kilometre-scale rather than metre-scale problem, which rules out Zigbee (10–100 m per hop, and its relay nodes draw more power and would need their own supply) and BLE (10–50 m, and designed to pair with a nearby phone). That leaves the two long-range options.

Between them, LoRaWAN wins here on three grounds. Its range — 15+ km rural line-of-sight — means one or two gateways cover the entire field; at a 15 km radius a single gateway covers roughly 700 km². Its power profile is the lowest of the four, giving years of battery life on a coin cell for infrequent small messages, which matters enormously when there is no mains power and 200 nodes to maintain. And it runs on unlicensed spectrum, so it is operator-independent — no carrier subscription for 200 devices, and no dependence on cellular coverage reaching every corner of the field.

Its data rate of 0.3–50 kbps is no constraint at all for this application. Soil moisture is a few bytes reported occasionally: 200 nodes sending 20-byte messages every 10 minutes would generate well under a megabyte a day across the whole network.

When NB-IoT would be the better choice instead — worth stating, because it shows judgement rather than pattern-matching. If the field sits inside good cellular coverage and the team would rather not own and maintain gateways, NB-IoT becomes attractive: the lecture notes that Bangladesh's relatively dense mobile coverage makes NB-IoT attractive for metering and tracking precisely because there is no private gateway infrastructure to maintain. The trade is a carrier subscription per device and slightly higher power draw.

Power strategy: solar harvesting with aggressive duty cycling. These are not alternatives — the design only works with both, and harvesting must sit on top of a power budget that has already been minimised.

  • Aggressive duty cycling first. Soil moisture changes slowly, so reporting every 15–30 minutes rather than continuously is entirely adequate. This puts the node comfortably within the 1% rule — active for well under 1% of its lifetime — which is the single biggest lever on battery life.
  • Deep sleep between wakes (0.02–1 µA), not merely standby, using an ultra-low-power microcontroller chosen for its sleep-current specification rather than for raw speed.
  • Minimise radio time — small payloads, and aggregate several readings locally into one transmission where possible, since radio is the most expensive operation per event.
  • A small solar panel with a supercapacitor or rechargeable buffer. Outdoor panels generate 10–200 mW, and even 50 mW over 6 usable hours yields 300 mWh/day — comparable to an entire coin cell's stored energy, and far more than a duty-cycled node consumes. The buffer covers nights and the monsoon overcast period, which is the local design constraint that matters most.

Why this combination is the right answer for Bangladesh specifically. The lecture's framing is that off-grid operation is not optional here — it is the default design constraint from day one, and that low-power design is often the only thing that makes deployment feasible at all. With 200 nodes across a large field, the cost that dominates is not hardware but site visits. A design lasting one season means repeated trips across farmland to swap batteries; a design lasting years means almost none. That is the fleet-scale argument from Topic 04 applied at a smaller but still decisive scale — and it is why the answer is a long-range, low-power, harvested design rather than anything faster or more capable.

Question 11 · long answer

A river water-level sensor must run unattended for 5+ years in a remote flood-prone area of Bangladesh. Set out the design choices that matter most, and explain the reasoning behind each.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from The faculty's flood-monitoring scenario from Section 7. It pulls together Topic 02 (duty cycling and sleep states), Topic 03 (protocol), Topic 04 (harvesting) and Topic 05 (the local context). The constraint "unattended for 5+ years" is doing the work — every design choice should be justified by reference to it.

The governing constraint. Five years unattended in a remote location means a site visit is effectively a project, not a maintenance task. Every design decision should be evaluated against one question: does this reduce the probability of needing to visit? That reframes the problem from "make it efficient" to "make it not require attention", which is a stricter requirement.

1 · Aggressive duty cycling — the single biggest lever. Report every 15–30 minutes, not continuously. Water levels in a river change over hours, not seconds, so a high sampling rate buys no useful information while consuming the entire energy budget. The faculty's own answer to this scenario specifies exactly this. It also puts the node inside the 1% rule, where a well-designed sensor is active for well under 1% of its lifetime.

An important refinement: consider an adaptive duty cycle. During normal conditions report infrequently; when the level crosses a threshold, increase the reporting rate. This is the event-driven wake technique — using a hardware interrupt on a threshold crossing rather than pure timer polling — and it means the energy is spent precisely when the data actually matters, which for a flood sensor is the whole point of the deployment.

2 · Deep-sleep hardware. Use an ultra-low-power microcontroller selected for its sleep-current specification, trading raw speed for standby efficiency, and use the deep sleep / shutdown state (0.02–1 µA) rather than sleep/standby (1–50 µA) between wakes. That is up to a 50× difference in the baseline current, and over five years the baseline is what accumulates.

3 · Solar panel with a supercapacitor buffer. The faculty's answer names this specifically, and the reasoning is that no primary battery should be trusted to last five years unattended given self-discharge. A small panel generating even 50 mW over six usable hours yields 300 mWh/day — comfortably more than a duty-cycled node consumes — while the supercapacitor buffers cloudy days and nights. In Bangladesh the monsoon is the binding case: the design must survive an extended overcast period, which is precisely when flood monitoring matters most. Size the buffer for the worst realistic run of dark days, not the average.

4 · A long-range, low-power protocol — LoRaWAN. The site is remote, so cellular coverage cannot be assumed, and LoRaWAN's operator independence and 15+ km rural range mean a single gateway at a serviceable location can cover a wide stretch of river. Its very low power profile is what makes multi-year unattended operation achievable, and its modest data rate is irrelevant for a few bytes of level reading. The faculty's stated rationale is avoiding frequent site visits, which is exactly the governing constraint.

5 · Physical and environmental design — not in the lecture's list but implied by "flood-prone" and worth a mark for judgement. The enclosure must survive submersion, humidity and debris; the solar panel needs mounting above the expected flood line and positioning to shed silt; and the sensor itself should ideally be non-contact (ultrasonic or radar) rather than a float mechanism that can foul. A five-year electronic design defeated by a blocked float is a common real failure.

6 · Fail-safe behaviour. With no one attending the device, it must recover on its own: a watchdog timer to reset a hung processor, brown-out protection so a depleted buffer does not corrupt state, and periodic heartbeat messages so the network can distinguish "no flood" from "sensor died". Without heartbeats, silence is ambiguous — and for a flood warning system, ambiguous silence is the dangerous failure mode.

Why the whole design hangs together. The lecture's framing for Bangladesh is that low-power design here is often the only thing that makes deployment feasible at all, and that off-grid operation is the default constraint rather than an edge case. Every choice above is subordinate to the fleet-scale logic from Topic 04: the cost of a deployment like this is dominated by visits, not hardware, so the design target is not efficiency for its own sake but years of untouched operation.

Question 12 · long answer · stretch

A city proposes deploying 10,000 IoT sensors to optimise street lighting, projecting a 35% reduction in lighting energy. A critic argues the deployment's own footprint will negate the benefit. Evaluate both positions and state what would determine which is right.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from This is the Green IoT paradox (Topic 01) posed as an argument rather than a definition, and it pulls in the fleet-scale reasoning from Topic 04 and the e-waste material from Class 7. The examiner wants you to refuse to pick a side reflexively and instead identify the variables that decide it — the answer is genuinely conditional.

Framing: both positions are coherent, and which is correct depends entirely on the design. That is precisely what makes this the Green IoT paradox rather than a disagreement about facts. The lecture's formulation applies directly: IoT can be an enormous tool for sustainability while simultaneously being an enormous source of energy and e-waste burden if devices, networks and data pipelines are not designed efficiently.

The case for the deployment — greening by IoT. Street lighting is a large, continuous municipal load, and sensing-driven control (dimming when no one is present, adapting to ambient light) is exactly the category the lecture identifies as delivering 30%+ energy savings in a building from smart sensing and automated control. A 35% projection for street lighting is therefore plausible rather than optimistic. There is also a strong embodied-carbon argument on the deployment's side: the lecture notes that thousands of sensor nodes can be deployed for the embodied-carbon cost of a single server, so 10,000 nodes may represent a modest one-off carbon investment against a continuous recurring saving. And each node draws under 1 W, so their operational consumption is negligible beside the lighting load they control.

The case for the critic — greening of IoT. The concern is not primarily operational energy; it is lifecycle. The lecture identifies the typical replacement cycle for poorly designed IoT devices as 2–5 years, and calls this a major e-waste driver at scale. At 10,000 nodes on a 3-year cycle, the city generates roughly 3,300 discarded electronic devices per year, indefinitely. Each carries embodied carbon, each contains a battery and a radio, and — critically for Bangladesh — Class 7 establishes that only a handful of DoE-certified recyclers operate nationally, with roughly 100,000 informal workers handling most e-waste using hazardous methods. So the disposal end of that stream is a real and unresolved problem, not a theoretical one.

The maintenance footprint compounds it. The faculty's fleet-scale example is directly applicable: across a 10,000-node deployment, the difference between an 18-month battery swap and a 10-year design is roughly 6,700 truck-rolls and battery replacements per year versus almost none. Those truck-rolls carry their own fuel emissions and labour cost, and they are invisible in a naive energy-savings projection.

What actually determines the outcome — the variables to specify.

  • Device lifetime. The single most decisive factor. A 10+ year design — achievable with proper low-power engineering, per the lecture — versus a 2–5 year one changes the e-waste and maintenance figures by a factor of three or more.
  • Power source. Battery-only means periodic swaps at fleet scale. Energy harvesting — solar is the obvious fit for street furniture, which is already positioned for sun exposure — can eliminate battery replacement entirely, and "the greenest battery is the one you never have to replace or dispose of".
  • Duty cycle and protocol. A node reporting continuously over a power-hungry link drains far faster than one duty-cycled under the 1% rule on LoRaWAN or NB-IoT. Protocol choice here is not a detail; it directly sets the replacement interval.
  • End-of-life planning. Is there a take-back and certified recycling arrangement contracted at procurement, per the EPR framework in Bangladesh's E-Waste Rules 2021? A deployment with no disposal plan is exactly what the critic is describing.
  • The counterfactual. How large is the lighting load actually being reduced, and could a simpler intervention — LED replacement, or timer-based control with no sensors at all — capture most of the benefit with none of the fleet?

Conclusion. The critic is right about a badly designed deployment and wrong about a well-designed one, and the projection alone does not tell you which this is. The productive response is not to accept or reject the proposal but to make the answer conditional: require a specified minimum device lifetime, solar harvesting, a duty-cycled low-power protocol, and a contracted take-back and certified-recycling arrangement. With those specified, the greening-by benefit is real and the greening-of cost is contained. Without them, the city has bought a 35% lighting saving and a permanent e-waste stream — and the lecture's whole argument is that this is a design choice, not an inherent property of IoT.

Last-minute revision — Class 16

The paradox

Greening OF IoT = efficient devices/networks. Greening BY IoT = using sensing to cut energy elsewhere. IoT is both tool and source.

The 1% rule

Well-designed nodes are active well under 1% of their lifetime. Example: 100 ms every 10 min = 0.017%.

Duty cycle

\( d = \dfrac{t_{\text{active}}}{T_{\text{period}}}\times 100\% \) — convert both to the SAME unit first.

Battery life

\( t_{\text{hrs}} = \dfrac{C\ (\text{mAh})}{I\ (\text{mA})} \), then ÷ 8,760 for years. Convert µA → mA by ÷1000.

Average current

\( I_{\text{avg}} = d\,I_{\text{active}} + (1-d)\,I_{\text{sleep}} \) — time-weighted. Check WHICH term dominates before optimising.

Coverage area

\( A = \pi r^2 \). r = 15 km → ≈ 707 km². Gateways = area ÷ coverage, round up.

Harvested energy

\( E = P \times t \). 50 mW × 6 h = 300 mWh/day. Cell energy = mAh × V (mAh is CHARGE, not energy).

Power states (mA)

Active/transmit 10–120 mA · Compute only 1–15 mA · Idle 0.5–2 mA.

Power states (µA)

Sleep/standby 1–50 µA (RTC + wake logic, RAM retained) · Deep sleep 0.02–1 µA (wake by reset/long timer only).

4 design techniques

Duty cycling (biggest lever) · Event-driven wake · Radio efficiency · Right-sized compute (pick MCU by sleep current).

Radio > CPU

Radio transmission is the most expensive operation per event. Minimise payload and frequency BEFORE optimising CPU.

LoRaWAN

2–15+ km · very low power · 0.3–50 kbps · unlicensed, operator-independent. Agriculture, rural metering, environmental.

NB-IoT

1–10 km · very low power · ~250 kbps · licensed cellular, no gateways needed. Smart metering, asset tracking.

Zigbee

10–100 m per hop · low power · 250 kbps · self-healing mesh. Routers draw more than end devices. Indoor/building.

BLE

10–50 m · very low power · up to 2 Mbps (fastest) · smartphone compatible. Wearables, beacons.

4 harvesting sources

Photovoltaic (10–200 mW outdoors) · Piezoelectric (vibration) · Thermoelectric (temp differential) · RF (Wi-Fi/cellular ambient).

Fleet-scale argument

10,000 nodes: 18-month battery vs 10-year design = ~6,700 truck-rolls/yr vs almost none.

Key figures

29 B devices by 2030 · <1 W per node · 1.5% of global electricity (ICT) · 30%+ building savings · 2–5 yr bad-design replacement cycle.

Bangladesh

Smart agriculture & flood sensing → LoRaWAN. Metering/tracking on dense mobile coverage → NB-IoT. Off-grid is the DEFAULT constraint.

Flood sensor design

Aggressive duty cycling (15–30 min) + deep-sleep hardware + solar with supercapacitor buffer + LoRaWAN → avoids site visits.
