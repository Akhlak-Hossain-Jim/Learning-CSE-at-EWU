How to use this guide

Class 11 — Data Center Design & Redesign. Velete Ch.12 (all), Lamb Ch.6 (all). Course outcome CO3.

This is the most calculation-heavy topic in the six. The faculty said so themselves in the wrap-up: "the formulas are simple — PUE, DCiE, and capacity planning are all one- or two-step calculations; the engineering judgment is in choosing the right inputs." That sentence tells you exactly how this material will be examined. You will not be asked to do difficult arithmetic. You will be asked to pick the right formula, use the right inputs, and then interpret the result — and the interpretation is where most of the marks are.

The one-sentence version of Class 11

A data centre's reliability is bought with redundancy, redundancy is described by the N / N+1 / 2N notation and certified by the Uptime Institute's four Tiers, and its efficiency is measured by PUE — and because site and structure are fixed the day construction begins, all of this has to be decided before the first rack arrives.

There are six things to be able to do cold, and everything in this guide serves them: compute a PUE, convert PUE to DCiE, convert an uptime percentage to downtime minutes, size a redundancy model, do an area-and-density capacity plan, and explain the difference between Tier II and Tier III without hesitating.

Topic 01

Designing from scratch — the decisions that lock in

Why data centre design is unlike software: you cannot patch a site selection.

The faculty opened with a framing worth keeping: a data centre is one of the few pieces of civil engineering where a bad early decision cannot be patched with software later. Site selection, structural loading and electrical capacity are largely fixed once construction begins. Design therefore starts long before the first server rack arrives, and a purpose-built enterprise facility takes 18–24 months to construct.

The core design question

Every design decision traces back to one question: what level of risk is acceptable, and what will the organisation pay to reduce it? A bank's core transaction system and a university's research file server have very different answers, and that difference drives everything downstream — Tier level, redundancy model, and site choice. If a scenario question asks you to justify a design, start here.

The numbers from the foundation section

  • 18–24 months — typical construction timeline for a purpose-built enterprise facility.
  • 2–4× — land footprint multiplier needed beyond the building itself, for cooling plant, generators and expansion.
  • ≥2 independent utility power feeds, from separate substations.
  • ≥2 diverse fibre paths, physically separated — the faculty's phrasing was "to avoid a single backhoe cutting both", which is a memorable way of saying that redundancy only counts if the two paths cannot fail from the same cause.
  • 15–20 years — typical design life before a major redesign is triggered.

Site selection criteria

The faculty's point was that these criteria rarely change — only their relative importance does, which is why the Bangladesh section later reorders them.

  • Grid power availability and cost per kWh
  • Natural disaster risk — flood, earthquake, cyclone
  • Climate — cooling potential, humidity
  • Fibre connectivity and network latency to users
  • Water availability for cooling
  • Land cost, zoning and tax incentives

Early design decisions

  • Target Tier level — the reliability-versus-cost trade
  • Raised floor vs slab — cabling and airflow strategy
  • Modular vs monolithic build-out
  • Target rack density (kW/rack) for the building's whole life
  • Structural load rating for future high-density racks
Watch out — the over-build vs under-build trap

Building for peak day-one capacity wastes capital and runs the infrastructure at inefficient partial load for years (partial load is where PUE gets bad). Building too small forces a disruptive expansion later. The industry-standard compromise, and the answer the faculty wants, is modular design: build the shell for full capacity, but install power and cooling in phases as demand grows.

Topic 02

The Uptime Institute Tier classification

Four rows of a table. Learn the table, then learn the one distinction that trips everyone up.

When someone says a facility is "Tier III", they are referencing a specific, certifiable standard from the Uptime Institute. Four tiers describe increasing levels of redundancy and fault tolerance, and each step up costs significantly more to build and to run.

TierDescription Uptime %Max downtime/yrRedundancy
Tier I Basic capacity — single path for power & cooling, no redundancy 99.671%28.8 hrsN
Tier II Redundant components, still a single distribution path 99.741%22.0 hrsN+1
Tier III Concurrently maintainable — any component can be serviced without downtime 99.982%1.6 hrsN+1
Tier IV Fault tolerant — a single failure anywhere causes no impact 99.995%0.4 hrs (26 min)2N or 2N+1
Key idea — redundancy vs concurrent maintainability

Look at the redundancy column: Tier II and Tier III are both N+1. So what separates them? Tier II has spare components, but taking the single distribution path down for maintenance still causes an outage. Tier III's defining feature is that every capacity component and every distribution path can be shut down for planned maintenance with zero impact on IT operations. That is what "concurrently maintainable" means. It is a subtle but critical engineering difference, and it is the single most likely conceptual question from this section.

Watch out — the phrasing that distinguishes III from IV

Tier III is about planned maintenance — you can take things down deliberately without an outage. Tier IV is about unplanned failure — a single failure anywhere causes no impact, which requires two fully independent paths (2N), not just a spare unit. "Concurrently maintainable" ≠ "fault tolerant". If a question says a component failed unexpectedly and the load survived, that is Tier IV behaviour.

Cost and who needs what

  • Each Tier step adds roughly 20–50% to both capital and operating cost. A Tier IV facility can cost 2× or more per kW of IT capacity than Tier I.
  • Core banking systems and stock exchanges typically demand Tier III or IV.
  • Most enterprise back-office and university systems are well served by Tier II or III.
Topic 03

Redundancy models — N, N+1, 2N, 2N+1

The notation is simple once you fix what N means. Most errors come from misreading N.

Redundancy notation describes how much spare capacity exists beyond what is strictly needed to run the load. The definition to anchor on:

Definition of N

N is the number of units required to meet demand. Everything else is expressed relative to N.

ModelWhat is installed ReliabilityTypical use
N Bare minimum — every unit installed is required, no spares Any single component failure causes an outage. Lowest cost, lowest reliability. Dev/test environments, non-critical internal tools, Tier I facilities
N+1 One extra unit beyond what is required Tolerates one component failure, or one unit down for maintenance, without impact Most Tier II/III enterprise data centres — the most common commercial standard
2N The entire capacity path built twice, end to end Two completely independent power/cooling paths — a failure in one has zero effect on the other Tier IV facilities, financial exchanges, national critical infrastructure
2N+1 Two full paths, each with its own N+1 spare Highest commercially deployed reliability — extremely rare given the cost Central bank settlement systems, stock exchange matching engines, hyperscale flagship sites
Watch out — the two classic mistakes

(1) N+1 means "one spare in total", not "one spare each". If N = 3 chillers, N+1 is 4 chillers, not 6. (2) 2N is not "N+2". 2N doubles the whole path, so with N = 3 that is 6 units arranged as two independent sets of 3 — and the independence is the point, not just the count. 2N+1 with N = 3 would be 8 units (two paths of 3, each with one spare).

Note the mapping the faculty's calculator used: N → roughly Tier I, N+1 → Tier II/III, 2N → Tier IV. And note the overbuild cost that comes with it — under 2N you install twice the capacity you need, so a 800 kW load requires 1,600 kW of installed infrastructure, running permanently at partial load.

Topic 04

PUE and DCiE — the two efficiency metrics

Two formulas, one relationship. This is the most examinable maths in the topic.

Here is the idea before the formula. Electricity enters a data centre building. Some of it reaches the servers and does actual computing. The rest runs the air conditioning, the power conversion losses, the lighting, the UPS. Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE) asks: for every watt that reaches a server, how many watts did the building draw in total?

PUE — Power Usage Effectiveness
\[ \text{PUE} = \frac{\text{Total facility power}}{\text{IT equipment power}} \]

Because total facility power always includes the IT power plus overhead, PUE is always ≥ 1. A PUE of 1.0 would be a perfect facility with zero overhead — physically impossible. The lecture's worked example: 600 kW IT load, 900 kW total, giving PUE = 1.5, which it describes as "roughly industry average" and interprets as 0.5 kW of overhead for every 1 kW that actually reaches computing.

Data Center Infrastructure Efficiency (DCiE) asks the same question the other way round: what fraction of total power reaches the IT equipment? It is simply the inverse, expressed as a percentage.

DCiE — Data Center Infrastructure Efficiency
\[ \text{DCiE} = \frac{\text{IT equipment power}}{\text{Total facility power}} \times 100\% = \frac{1}{\text{PUE}} \times 100\% \]
Key idea — the relationship in both directions

\( \text{DCiE} = 1/\text{PUE} \) and \( \text{PUE} = 1/\text{DCiE} \). Lower PUE is better; higher DCiE is better. Useful reference points to memorise: PUE 1.25 → DCiE 80% (the lecture calls 80% "a strong, well above average result"). PUE 1.5 → DCiE 66.7% (industry average). PUE 2.0 → DCiE 50% (half the electricity never reaches a computer).

Downtime from an uptime percentage

The Tier table gives uptime as a percentage; questions often ask you to convert it to actual time. The method is the same regardless of the unit you want the answer in.

Allowed downtime
\[ \text{Downtime} = (1 - \text{uptime fraction}) \times \text{period} \]

One year = 525,600 minutes = 8,760 hours = 365 days. Memorise 525,600 and 8,760.

Capacity planning from area and density

Rack count and IT capacity
\[ \text{Racks} = \frac{\text{Hall area}}{\text{Footprint per rack}} \qquad \text{Capacity} = \text{Racks} \times \text{Density per rack} \]

Note the faculty's convention: the per-rack footprint already includes aisle space, so do not deduct aisles separately.

▶ What is PUE? — Data Center Efficiency. Why it helps: PUE is easy to compute and easy to misinterpret. Seeing where the overhead power physically goes — chillers, CRAC units, UPS conversion losses — makes the ratio mean something rather than being a number you divide.

Topic 05

Redesigning an existing facility

Five triggers, and the greenfield vs brownfield trade-off table.

Most data centres running today were not built for today's workloads — the faculty singled out GPU-dense AI clusters as the pressure point. Redesign is rarely a full rebuild; it is usually a series of targeted interventions layered onto an existing facility.

The five redesign triggers

  1. Density growth — new equipment, especially AI/HPC, exceeds the original per-rack design limit. This is a structural and electrical constraint, not a software one.
  2. PUE degradation — ageing cooling plant becomes less efficient than modern alternatives, raising operating cost.
  3. End of equipment life — UPS batteries, chillers and generators typically need replacement every 10–15 years, which is a natural moment to upgrade rather than replace like-for-like.
  4. New compliance requirements — a regulator (the lecture's example: a central bank mandating Tier III for banking data) can force reliability upgrades.
  5. Sustainability targets — corporate net-zero commitments driving efficiency retrofits and renewable power sourcing.
Greenfield (new build)Brownfield (retrofit)
Design freedomFull control of layout, density, siteConstrained by existing structure & layout
Downtime riskNone — built before go-liveHigh — must work around live systems
Capital costHighest — land, structure, everything newLower — reuses existing shell & some infrastructure
Time to deploy18–24+ monthsOften 3–9 months for targeted upgrades
Modular & containerised data centres

Prefabricated modules — power, cooling and IT space built off-site and shipped in — let operators add capacity in discrete chunks rather than committing to a full facility upfront. This shortens deployment from years to months, and is used both for greenfield expansion and for rapid capacity relief at brownfield sites. If a scenario asks how to add capacity quickly without a two-year build, this is the answer.

Topic 06

The Bangladesh context

The faculty's point: the criteria are universal, but their ranking here is different.

Bangladesh's geography and infrastructure make certain design criteria far more decisive here than elsewhere. This is the section most likely to generate a scenario question.

  • Flood and elevation risk. Much of the country is low-lying and flood-prone. Site elevation and drainage are often the single deciding constraint on location. The example given: the National Data Center is deliberately sited on elevated, well-drained land at Kaliakoir Hi-Tech Park, Gazipur.
  • Grid reliability. Voltage fluctuation and load shedding make N+1 diesel generator capacity close to mandatory rather than optional — adding capital cost and Scope 1 emissions that greener grids elsewhere avoid.
  • Regulatory pressure. Bangladesh Bank's data protection and IT governance guidance increasingly pushes banks toward Tier III-equivalent facilities for core systems.
  • Retrofit reality. Most existing private data centres in Dhaka were not built to modern Tier standards, so redesign here typically means phased retrofits — containment, UPS upgrades, generator redundancy — rather than full rebuilds.
Watch out — the Bangladesh-specific carbon trade-off

There is a genuine tension the faculty flagged and it makes a good analytical point: an unreliable grid forces N+1 diesel generator redundancy, which is Scope 1 emissions the facility would not otherwise have. So the same reliability requirement that is environmentally neutral in Norway is carbon-positive in Dhaka. Reliability and sustainability are not always aligned, and saying so shows you have understood the local section rather than memorised it.

Practice A

Numerical practice — worked line by line

These extend the faculty's own five math-practice cards. Do them on paper first.

Question 1 · easy · warm-up

A facility's IT equipment load is 600 kW. Total facility power draw is 900 kW. (a) What is the PUE? (b) What is the DCiE? (c) Interpret both.

Show worked solution

  1. \( P_{\text{IT}} = 600\ \text{kW},\quad P_{\text{total}} = 900\ \text{kW} \)

    Identify which figure is which. "Total facility power" includes IT plus cooling and everything else — if you swap them you get a PUE below 1, which is impossible and is your check.

  2. \( \text{PUE} = \dfrac{P_{\text{total}}}{P_{\text{IT}}} \)

    Definition of PUE: total facility power divided by IT equipment power.

  3. \( \text{PUE} = \dfrac{900}{600} = 1.5 \)

    Divide. The kW units cancel, leaving a dimensionless ratio — as PUE must be.

  4. \( \text{DCiE} = \dfrac{1}{\text{PUE}} \times 100\% \)

    DCiE is the reciprocal of PUE, expressed as a percentage — it asks what share of total power reaches IT.

  5. \( = \dfrac{1}{1.5} \times 100\% = 66.7\% \)

    Compute the reciprocal and scale by 100. Cross-check directly from the raw figures: 600/900 = 0.667, same answer.

Answer

(a) PUE = 1.5 · (b) DCiE ≈ 66.7%

(c) Interpretation, which is where the marks are: a PUE of 1.5 means 0.5 kW of overhead for every 1 kW that actually reaches computing — the facility draws half as much again as the servers need. Equivalently, only two-thirds of the electricity entering the building does useful work; one-third is consumed by cooling, power conversion and lighting. The lecture describes 1.5 as roughly industry average, so this facility is neither efficient nor unusually poor.

Question 2 · easy · warm-up

A cooling system needs exactly 3 chillers (N = 3) to meet full load. (a) Under N+1, how many chillers are installed? (b) Under 2N? (c) Under 2N+1? (d) For each, how many simultaneous chiller failures can the facility survive?

Show worked solution

  1. \( N = 3 \)

    Definition of N: the number of units required to meet demand. Everything else is expressed relative to this.

  2. \( N+1 = 3 + 1 = 4\ \text{chillers} \)

    N+1 adds one spare in total, not one per unit. This is the most common arithmetic slip in the whole topic.

  3. \( 2N = 2 \times 3 = 6\ \text{chillers} \)

    2N duplicates the entire capacity path, so you install two independent sets of 3 — not 3 plus 2.

  4. \( 2N+1 = 2 \times (3+1) = 8\ \text{chillers} \)

    2N+1 is two full paths, each with its own N+1 spare (the lecture's own wording). So it is 2 × 4, giving 8 — not 6 + 1 = 7.

Answer

(a) 4 · (b) 6 · (c) 8

(d) Survivable failures: N+1 survives 1 — any one chiller can fail or be taken offline for maintenance while the remaining three cover the full load. 2N survives 3 if all three failures fall in the same path, since the other path independently carries the whole load; but only 1 guaranteed if failures are distributed arbitrarily. 2N+1 survives at least 1 per path plus a whole path.

Note the important qualitative point: with 2N the value is not merely the extra units, it is that the two paths are completely independent, so a fault in one has zero effect on the other. That independence is what buys Tier IV fault tolerance; simply owning six chillers on one distribution path would not.

Question 3 · medium · core

Tier III promises 99.982% uptime. There are 525,600 minutes in a year. (a) How many minutes of downtime does that allow? (b) Repeat for Tier IV at 99.995%. (c) How many times more downtime does Tier III permit than Tier IV?

Show worked solution

  1. \( U_{\text{III}} = 99.982\% = 0.99982 \)

    Convert percentage to a fraction (÷100) so it can be subtracted from 1. Keep all the decimal places — rounding to 99.98% changes the answer by over 10%.

  2. \( D_{\text{frac}} = 1 - 0.99982 = 0.00018 \)

    Complement: the facility is either up or down, so the downtime fraction is one minus the uptime fraction.

  3. \( D_{\text{III}} = 0.00018 \times 525{,}600 = 94.6\ \text{min} \)

    Downtime = downtime fraction × period. Using minutes per year gives the answer directly in minutes.

  4. \( 1 - 0.99995 = 0.00005 \)

    Same complement step for Tier IV.

  5. \( D_{\text{IV}} = 0.00005 \times 525{,}600 = 26.3\ \text{min} \)

    Same multiplication. This matches the "≈26 min" figure the faculty put on the title slide, confirming the method.

  6. \( \dfrac{94.6}{26.3} \approx 3.6 \)

    Ratio of the two downtimes answers "how many times more". Both are in minutes so the units cancel.

Answer

(a) ≈ 94.6 minutes — under 1 hour 35 minutes for an entire year. (b) ≈ 26.3 minutes. (c) Tier III permits about 3.6× as much downtime as Tier IV.

Sanity check against the Tier table: it lists Tier III at 1.6 hrs, and 94.6 min = 1.58 hrs. Agreement confirms the arithmetic. The engineering point worth adding: that 68-minute difference between Tier III and Tier IV per year is what costs an extra 20–50% in capital and operating expenditure — which is why the lecture says most enterprise and university systems are well served by Tier II or III, and only core banking and exchanges justify Tier IV.

Question 4 · medium · core

A server hall is 500 m². Each rack, including aisle space, needs 2 m², and average density is 6 kW/rack. (a) How many racks fit? (b) What is the hall's total IT capacity? (c) If the facility runs at PUE 1.4, what total facility power must the electrical design support?

Show worked solution

  1. \( A = 500\ \text{m}^2,\quad a = 2\ \text{m}^2/\text{rack} \)

    Hall area and per-rack footprint. Note the footprint already includes aisle space (stated in the problem), so no further deduction is made.

  2. \( n = \dfrac{A}{a} = \dfrac{500}{2} = 250\ \text{racks} \)

    Area ÷ footprint per unit = number of units. Units: m² ÷ (m²/rack) = racks.

  3. \( P_{\text{IT}} = n \times d = 250 \times 6\ \text{kW} \)

    Count × density per unit = total capacity. This is the same "per-unit × count" pattern as the rack calculation, one level up.

  4. \( P_{\text{IT}} = 1{,}500\ \text{kW} = 1.5\ \text{MW} \)

    Multiply, then convert to MW (÷1,000) since facility capacity at this scale is conventionally quoted in megawatts.

  5. \( \text{PUE} = \dfrac{P_{\text{total}}}{P_{\text{IT}}} \Rightarrow P_{\text{total}} = \text{PUE} \times P_{\text{IT}} \)

    Rearrange the PUE definition for total power. This is the form you need whenever a question gives you IT load and PUE and asks for facility draw.

  6. \( P_{\text{total}} = 1.4 \times 1{,}500 = 2{,}100\ \text{kW} = 2.1\ \text{MW} \)

    Substitute and multiply. The result must exceed the IT load, since PUE > 1 always — that is the check.

Answer

(a) 250 racks · (b) 1,500 kW (1.5 MW) IT capacity · (c) 2,100 kW (2.1 MW) total facility power.

The faculty called this "the starting point for any electrical and cooling capacity plan", and part (c) shows why: the electrical design must be sized for the facility figure, not the IT figure. Designing the incoming supply for 1.5 MW when the building will actually draw 2.1 MW is a 600 kW error — which is the kind of early decision that, as Topic 01 puts it, cannot be patched later. Sanity check: 6 kW/rack is a moderate density; modern AI/GPU racks can exceed 30 kW, which is exactly the density growth redesign trigger from Topic 05.

Question 5 · medium · core

A data centre has an IT load of 800 kW. (a) Under a 2N redundancy model, how much total infrastructure capacity must be installed, and what is the overbuild in kW and as a percentage? (b) Which Tier does this roughly map to, and what annual downtime does that imply?

Show worked solution

  1. \( P_{\text{required}} = 800\ \text{kW} \Rightarrow N = 800\ \text{kW} \)

    N is the capacity required to meet demand, so here N is expressed directly in kW rather than as a unit count — the notation works either way.

  2. \( P_{\text{installed}} = 2N = 2 \times 800 = 1{,}600\ \text{kW} \)

    2N duplicates the whole capacity path, so installed capacity is exactly twice the required load.

  3. \( \text{Overbuild} = 1{,}600 - 800 = 800\ \text{kW} \)

    Installed minus required is the spare capacity — infrastructure that is paid for and maintained but does no work under normal conditions.

  4. \( \dfrac{800}{800} \times 100\% = 100\% \)

    Overbuild as a percentage of the required load. For 2N this is always 100% — a useful shortcut worth remembering.

Answer

(a) 1,600 kW installed, an overbuild of 800 kW (100%). (b) 2N maps to Tier IV, which allows 0.4 hrs ≈ 26 minutes of downtime per year at 99.995% uptime.

Add this for the analytical marks: 100% overbuild means the facility permanently runs its power and cooling infrastructure at roughly 50% load, and equipment at partial load is less efficient than at design load — so a 2N facility tends to carry a worse PUE than an equivalent N+1 one. That is the real cost of Tier IV: not just the 20–50% capital premium the lecture quotes, but a permanent efficiency penalty. It is also the reason the over-build vs under-build trap from Topic 01 recommends modular phasing.

Question 6 · hard · stretch

A Dhaka facility has an IT load of 400 kW and a PUE of 1.8. A retrofit — hot-aisle containment plus a modern chiller plant — will bring PUE down to 1.35. Electricity costs $0.14/kWh and the retrofit costs $260,000.

(a) Annual facility energy before and after. (b) Annual money saved. (c) Simple payback period. (d) At a grid carbon intensity of 0.6 kg CO₂e/kWh, annual carbon saved in tonnes. (e) Which redesign trigger does this represent?

Show worked solution

  1. \( P_{\text{IT}} = 400\ \text{kW},\ \text{PUE}_1 = 1.8,\ \text{PUE}_2 = 1.35 \)

    The IT load is unchanged by the retrofit — containment and chillers affect overhead, not the servers. That is why the same \(P_{\text{IT}}\) is used for both cases.

  2. \( P_{\text{total},1} = 1.8 \times 400 = 720\ \text{kW} \)

    Rearranged PUE definition: total = PUE × IT. Before the retrofit the building draws 720 kW to run 400 kW of servers.

  3. \( P_{\text{total},2} = 1.35 \times 400 = 540\ \text{kW} \)

    Same rearrangement with the improved PUE.

  4. \( E_1 = 720 \times 8{,}760 = 6{,}307{,}200\ \text{kWh/yr} \)

    Energy = power × time, with 8,760 h for continuous annual operation — data centres run 24/7 by definition.

  5. \( E_2 = 540 \times 8{,}760 = 4{,}730{,}400\ \text{kWh/yr} \)

    Same calculation after the retrofit.

  6. \( \Delta E = 6{,}307{,}200 - 4{,}730{,}400 = 1{,}576{,}800\ \text{kWh/yr} \)

    Difference of the two annual energies. Shortcut check: \(\Delta P = (1.8-1.35)\times400 = 180\) kW, and 180 × 8,760 = 1,576,800 — same figure, which validates the longer route.

  7. \( S = 1{,}576{,}800 \times \$0.14 = \$220{,}752/\text{yr} \)

    Energy × tariff = money. kWh × $/kWh leaves dollars.

  8. \( \text{Payback} = \dfrac{260{,}000}{220{,}752} \approx 1.18\ \text{years} \)

    Simple payback = CapEx ÷ annual saving. Units: $ ÷ ($/yr) = yr.

  9. \( C = \dfrac{1{,}576{,}800 \times 0.6}{1{,}000} = 946.1\ \text{t CO}_2\text{e/yr} \)

    Carbon = energy × grid intensity, then ÷1,000 to convert kg to metric tonnes, which is the reporting convention.

Answer

(a) 6,307,200 kWh/yr before, 4,730,400 kWh/yr after. (b) ≈ $220,752/year saved. (c) ≈ 1.2 years payback. (d) ≈ 946 tonnes CO₂e/year avoided. (e) This is PUE degradation — trigger 2 from Topic 05, where ageing cooling plant becomes less efficient than modern alternatives and raises operating cost.

Sanity check on the payback: the retrofit costs roughly one year's saving, so a payback just over 1 year is exactly what you should expect — if you had got 12 years, you would know a factor of 8,760 had gone astray. Note that the IT load never changes here; the entire saving comes from shrinking the overhead multiplier. That is the defining characteristic of a facility-layer intervention, and it is worth contrasting with the Class 12 virtualisation material, which attacks the IT load itself and then gets the PUE saving on top as a multiplier.

Practice B

Analytical questions

Question 7 · short answer

Both Tier II and Tier III use N+1 redundancy, yet Tier III allows only 1.6 hours of annual downtime against Tier II's 22 hours. Explain how the same redundancy model produces a 14-fold difference in reliability.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from This is the conceptual question of Class 11, joining Topic 02 (the Tier table) to Topic 03 (redundancy notation). The examiner has deliberately picked the one place where the redundancy column does not explain the uptime column, and wants the term concurrent maintainability. If you only discuss spare components you cannot answer it.

Model answer. The difference is not in the number of spare components — both tiers are N+1, so both have one unit of spare capacity beyond what the load requires. The difference is in the distribution path.

Tier II has redundant components but a single distribution path. So if a chiller fails, the spare picks up the load and nothing happens. But if the single power or cooling distribution path itself needs maintenance — a UPS module replaced, a switchgear inspection, a pipe section isolated — the load must be dropped, because there is no alternative route to the racks. Since that maintenance is routine and scheduled, not a rare failure, it happens several times a year, and that is where the 22 hours goes.

Tier III is concurrently maintainable, which is the lecture's defining phrase. Every capacity component and every distribution path can be shut down for planned maintenance with zero impact on IT operations. Practically that means dual power paths to each rack — dual-corded PDUs — and isolatable cooling loops, so any single element can be taken out of service live while the load continues on the alternative route.

Why the effect on annual downtime is so large: in a well-run facility, the dominant source of downtime is not component failure at all — it is planned maintenance. Tier II eliminates outages from component failure but not from maintenance. Tier III eliminates both. Removing the larger of the two categories is what produces the drop from 22 hours to 1.6.

Worth adding for a final mark: Tier III still is not fault tolerant. It guarantees that planned work causes no outage. Tier IV, at 2N, guarantees that an unplanned single failure anywhere causes no impact — a stronger and more expensive promise.

Question 8 · short answer

A facility reports PUE 1.2 and claims to be highly efficient. Its servers run at 8% average CPU utilisation. Critique the claim.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from This bridges Topic 04 (PUE) to the Class 12 virtualisation material, and it is exactly the bridge the faculty built in the Class 12 opening: "even the greenest Tier IV data centre with a 1.15 PUE is inherently wasteful if its underlying servers sit idle at 10% utilisation." The examiner is testing whether you know what PUE does not measure.

Model answer. The PUE figure itself is genuinely good. At 1.2, the DCiE is 1/1.2 = 83.3%, so 83% of the electricity entering the building reaches the IT equipment — well above the industry-average 1.5 (66.7%). The facility layer is efficient.

But PUE measures only the ratio of total facility power to IT power. It says nothing whatsoever about whether the IT power is doing useful work. A server drawing 200 W while computing nothing counts as "IT power" in the numerator's denominator just as much as a server running at full utilisation. PUE can therefore be improved by making the servers less efficient — if idle servers drew more power, the IT figure would rise and PUE would fall, which is a clear sign the metric cannot be the whole story.

At 8% CPU utilisation the facility is badly wasteful in a way PUE is structurally incapable of detecting. An idle server draws roughly 50–70% of its peak power, so nearly all of that "efficiently delivered" electricity is powering idle silicon. The facility has optimised the overhead multiplier while ignoring the base it multiplies.

What should be done instead: attack the IT load itself through server consolidation and virtualisation, raising utilisation toward the 65–80% target and decommissioning the surplus hosts. This is strictly more valuable than a facility retrofit, because every watt eliminated at the IT layer is multiplied by the PUE — removing 1 kW of IT load removes 1.2 kW of facility load here. The correct sequence is therefore: reduce IT load first, then optimise PUE, not the reverse.

Conclusion. The claim is not false but it is incomplete, and relying on it would lead to the wrong investment decision. A complete efficiency picture needs PUE and a utilisation or work-delivered metric alongside it.

Question 9 · short answer

Explain why modular design has become the industry-standard approach to build-out, with reference to both capital efficiency and operating efficiency.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from Topic 01 (the over-build vs under-build trap) and Topic 05 (modular and containerised facilities). The phrase "both capital and operating efficiency" is the examiner signposting that there are two distinct arguments and they want both — most students give only the capital one.

The problem modular design solves. A data centre must be designed for a load that does not exist yet. Two failure modes follow. Building for peak day-one capacity ties up capital in infrastructure that sits unused for years. Building too small forces a disruptive expansion later — and expansion at a live site is a brownfield project with high downtime risk, constrained by the existing structure and layout.

The capital argument. Modular design builds the shell for full capacity but installs power and cooling in phases as demand grows. The expensive, slow, hard-to-change elements — land, structure, structural load rating — are committed once, since those genuinely cannot be patched later (Topic 01). The expensive but modular elements are deferred, so capital is spent closer to the point it earns a return. Prefabricated containerised modules take this further, shortening deployment from years to months and letting capacity be added in discrete chunks.

The operating argument, which is the half usually missed. Power and cooling infrastructure is less efficient at partial load than at design load. A facility built for 5 MW but running 1 MW of IT does not simply waste the unused capital — it runs its chillers, UPS and distribution far below their efficient operating point, which shows up directly as a worse PUE for years. Phasing installation keeps the installed plant closer to its design load, so the facility runs at a better PUE throughout the ramp-up rather than only once it is full.

Connecting to redesign. Modularity also helps later: when a redesign trigger arrives — density growth from AI/HPC racks, or end-of-life chillers and UPS batteries at 10–15 years — a modular facility can absorb targeted upgrades in 3–9 months instead of requiring a greenfield rebuild at 18–24 months. It converts a structural constraint into an incremental one.

Question 10 · long answer

A bank wants to build a new data centre near the Buriganga River, citing cheap land and easy access to cooling water. Evaluate the proposal and recommend a course of action.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from The faculty's own site scenario from Section 7. It tests whether you can take the generic site-selection criteria (Topic 01) and re-rank them for Bangladesh (Topic 06). The examiner's key point is that the criteria are universal but their relative importance is local. A good answer does not just say "flooding" — it explains why flooding outranks the other criteria here and then solves the cooling problem the site was chosen for.

First objection: flood and monsoon risk. Riverside sites are attractive for water-side cooling, but they carry high inundation risk. Much of Bangladesh is low-lying and flood-prone, and in this country site elevation and drainage are frequently the single deciding constraint on location — outranking land cost, cooling access and even grid proximity. The precedent is the National Data Center, deliberately sited on elevated, well-drained land at Kaliakoir Hi-Tech Park, Gazipur, rather than anywhere cheaper or closer to water.

Why the risk is disqualifying rather than merely costly. Site selection is one of the decisions that is fixed the moment construction begins (Topic 01) — it cannot be patched later the way a cooling plant or a UPS can be replaced. And the consequence of getting it wrong is not degraded efficiency but total loss of the facility. For a bank this is compounded by Bangladesh Bank's data protection and IT governance guidance, which pushes core banking systems toward Tier III-equivalent reliability. A facility that can be inundated cannot credibly offer 99.982% availability regardless of how much redundancy is installed inside it, because the risk is to the whole site rather than to any component the N+1 model protects.

The cheap-land argument. Land near a flood-prone river is cheap because it floods. That discount is the market pricing the risk the bank would be assuming. Note also that the site must accommodate a 2–4× footprint multiplier beyond the building for cooling plant, generators and expansion — so raw land price is a smaller share of project cost than it appears.

Recommendation. Reject the riverside site. Select on adequate elevation and drainage first, then apply the remaining criteria — grid power availability and cost, fibre connectivity with ≥2 physically separated diverse paths, and land cost and zoning. Solve the cooling-water requirement independently through closed-loop cooling systems or piped supply, which decouples the water need from the site choice entirely. That is the key insight: the river was solving a cooling problem that has other solutions, while creating a flood problem that has none.

Additional Bangladesh-specific design requirements for whichever site is chosen: given voltage fluctuation and load shedding, N+1 diesel generator capacity is close to mandatory rather than optional. The bank should budget for that capital cost and note the associated Scope 1 emissions — an unavoidable trade-off where grid reliability is poor.

Question 11 · long answer

An existing Dhaka data centre is Tier II. A new central bank guideline requires Tier III for core banking systems. The operations director proposes buying additional UPS units and chillers. Assess this proposal and set out what is actually required.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from The faculty's redesign scenario from Section 7, and it is deliberately a trap. The director's proposal is the intuitive one and it is wrong. It tests the concurrent maintainability distinction (Topic 02) against the redundancy notation (Topic 03), plus the brownfield realities from Topic 05.

Assessment: the proposal misdiagnoses the gap. The facility is already Tier II, which means it is already N+1 — it already has redundant components. Buying more UPS units and chillers adds spare capacity the facility already possesses. It would increase capital and maintenance cost while moving the facility no closer to Tier III certification.

What actually separates Tier II from Tier III. Both are N+1. The distinguishing requirement is concurrent maintainability: every capacity component and every distribution path must be able to be shut down for planned maintenance with zero impact on IT operations. Tier II fails this not because it lacks spares but because it has a single distribution path — so servicing that path drops the load. The gap is in the topology, not the inventory.

What is actually required. The minimum change is redundant distribution paths, specifically:

  • Dual power paths to every rack — dual-corded PDUs, with each cord fed from an independent distribution path, so either path can be isolated for maintenance while the racks continue on the other.
  • Isolatable cooling loops, so any section of the cooling distribution can be valved off and serviced without removing cooling from the hall.
  • Corresponding switchgear and distribution board changes to make the paths genuinely independent rather than nominally separate but sharing a common element.

Practical implications for a brownfield project. This is a retrofit on a live facility, so downtime risk is high and the work is constrained by the existing structure and layout (Topic 05). Capital cost is lower than a new build and the timeline is far shorter — typically 3–9 months for targeted upgrades against 18–24 months for greenfield — but the sequencing must be planned so that core banking systems stay up throughout. In practice this means phasing the work rack row by rack row, and it may require temporary distribution during the transition. The lecture notes that most existing private data centres in Dhaka were not built to modern Tier standards, so phased retrofit is the normal path here rather than the exception.

A caution to include. Dual-corded PDUs only deliver Tier III if the servers themselves have dual power supplies. Single-corded equipment plugged into a dual-path facility reintroduces a single point of failure at the last metre, so an equipment audit belongs in the project scope.

Recommendation. Redirect the budget from additional capacity units to distribution path redundancy, commission an Uptime Institute assessment to confirm what the certification actually requires for this facility, and phase the work to protect live systems. And be explicit with the board that Tier III delivers concurrent maintainability, not fault tolerance — if the central bank's intent is survival of unplanned single failures, that is Tier IV at 2N, a materially larger project.

Question 12 · long answer · stretch

You are walking the floor of a data centre on a site visit. Describe the physical evidence you would look for to judge whether the facility is closer to Tier II or Tier III, and explain the reasoning behind each observation.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from This is the faculty's own field-trip discussion prompt from Section 7, so it is a strong exam candidate. It tests whether your understanding of concurrent maintainability (Topic 02) is concrete enough to be observed rather than merely recited. Every observation you list must be tied to the reasoning — an unexplained checklist earns little.

The single question to answer by observation: can every distribution path be taken out of service without dropping the load? Tier II has redundant components on one path; Tier III has redundant paths. So look for evidence of duality of route, not quantity of equipment.

1 · Look at the back of the racks — power cords per server. The clearest single indicator. Dual-corded servers, with the two cords running to two different PDUs, indicate dual power paths and point to Tier III. Single-corded equipment means servicing that one path drops those servers, which is Tier II behaviour regardless of what is installed upstream.

2 · Trace the PDUs back. Two PDUs per rack is only meaningful if they are fed from genuinely independent distribution boards. If both trace back to the same panel or the same UPS output, the redundancy is cosmetic and there is still a single point of failure. Colour-coded A/B power infrastructure — separate cable trays, distinct labelling — is strong evidence of a deliberate dual-path design.

3 · Cooling loop isolation valves. Look for valves that allow a section of the chilled water loop to be isolated and drained while the rest of the hall stays cooled. Their presence indicates cooling distribution designed for concurrent maintenance. A single loop with no isolation points means any cooling pipework maintenance requires shutting down the hall.

4 · UPS and switchgear arrangement. Tier II typically shows N+1 UPS modules feeding a single distribution bus. Tier III shows two separate systems with independent output paths. Ask whether a UPS can be put into maintenance bypass without the load riding on raw utility power — if the answer is no, that is not concurrently maintainable.

5 · Ask the operational question, not the equipment question. The most revealing thing to ask the facility manager is: "when you last replaced a UPS battery string or serviced a chiller, did the IT load stay up?" Tier III means yes, routinely, as normal practice. Tier II means it was done in a scheduled maintenance window with systems down — and their maintenance-window calendar is documentary evidence either way.

6 · Generator and fuel arrangements — particularly relevant in Dhaka. Given grid voltage fluctuation and load shedding, N+1 generator capacity is close to mandatory here, so its presence indicates local necessity rather than Tier level. What is more informative is whether generators feed both distribution paths independently, and whether on-site fuel storage and refuelling contracts match the claimed autonomy.

Interpreting what you find. The faculty's framing is worth stating in conclusion: the difference between the tiers is a subtle but critical engineering difference, and it lives in topology. A facility can look impressive — plenty of units, clean halls, good containment — and still be Tier II, because Tier II is defined by having only one road to the racks, however many vehicles are parked on it.

Last-minute revision — Class 11

PUE

\( \text{PUE} = \dfrac{\text{Total facility power}}{\text{IT power}} \) — always ≥ 1. Lower is better. 1.5 ≈ industry average.

DCiE

\( \text{DCiE} = \dfrac{1}{\text{PUE}}\times 100\% \) — higher is better. PUE 1.25 → 80%. PUE 1.5 → 66.7%. PUE 2.0 → 50%.

Downtime

\( D = (1-\text{uptime})\times \text{period} \). Year = 525,600 min = 8,760 hrs.

Capacity plan

Racks = area ÷ footprint (footprint INCLUDES aisles). Capacity = racks × kW/rack. Facility kW = PUE × IT kW.

Tier I

Single path, no redundancy. N. 99.671% → 28.8 hrs/yr. Dev/test.

Tier II

Redundant components, SINGLE distribution path. N+1. 99.741% → 22.0 hrs/yr.

Tier III

Concurrently maintainable. N+1. 99.982% → 1.6 hrs/yr (≈94.6 min). Dual-corded PDUs.

Tier IV

Fault tolerant — single failure anywhere = no impact. 2N / 2N+1. 99.995% → 0.4 hrs (26 min).

The II vs III answer

Both are N+1. Difference = distribution PATH, not components. III can service ANY path live. That's concurrent maintainability.

III vs IV

III = planned maintenance causes no outage. IV = UNPLANNED single failure causes no impact. Different promises.

Redundancy counts (N=3)

N = 3 · N+1 = 4 · 2N = 6 · 2N+1 = 8 (two paths of N+1). 2N overbuild is always 100%.

Tier cost

Each step ≈ +20–50% capital and operating. Tier IV can be 2×+ per kW vs Tier I.

5 redesign triggers

Density growth (AI/HPC) · PUE degradation · Equipment end-of-life (10–15 yrs) · New compliance · Sustainability targets.

Greenfield vs brownfield

Greenfield: full freedom, no downtime risk, highest cost, 18–24 mo. Brownfield: constrained, HIGH downtime risk, lower cost, 3–9 mo.

Design numbers

18–24 mo build · 2–4× land footprint · ≥2 utility feeds (separate substations) · ≥2 diverse fibre paths · 15–20 yr design life.

Over/under-build

Answer is always modular: shell for full capacity, power & cooling installed in phases.

Bangladesh

Flood/elevation is the DECIDING constraint (Kaliakoir, Gazipur). N+1 diesel gensets near-mandatory. BB pushes Tier III for core banking. Retrofit not rebuild.

What PUE can't see

PUE ignores server UTILISATION. A 1.15 PUE facility at 10% CPU is still wasteful. Cut IT load first — the saving multiplies by PUE.
