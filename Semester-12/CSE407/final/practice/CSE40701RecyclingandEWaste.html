How to use this guide

Class 7 — Recycling & E-Waste Management. Velete Ch.7 (all). Course outcome CO2.

This is one of six topic guides for CSE407. It assumes you know nothing about e-waste and builds every idea from the ground up. Read the explanation sections first, then attempt each practice question before opening its solution — the solutions are hidden behind a click on purpose.

Your exam is maths, analytical reasoning, and scenario-style long answers. So the practice here is split three ways: numerical problems (worked line by line, with a reason attached to every single line), analytical questions that ask you to distinguish between things the faculty deliberately contrasted, and scenario answers written the way you would actually write them in a script book, with a note above each one telling you exactly which part of the lecture the examiner is fishing for.

The one-sentence version of Class 7

Electronics contain both a fortune in recoverable metals and a load of poison, almost none of it is recovered properly, and the single biggest lever on that problem sits with the engineer who decided how the device was built in the first place.

Topic 01

What e-waste is, and why the numbers matter

The four figures the faculty put on the title slide are the four figures most likely to appear in a question.

Start with the plainest possible definition, because the exam will want it stated cleanly: e-waste is any discarded electrical or electronic device. Not "broken" — discarded. A working phone in a drawer that nobody will ever switch on again is, in practice, e-waste waiting to happen. That distinction matters, and the faculty made a point of it by opening the class with a homework tally of old devices sitting in students' drawers.

Now picture the flow. Somewhere, metal is dug out of the ground. It gets refined, shipped, assembled into a phone, sold, used for a couple of years, and then it stops. At that moment one of two things happens to it. Either it enters a formal system — a licensed, certified recycler with industrial equipment and pollution controls — or it enters an informal one, meaning someone burns the plastic off the wires in an open yard to get at the copper. Globally, the split between those two paths is roughly one to five.

The four headline figures from Class 7

62 Mt — e-waste generated globally in 2023, a record.
17.4% — the share formally recycled. The other ~83% is dumped, burned, or handled informally.
$57 billion — the value of raw materials lost inside unrecycled e-waste each year.
2.6 kg — e-waste generated per person globally per year, and rising.

Learn those four. They are the kind of thing that gets asked as a one-mark recall question and then re-used as the input to a calculation later in the same paper.

Why it is growing faster than anything else

The faculty listed e-waste as growing at 3–4× the rate of any other waste category, and gave five drivers. They are worth memorising as a list because "explain why e-waste is the fastest-growing waste stream" is a natural short-answer question:

  • Shortening lifespans. The average smartphone is now replaced every 2.5 years, down from 4+ years in 2010.
  • Falling hardware prices mean more devices per household, so more devices eventually discarded.
  • The IoT explosion — 50 billion+ connected devices projected by 2030, every one of them with a finite life.
  • Planned obsolescence — software updates stop, batteries degrade, repairability is designed out.
  • A fast-growing middle class in Asia and Africa buying first-time electronics.

What actually happens to the 83%

Three specific mechanisms were named, and each has a specific pollutant attached to it. Do not blur them together — the examiner can ask about any one on its own.

  • Open burning. Wire insulation is set alight to expose the copper inside. This releases dioxins, furans, and polyaromatic hydrocarbons straight into air and soil. Named locations: Agbogbloshie in Ghana, Guiyu in China, and parts of Bangladesh.
  • Landfill leaching. Lead, cadmium, and mercury from batteries and solder seep into groundwater. One CRT monitor alone holds up to 4 kg of lead.
  • Informal acid baths. Circuit boards are dunked in acid to dissolve out the gold, and the spent acid — full of heavy metals — goes into the waterway.
Watch out

Don't say "e-waste is bad because it fills landfills." That is a rubbish answer and it throws away the actual content of the lecture. The problem is dual: it is simultaneously a toxicity problem (lead, mercury, cadmium, dioxins reaching people) and a resource loss problem ($57 bn of metal burned or buried). Any good answer names both halves.

Who generates it, who suffers from it

The lecture drew a deliberate line between these two groups, and that gap is the moral core of the topic. High-income countries generate the most per person — Norway at roughly 22 kg/person/year against a global average of 2.6. But a great deal of it is exported to lower-income countries, often mislabelled as "second-hand goods" to slip past the rules. The workers who then process it informally, frequently children, take the direct toxic exposure. Bangladesh sits on the receiving end of this, taking in material from India, the Gulf states, and informal trade networks. The Basel Convention bans hazardous e-waste export, but as the faculty put it, enforcement is weak.

Topic 02

What is inside the device — the eight materials

The faculty built an interactive panel around exactly eight materials. Expect at least one question drawn from it.

A modern smartphone contains over 60 different elements. The lecture picked out eight and sorted them into three jobs: precious metals worth recovering, critical minerals with fragile supply chains, and toxic materials that cause harm if released. Getting the category right matters as much as the number, because analytical questions tend to be phrased as "which of these is a conflict mineral" rather than "how much gold is in a phone".

Key idea — the urban mine

Urban mining means treating stockpiles of discarded electronics as an ore body to be mined, rather than a waste problem to be disposed of. The reason this is not just a slogan is concentration: a tonne of scrapped smartphones carries roughly 300 g of gold, while natural gold ore might carry a few grams per tonne. UNEP puts the annual urban mine at over $57 billion — which is currently being burned or buried.

Watch out — the trap in this table

Gold has the highest value per gram, but copper is the most valuable metal in e-waste by weight, because there is roughly 500 times more of it. If a question asks "which material contributes most value" you must check whether it means per gram or in total. The lecture's own phrasing is: copper is "by weight, the most valuable common metal in e-waste."

Topic 03

Product lifecycle and Extended Producer Responsibility

This is the conceptual heart of Class 7 and the most likely long-answer question.

Here is the problem EPR was invented to solve. A manufacturer designs a device, sells it, and books the profit. Years later the device becomes waste, and the cost of dealing with that waste lands on a municipality, a consumer, or an informal worker's lungs. The manufacturer, who made every decision that determined how hard the thing would be to recycle, pays nothing. The incentives point the wrong way.

Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) is the policy principle that the manufacturer is responsible for the entire lifecycle of the product, including collection and recycling at end of life. In one move it shifts the financial and operational burden from governments and consumers back to the company that created the product.

Key idea — why EPR is an engineering topic, not a legal one

Once a manufacturer pays the end-of-life bill, they gain a direct financial incentive to design products that are cheaper to recycle: fewer materials, fewer types of material, fewer toxic substances, and designed for disassembly. EPR therefore creates a feedback loop from end-of-life economics back into design decisions. That loop is why RoHS and WEEE together transformed European electronics design. The faculty flagged this explicitly from Velete Ch.7 — it is the sentence to reproduce in an exam.

The four lifecycle phases

The lecture walked the lifecycle in four phases, plus the EPR frameworks that wrap around them. Each phase has its own emissions scope and its own engineering lever.

1 · Raw materials — extraction, refining, supply chain

Scope 3 emissions, conflict minerals, water use. Manufacturing a smartphone emits roughly 70 kg CO₂e, and 70–80% of the device's lifetime footprint occurs here and in manufacturing, before the device is ever switched on — this is embodied carbon. Supply is concentrated: cobalt in the DRC, lithium in South America, rare earths in China (60%+). And semiconductor fabrication is brutally water-hungry — a single 300 mm silicon wafer needs about 2,200 litres of ultra-pure water.

2 · Manufacturing — assembly, testing, packaging

Scope 1 and 2 emissions, chemical waste, packaging waste. A factory on coal versus one on renewables can differ 10× in carbon intensity for the identical process. PCB etching, soldering and cleaning all generate hazardous chemical waste. The lecture's packaging example: Apple removing chargers and headphones from the iPhone box cut box size 70%, fitting more units per shipping container and cutting transport emissions. RoHS constrains material choice at this stage.

3 · Use phase — operational energy and lifespan

Only 20–30% of lifetime emissions for most consumer devices. Which leads to the counter-intuitive conclusion the faculty wanted you to reach: efficiency improvements here matter less than extending the device's life. Two mechanisms shorten that life — software obsolescence (updates stop, device becomes insecure though the hardware works; the EU now requires 5–10 year support windows) and unrepairability (the EU Right to Repair Regulation, 2021+, requires spare parts for 7–10 years and public repair manuals). Extending a phone from 2 years to 4 halves its per-year manufacturing emissions.

4 · End of life — collection, sorting, recovery, disposal

EPR obligation, Basel Convention, informal sector. Collection rate is the primary bottleneck — the hard part is not the recycling technology, it is getting devices out of people's hands and into certified facilities. Design for disassembly (snap-fits instead of glue, fewer material types, accessible batteries, modular components) cuts recycling cost and lifts recovery rates; the frameworks named were IEC 62474 and iNEMI.

The three legal frameworks

FrameworkJurisdictionWhat it actually requires
WEEE DirectiveEU Manufacturers must finance collection and recycling. Collection target 65% of average weight placed on market over last 3 years; recovery targets 75–85% by device type. Producer Responsibility Organisations (PROs) pool the cost.
E-Waste Management Rules 2021Bangladesh Establishes EPR for EEE manufacturers and importers: registration with the DoE (Department of Environment), take-back schemes, and certified recycler partnerships. Framework exists; enforcement is limited.
Basel ConventionInternational Controls transboundary movement of hazardous waste. The Basel Ban Amendment (2019) prohibits hazardous e-waste export from developed to developing nations. 187 parties; enforcement varies widely. Bangladesh is a signatory.
Watch out — RoHS is not EPR

Students mix these up constantly. RoHS restricts substances (lead, mercury, cadmium, hexavalent chromium, PBB, PBDE) inside new equipment — it is a materials rule. WEEE / EPR assigns financial responsibility for end-of-life collection and recycling — it is a money-and-logistics rule. They were introduced together and reinforce each other, but they do different jobs. The lecture's phrasing: "RoHS + WEEE together transformed European electronics design."

The engineer's liability angle

The faculty ended this section with a line worth quoting back: under EPR, an engineer who specifies toxic materials, designs a device that is hard to disassemble, or picks non-recyclable material combinations is creating a future financial liability for their employer — not merely causing environmental harm. That reframing (environmental decision → balance-sheet decision) is exactly the kind of point that lifts a scenario answer.

Topic 04

How formal recycling actually works — the six stages

Learn these in order. The order itself is the answer to several possible questions.

Formal recycling is a multi-stage industrial process, and each stage exists because of a specific hazard or a specific recovery need. If you learn the reason for each stage rather than just its name, you can reconstruct the list under pressure.

  1. Collection & logistics. Getting devices from consumers to certified facilities — the biggest challenge in the whole chain. Methods: retail take-back (the lecture's example is the Apple Genius Bar), municipal events, mail-in programmes, certified recycler drop-offs. In Bangladesh: informal aggregators, DoE collection drives, and a handful of WEEE-accredited processors.
  2. Data destruction. Before any physical processing, storage media are cryptographically wiped to the NIST 800-88 standard or physically destroyed. This is a privacy-compliance step (GDPR, Bangladesh Data Protection Act), and a business asset needs a certificate of destruction.
  3. Manual pre-processing. Trained technicians remove batteries (a fire hazard if they reach the shredder), capacitors, CRT screens (lead glass), mercury-containing components, and large PCBs for separate handling. This stage protects both the downstream equipment and the workers.
  4. Mechanical shredding & separation. What's left goes into industrial shredders, and the mixed stream is sorted by physics: eddy-current separators for non-ferrous metals, magnetic separators for ferrous metals, density separation for plastics vs metals, and optical/IR sorting.
  5. Hydrometallurgical recovery. Circuit board concentrates go through chemical leaching and solvent extraction to pull out gold, silver, palladium and platinum-group metals. Needs chemical expertise and waste-treatment infrastructure — this is the controlled cousin of the informal acid bath.
  6. Pyrometallurgical smelting. High-temperature smelting (1,300 °C+) of mixed metal fractions recovers copper, precious metals and other base metals. Energy-intensive, but achieves very high recovery across several metal streams at once.
Watch out — the two questions hiding in this list

(a) Why is battery removal before shredding? Fire hazard — lithium cells ignite when punctured. (b) Why does data destruction come before physical processing, not after? Because once a device is shredded you can no longer produce a certificate proving which drive was destroyed, and the chain of custody for the data is broken. Both are classic "explain why this step is ordered where it is" questions.

Topic 05

The circular economy hierarchy

If you memorise one ordered list from this whole lecture, make it this one.

The current IT hardware model is linear: extract → manufacture → use → discard. A circular model tries to keep materials in use at their highest value for as long as possible. The word doing the work there is highest value, and it is what gives the hierarchy its order.

The circular hierarchy — highest to lowest value retention

Reuse → Repair → Refurbish → Remanufacture → Recycle … and below all of these, landfill / burn.

  • Reuse — give or sell the working device to another user. Nothing is consumed; the full embodied carbon keeps doing work.
  • Repair — fix the one broken part and carry on using it.
  • Refurbish — restore to good condition (clean, replace worn parts, re-certify).
  • Remanufacture — industrial-scale restoration to like-new condition.
  • Recycle — break it down to recover raw materials. The last resort, not the goal.
Key idea — recycling is the failure state

This is the single most counter-intuitive point in Class 7, and the faculty hammered it. Recycling recovers materials only. Everything else — the design work, the fabrication energy, the assembly, the functionality — is thrown away. Every earlier stage in the hierarchy retains more of that. So an engineer who makes a battery user-replaceable (enabling Repair) creates far more value than an engineer who optimises the recycling process.

Watch out

An exam question that says "a device has reached end of useful life, what should the engineer do first?" is testing this hierarchy. The wrong-but-tempting answer is "send it to a certified recycler immediately" — it sounds responsible. The correct answer is evaluate whether it can be reused, repaired, or refurbished first, and only recycle what fails all of those tests.

Topic 06

The Bangladesh context

CO2 material, and the faculty's own local-focus section. Scenario questions will very likely be set here.

Bangladesh generates roughly 200,000–400,000 tonnes of e-waste a year, driven by rapid adoption of mobile phones, consumer electronics and industrial equipment. The overwhelming majority is handled by the informal sector.

  • ~100,000 informal e-waste workers, concentrated in Dhaka's Nimtoli, Islampur and Mitford areas.
  • Bangladesh is a net importer of second-hand and end-of-life electronics — from India, the Gulf states, and Southeast Asia.
  • Mobile penetration exceeds 90%, which is a massive future e-waste pipeline, not just a connectivity statistic.
  • Only a handful of DoE-certified formal recyclers currently operate.
  • Lead poisoning from informal CRT and battery recycling is documented in Dhaka's recycling clusters.

On the regulatory side: the E-Waste Management Rules 2021 set up the EPR framework (registration, take-back, certified disposal); DoE enforcement is resource-limited, so most formal activity is registration-only; the National 3R Strategy prioritises the waste hierarchy; and Bangladesh is a Basel Convention party, so hazardous e-waste import is technically regulated.

The framing the faculty wanted

The gap between Bangladesh's formal framework and informal reality was presented as an engineering and entrepreneurial opportunity, not just a failure. Four specific needs were named: affordable informal-to-formal transition models; low-cost testing and sorting equipment for small recyclers; IoT-enabled e-waste collection tracking; and public-private collection schemes. Also note the pragmatic line from Section 4 — formalising the informal sector (training, equipment, certification) is more realistic than eliminating it, because that sector provides livelihoods. A scenario answer that proposes simply banning informal recycling is missing the lecture's point.

Practice A

Numerical practice — worked line by line

Every line's "why" tells you what rule licensed the step. Try each one on paper first.

Question 1 · easy · warm-up

Global e-waste generation in 2023 was 62 Mt, of which 17.4% was formally recycled. How many megatonnes were not formally recycled?

Show worked solution

  1. \( \text{Total} = 62\ \text{Mt},\quad r_{\text{formal}} = 17.4\% \)

    Write down what the question gives you, with units. Both figures are straight from the Class 7 title slide.

  2. \( r_{\text{informal}} = 100\% - 17.4\% = 82.6\% \)

    Complement rule: everything is either formally recycled or it isn't, so the two shares must sum to 100%.

  3. \( 82.6\% = 0.826 \)

    Convert percentage to decimal by dividing by 100, because you cannot multiply a quantity by a "%" symbol.

  4. \( 0.826 \times 62\ \text{Mt} = 51.2\ \text{Mt} \)

    Fraction of a whole: a share of a total is the decimal share multiplied by the total. Units carry through: (dimensionless) × Mt = Mt.

Answer

≈ 51.2 Mt not formally recycled. Sanity check: this should be a bit over four-fifths of 62, and 4/5 of 62 is 49.6 — so 51.2 sits right where it should. The lecture's own rounding ("~83% discarded unsafely") gives 51.5 Mt, which agrees.

Question 2 · easy · warm-up

One tonne of scrapped smartphones yields about 300 g of gold. A conventional gold ore body yields about 5 g per tonne. How many times richer is the e-waste stream, and what term does the lecture use for exploiting this?

Show worked solution

  1. \( C_{\text{ewaste}} = 300\ \text{g/t},\quad C_{\text{ore}} = 5\ \text{g/t} \)

    Both figures are concentrations in the same units (grams per tonne), so they can be compared directly with no conversion.

  2. \( \text{ratio} = \dfrac{C_{\text{ewaste}}}{C_{\text{ore}}} \)

    Definition of "how many times richer": it is a ratio of the two concentrations, not a difference.

  3. \( \dfrac{300\ \text{g/t}}{5\ \text{g/t}} = 60 \)

    Divide. The g/t units cancel top and bottom, leaving a pure number — which is what a "times richer" answer should be.

Answer

60× richer. The term is urban mining. This sits inside the lecture's stated band of "40–80× richer than gold ore", which is your sanity check — an answer of 6 or 600 would tell you the decimal point slipped. The lecture adds that urban mining can be 6–13× more economical than primary mining.

Question 3 · medium · core

A Dhaka recycler processes 2.5 tonnes of scrapped smartphones. Using the lecture's figures — 300 g gold and 15 kg copper per tonne — and taking gold at $60/g and copper at $9,000/tonne, calculate the gross recoverable value of each metal. Then state which metal contributes more, and explain why that might surprise someone.

Show worked solution

  1. \( m = 2.5\ \text{t} \)

    The mass of feedstock. Everything below scales linearly from this, because the lecture's yields are given per tonne.

  2. \( M_{\text{Au}} = 300\ \text{g/t} \times 2.5\ \text{t} = 750\ \text{g} \)

    Yield × mass: a per-tonne yield multiplied by the number of tonnes gives total mass. Units: g/t × t = g.

  3. \( V_{\text{Au}} = 750\ \text{g} \times \$60/\text{g} = \$45{,}000 \)

    Mass × unit price gives value. Units: g × $/g = $, so the grams cancel — confirming the price was quoted in a compatible unit.

  4. \( M_{\text{Cu}} = 15\ \text{kg/t} \times 2.5\ \text{t} = 37.5\ \text{kg} \)

    Same yield × mass step, now for copper. Note the yield is in kg/t, not g/t — read the units before multiplying.

  5. \( 37.5\ \text{kg} = 0.0375\ \text{t} \)

    Unit conversion: copper is priced per tonne, so the mass must be in tonnes before multiplying. Divide by 1,000 because 1 t = 1,000 kg.

  6. \( V_{\text{Cu}} = 0.0375\ \text{t} \times \$9{,}000/\text{t} = \$337.50 \)

    Mass × unit price again, now with matching tonne units so they cancel cleanly.

Answer

Gold ≈ $45,000; copper ≈ $338. Gold contributes far more value here.

Why this can surprise: the lecture states copper is "by weight, the most valuable common metal in e-waste", and by mass it certainly dominates — 37.5 kg of copper against 750 g of gold. But value is mass × price, and gold's price per gram is roughly 6,600 times copper's. So copper wins on tonnage and gold wins on revenue. Read carefully which one the question is asking for. Sanity check on the ratio: 750 g × $60 versus 37.5 kg × $9/kg — the gold figure should land in the tens of thousands and the copper figure in the hundreds, and it does.

Question 4 · medium · core

Bangladesh generates e-waste at the low end of the lecture's range, 200,000 t/year, with a population of about 170 million. (a) Calculate the per-capita generation in kg/person/year. (b) Compare it with the global average of 2.6 kg and with Norway's ~22 kg. (c) What does this comparison imply about the future problem?

Show worked solution

  1. \( W = 200{,}000\ \text{t/yr},\quad P = 170{,}000{,}000 \)

    Total annual waste and population. Per-capita anything is total ÷ population, so those are the only two inputs needed.

  2. \( W = 200{,}000 \times 1{,}000 = 2\times10^{8}\ \text{kg/yr} \)

    Convert tonnes to kilograms (×1,000) before dividing, because the answer is wanted in kg/person and mixing units mid-division is where marks get lost.

  3. \( \text{per capita} = \dfrac{W}{P} = \dfrac{2\times10^{8}}{1.7\times10^{8}} \)

    Definition of per capita: divide the total by the number of people. Writing both in scientific notation makes the division a one-line mental step.

  4. \( \approx 1.18\ \text{kg/person/yr} \)

    Divide the mantissas (2 ÷ 1.7 ≈ 1.18); the \(10^8\) terms cancel exactly, so no power of ten survives.

Answer

(a) ≈ 1.2 kg/person/year.

(b) That is well under half the global average of 2.6 kg, and roughly 1/19th of Norway's 22 kg.

(c) The implication is the important part, and it is the analytical half of the mark. Bangladesh's low per-capita figure reflects lower historical device ownership, not better waste management. With mobile penetration already above 90% and a fast-growing middle class buying first-time electronics, per-capita generation will climb toward the global average. Meanwhile the country has only a handful of DoE-certified recyclers and ~100,000 informal workers. So the current low number is a window of opportunity to build formal capacity before the wave arrives, not evidence that the problem is small.

Question 5 · hard · stretch

An organisation retires 400 laptops. Manufacturing each emitted 300 kg CO₂e (embodied carbon). Option A: send all 400 straight to a certified recycler, which recovers materials but avoids no new manufacturing. Option B: assess them first — 25% are reusable as-is, a further 40% are repairable, and the remaining 35% go to the recycler. Each device kept in service under Option B displaces the manufacture of one new laptop.

(a) How much embodied CO₂e does Option B avoid that Option A does not? (b) Express that as a percentage of the fleet's total embodied carbon. (c) Name the circular-economy principle this demonstrates.

Show worked solution

  1. \( N = 400,\quad e = 300\ \text{kg CO}_2\text{e per device} \)

    Fleet size and per-device embodied carbon — the two numbers everything else is built from.

  2. \( E_{\text{fleet}} = 400 \times 300 = 120{,}000\ \text{kg CO}_2\text{e} \)

    Total = count × per-unit. Needed for part (b), where the answer is a share of this total.

  3. \( f_{\text{kept}} = 25\% + 40\% = 65\% \)

    Add the two retained categories. Reuse and repair both keep a device in service, so both displace a new build — the recycled 35% does not.

  4. \( N_{\text{kept}} = 0.65 \times 400 = 260\ \text{devices} \)

    Share × total converts the percentage into a device count. Check it is a whole number — it is, so no rounding decision is needed.

  5. \( E_{\text{avoided}} = 260 \times 300 = 78{,}000\ \text{kg CO}_2\text{e} \)

    Each retained device displaces one new manufacture (stated in the problem), so the avoided emission is the retained count × the per-device embodied carbon.

  6. \( E_{\text{avoided}} = 78\ \text{t CO}_2\text{e} \)

    Convert kg to tonnes (÷1,000) — reporting environmental figures in tonnes is the convention used throughout the course.

  7. \( \dfrac{78{,}000}{120{,}000} \times 100\% = 65\% \)

    Part as a percentage of whole. It lands exactly on the retained share, which makes sense: every device carries identical embodied carbon, so the carbon share must equal the device share.

Answer

(a) 78 tonnes CO₂e avoided. (b) 65% of the fleet's embodied carbon. (c) The principle is that recycling is the last resort in the circular hierarchy — Reuse and Repair sit above it precisely because they retain the embodied carbon and functionality, whereas recycling recovers materials only.

Sanity check on (b): because every laptop has the same embodied carbon, the percentage of carbon avoided must equal the percentage of laptops retained. If your two answers hadn't matched, that would flag an arithmetic slip. Note also this calculation only counts embodied carbon — the certified recycler in Option A still recovers materials, so the correct answer is not "recycling is worthless", it is "recycling recovers materials but not the 65% of embodied carbon that reuse and repair preserve."

Practice B

Analytical questions — the distinctions the faculty set up

These mirror the six questions in the faculty's own in-class quiz. Each answer explains why the wrong options are tempting.

Question 6 · short answer

A 30-year-old CRT television is being disposed of at an informal recycling yard. Identify the principal toxic material, state the quantity involved, and explain the specific health consequence and why it is worse at an informal site than a formal one.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from This joins Topic 02 (what's inside the device) to Topic 04 (the formal recycling process). The examiner is not just checking whether you can name lead — they want you to connect the material fact to the process fact, i.e. why manual pre-processing exists as stage 3 and what happens when it is skipped.

Model answer. The principal toxic material is lead (Pb), present in the CRT's glass screen in quantities of up to 4 kg per unit. The lead is there deliberately — it shielded the viewer from X-rays emitted by the electron gun (Topic 02).

The health consequence is serious and irreversible neurological damage, and the lecture stressed that children are worst affected, with impacts on IQ, behaviour and development. This is not hypothetical for Bangladesh: lead poisoning from informal CRT and battery recycling is documented in Dhaka's recycling clusters in Nimtoli, Islampur and Mitford (Topic 06).

It is worse informally because of what the formal process does and the informal one doesn't. In a certified facility, stage 3 — manual pre-processing — has trained technicians remove CRT screens specifically so the lead glass is routed to specialist hazardous handling before anything reaches the shredder. Informally the CRT is simply crushed or burned, which releases the lead as dust and fume directly into the air, soil and the workers' bodies, with no containment and no protective equipment. The same mass of lead exists in both cases; the difference is entirely whether a containment step happens (Topic 04).

Question 7 · short answer

Define Extended Producer Responsibility. Then explain its most important effect on product engineering, and name the mechanism by which that effect operates.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from Straight from Topic 03, and the faculty flagged the answer explicitly as being from Velete Ch.7. The trap is answering only the first half. The word "most important effect" is the examiner telling you they want the feedback loop into design, not a list of legal obligations. Answer the definition in one sentence, then spend the rest on the mechanism.

Model answer. Extended Producer Responsibility is the policy principle that a manufacturer is financially and operationally responsible for its products across the entire lifecycle, including collection and recycling at end of life. Its immediate effect is to shift end-of-life costs away from governments and consumers and back onto the company that created the product.

The most important effect on engineering, however, is indirect. Once the manufacturer pays the recycling bill, end-of-life cost becomes a line on their own balance sheet, which gives them a direct financial incentive to design products that are cheaper to recycle. In practice that pushes four specific design changes, all named in the lecture: reducing material complexity (fewer material types means cheaper sorting), eliminating toxic substances (less costly hazardous-waste handling), designing for disassembly (lower labour cost to process), and extending product life (fewer units entering the waste stream at all).

The mechanism, then, is a market feedback loop running from end-of-life economics back into design decisions. This is why the lecture pairs EPR with RoHS: WEEE (which implements EPR in the EU) plus RoHS (which restricts substances) together transformed European electronics design. And it is why the faculty framed the engineer's position as one of liability — specifying a toxic material or a glued-shut enclosure now creates a future financial liability for the employer, not merely an environmental harm.

Question 8 · short answer

Distinguish between RoHS, WEEE and the Basel Convention. For each, state what it regulates and give one concrete requirement or threshold.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from Topic 03, the regulatory frameworks. This is a pure discrimination question — the three instruments are frequently mentioned in the same breath, and the examiner wants to know whether you can separate them. The structure of your answer should make the separation obvious: one sentence of what kind of thing it regulates before any detail.

RoHS — a substances rule. The EU Restriction of Hazardous Substances Directive prohibits specified hazardous substances inside new electrical and electronic equipment. The six named in Class 7 are lead, mercury, cadmium, hexavalent chromium, PBB and PBDE. Concrete effect: it forced the replacement of lead solder with tin-silver-copper alloys. It constrains material choice at the manufacturing stage.

WEEE — a financial-responsibility rule. The EU Waste Electrical and Electronic Equipment Directive requires manufacturers to finance collection and recycling systems; it is the instrument that implements EPR in the EU. Concrete requirement: a collection target of 65% of the average weight placed on the market over the previous three years, and recovery targets of 75–85% depending on device type. Producer Responsibility Organisations pool the cost across manufacturers.

Basel Convention — a movement rule. An international treaty controlling the transboundary movement of hazardous waste, including e-waste. Concrete requirement: the Basel Ban Amendment (2019) prohibits export of hazardous e-waste from developed to developing nations. It has 187 parties including Bangladesh, but the lecture notes that enforcement varies widely — which is precisely why e-waste still reaches Bangladesh mislabelled as second-hand goods.

The distinction in one line: RoHS governs what goes in the product, WEEE governs who pays when it comes out, and Basel governs where it is allowed to travel.

Question 9 · short answer

Which mechanism does Bangladesh's E-Waste Management Rules 2021 establish for making manufacturers responsible, and what are the three specific obligations it places on them? Comment on the gap between the framework and reality.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from Topic 06 plus the framework table in Topic 03. The faculty asked a version of this in the in-class quiz, and the phrasing "which specific mechanism" is a hint that they want the term EPR named, not a general description. The final sentence about the gap is where the analytical marks sit.

Model answer. The Rules establish an Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) framework covering EEE manufacturers and importers. The three obligations are: (1) registration with the Department of Environment (DoE); (2) operating take-back schemes for end-of-life products; and (3) partnering with certified recyclers for disposal. The Rules also reference RoHS thresholds as guidance for domestic product safety standards.

On the gap: the framework exists on paper but DoE enforcement is resource-limited, and the lecture's assessment is that most formal activity is registration-only — companies register, but the take-back and certified-disposal legs largely do not function. Meanwhile 200,000–400,000 t/year continues to flow through roughly 100,000 informal workers, with only a handful of certified recyclers operating nationally.

The lecture's framing of that gap is worth reproducing: it is an engineering and entrepreneurial opportunity rather than simply a policy failure. Four needs were named — affordable informal-to-formal transition models, low-cost testing and sorting equipment for small recyclers, IoT-enabled collection tracking, and public-private collection schemes. And crucially, the realistic goal is formalising the informal sector through training, equipment and certification, not eliminating it, because that sector currently provides around 100,000 livelihoods.

Question 10 · long answer

A phone manufacturer glues the battery in place to achieve a thinner form factor. Analyse the circular-economy consequences of this decision and state what the engineer should do, with reference to relevant regulation.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from This is the faculty's own design scenario from Section 6, and it asks you to trace a single engineering decision through the circular hierarchy (Topic 05) and out into regulation (Topic 03). The examiner wants a causal chain: design choice → which stage of the hierarchy it blocks → what value is lost → what should be done instead. A one-line answer of "glue is bad" earns almost nothing.

Model answer. The decision to glue the battery removes the possibility of user replacement, and battery replacement is the single most common repair that extends a phone's useful life. Lithium-ion cells degrade meaningfully after roughly 500 charge cycles, so this is not an edge case — it is the ordinary end-of-life trigger for a phone.

Tracing it through the circular hierarchy (Topic 05): the intended path when a battery degrades is Repair — replace a ~$20 component and continue using the device. Gluing the battery closes that path, and also makes Refurbish and Remanufacture harder, because all three depend on being able to open the device and swap the consumable part. The device therefore jumps straight from use to Recycle, which is the last resort in the hierarchy. The value lost is large and specific: recycling recovers materials only, so the embodied carbon — around 70 kg CO₂e for a smartphone, which is 70–80% of its lifetime footprint (Topic 03) — is discarded along with the design, fabrication and assembly value. An entire device is written off to avoid replacing a $20 part.

There is also a stage-4 recycling cost. Batteries must be removed manually before shredding because they are a fire hazard (Topic 04, stage 3). Glued batteries make that manual pre-processing slower and more dangerous, raising the recycler's per-unit cost — which, under an EPR regime, is a cost the manufacturer itself will eventually bear.

What the engineer should do: advocate for an accessible, user-replaceable battery design — mechanical fasteners or pull-tab adhesives rather than permanent glue — and treat the marginal thickness gained as a trade-off to be justified, not a free win. This is no longer merely good practice: the EU Right to Repair regulation now requires accessible battery design for many product categories, along with spare parts availability for 7–10 years and publicly available repair manuals (Topic 03). An engineer specifying a glued battery for a product sold in the EU is designing in a compliance problem as well as an environmental one.

Question 11 · long answer

EWU's IT department must replace 100 computers. A vendor offers new machines at $500 each, or certified refurbished machines at $280 each. Recommend a course of action, justify it on both financial and environmental grounds, and specify what due diligence you would require.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from The faculty's procurement scenario from Section 6. It tests whether you can apply the circular hierarchy (Topic 05) to a purchasing decision rather than a disposal decision — the hierarchy usually gets taught as "what to do with old kit", and the examiner is checking you understand it also governs what you buy. The due-diligence part is what separates a good answer from a complete one.

Recommendation: buy the certified refurbished machines.

Financial case. 100 × ($500 − $280) = $22,000 saved on acquisition, a 44% reduction in capital outlay for the same fleet size.

Environmental case. Buying refurbished keeps 100 devices circulating in the Remanufacture loop rather than triggering demand for 100 newly manufactured units. Since 70–80% of a computer's lifetime carbon is embodied in manufacturing (Topic 03), avoiding that manufacture is where nearly all the environmental benefit sits — far more than any operational efficiency difference between the two options. It also delays 100 devices entering the waste stream, which matters given only a handful of DoE-certified recyclers operate in Bangladesh (Topic 06).

Due diligence required before signing. "Refurbished" is only as good as the refurbisher, so the tender should require:

  • A recognised certification standard — the lecture names R2 or RIOS.
  • A data wipe certificate from the refurbisher, to the NIST 800-88 standard (Topic 04, stage 2) — these machines held someone else's data.
  • Warranty coverage, since the reliability risk is the genuine trade-off against the lower price.
  • Performance specification compatibility with the intended workload.

Conclusion. With those four conditions met, this is the better decision financially and environmentally — which is the wider point the faculty was making. Green procurement is frequently framed as paying a premium for virtue; here, as in most of the circular-economy cases in this course, the circular option is simply cheaper.

Question 12 · long answer

You are asked to design an e-waste collection system for a university campus in Dhaka. Describe your design: how devices are collected, where they go, and how the scheme is made financially self-sustaining. Reference the relevant Bangladeshi regulatory and sector context.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from This is the faculty's own discussion prompt from Section 7, so it is a strong candidate for a long-answer question. It pulls together Topic 04 (the process stages), Topic 05 (the hierarchy) and Topic 06 (the Bangladesh context). The examiner wants a design that is ordered by the hierarchy and honest about the local constraints — a generic "put bins in the corridor" answer misses both.

Design principle. The system must be organised around the circular hierarchy (Topic 05), not around a recycling bin. Recycling is the last resort, so the collection flow should be a triage funnel in which every device is assessed before disposal is even considered.

1 · Collection. Staffed drop-off points in each faculty building plus the library, with periodic collection drives timed to semester end when students discard the most. Collection rate is the primary bottleneck in the whole chain (Topic 04, stage 1), so the design priority is convenience and visibility, not container sophistication. Log every intake item — device type, age, condition — because that register is what makes the later stages and any Scope 3 reporting possible.

2 · Data destruction before anything else. Staff and student devices carry personal data. Every storage device is cryptographically wiped to NIST 800-88 or physically destroyed, with a certificate issued for university-owned assets (Topic 04, stage 2). Doing this at intake, not at disposal, preserves the chain of custody.

3 · Triage down the hierarchy. Working devices go to Reuse — reallocation to departments with lower specification needs, student loan pools, or IoT and lab sensor projects. Devices needing minor work go to Repair, which can be run as a supervised student workshop, giving the scheme an academic function as well as a logistical one. Devices beyond economic repair but structurally sound go to Refurbish for donation to schools or NGOs. Only what fails all three goes onward.

4 · Certified disposal. The residue goes to a DoE-certified recycler. This is the hard constraint in the Bangladeshi context — only a handful operate nationally (Topic 06) — so the partnership must be secured before the scheme launches, not after. Hazardous items identified at intake (CRT monitors with up to 4 kg of lead, batteries, mercury-containing backlights) are segregated for specialist handling, mirroring stage 3 manual pre-processing.

5 · Financial self-sustainability. Four revenue and cost-avoidance streams:

  • Material value from the certified recycler — the urban mine is real, and copper and precious-metal content in bulk PCB and cabling has a market price (Topic 02).
  • Avoided procurement cost — every reused or repaired device is a machine the university does not buy, which is the largest financial line by a distance and should be accounted for explicitly.
  • Refurbished sales of devices surplus to internal need, into the local second-hand market.
  • Manufacturer take-back — under the E-Waste Management Rules 2021, manufacturers and importers are obliged to operate take-back schemes, so the university should push collection cost onto vendors as a contractual term in its hardware tenders rather than absorbing it.

Regulatory framing. The scheme sits inside the E-Waste Management Rules 2021 EPR framework and supports the National 3R Strategy's waste hierarchy. Given that DoE enforcement is limited and most formal activity is registration-only (Topic 06), a functioning university scheme is genuinely useful as a demonstration model — which is the entrepreneurial opportunity the faculty pointed at. A realistic design also engages the informal sector rather than excluding it: local aggregators already have collection reach the university does not, and the lecture's stated position is that formalising that sector through training and certification is more achievable than eliminating it.

Question 13 · long answer · stretch

An employee finds 30 old but functional smartphones in a storage room, about to be sent to the e-waste bin. Set out the correct organisational response, and explain what reporting obligation attaches to the outcome.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from The faculty's third scenario card from Section 6. It looks trivial but there is a specific trap: the instinctive "responsible" answer is to send them to a certified recycler, and that is wrong under the hierarchy because the devices still work. The reporting clause at the end connects Topic 05 to the governance material that returns in Class 13.

Model answer. The devices are functional, so the circular hierarchy (Topic 05) requires that they be assessed against each stage in order before disposal is considered at all. Sending working hardware to a recycler destroys functional value and is the wrong first move.

Step 1 — Reuse. Are any suitable for redeployment as-is? The lecture's own examples: security staff handsets, a student loan pool, or repurposing as IoT sensor nodes — an outdated smartphone still has a camera, radios and a processor far beyond what a sensor node needs.

Step 2 — Refurbish for donation. If not needed internally, can they be restored to good condition and donated to schools or NGOs? This retains almost all the embodied value.

Step 3 — Repair. Are any recoverable with minor work — a battery, a screen — that would make them viable for either of the two routes above?

Step 4 — Certified recycling for whatever genuinely fails all of the above, with data destruction to NIST 800-88 first (Topic 04, stage 2), which for phones is non-negotiable given what they store.

Reporting obligation. The disposal route must be documented as part of the organisation's Scope 3 e-waste reporting. This matters for two reasons. First, Scope 3 covers supply-chain and end-of-life emissions, so how a device leaves the organisation is an accounting fact, not just an operational one. Second, an organisation cannot demonstrate compliance with EPR-linked take-back obligations, or claim credit for circular practice in an ESG report, without a documented chain of custody — which is exactly why the asset register and certificate of destruction exist.

The general rule to state at the end: the circular hierarchy should be applied before the recycling bin, not after it.

Last-minute revision — Class 7

The four headline numbers

62 Mt generated (2023) · 17.4% formally recycled · $57 bn value lost/yr · 2.6 kg per person per year.

The hierarchy (memorise the order)

Reuse → Repair → Refurbish → Remanufacture → Recycle. Recycling is the LAST resort, not the goal.

EPR in one line

Manufacturer pays for end-of-life → financial incentive to design for recyclability → feedback loop from disposal economics into design.

RoHS vs WEEE vs Basel

RoHS = what goes IN (6 substances). WEEE = who PAYS (65% collection target). Basel = where it may TRAVEL (Ban Amendment 2019).

The 6 recycling stages

Collection → Data destruction → Manual pre-processing → Shred & separate → Hydrometallurgy → Pyrometallurgy (1,300 °C+).

Why batteries come out first

Fire hazard in the shredder. Why data destruction is stage 2: once shredded, chain of custody is unprovable.

Urban mine

1 t of phones ≈ 300 g gold, 40–80× richer than ore. Urban mining 6–13× more economical than primary mining.

Gold vs copper trap

Gold = highest value per gram. Copper = most valuable BY WEIGHT (~15 kg/t, 95%+ recovery). Read which one is asked.

Toxic four

Lead (CRT glass, up to 4 kg) · Mercury (backlights) · Cadmium (batteries) · Chromium VI (coatings). All RoHS-restricted.

Critical / conflict minerals

Cobalt (DRC ~70%, recycled ~5%) · Lithium (recycled ~1%) · Tantalum (conflict mineral, Dodd-Frank §1502).

Bangladesh numbers

200,000–400,000 t/yr · ~100,000 informal workers (Nimtoli, Islampur, Mitford) · >90% mobile penetration · net importer.

BD regulation

E-Waste Rules 2021 = EPR: DoE registration + take-back + certified recycler. Enforcement weak, mostly registration-only.

Embodied carbon

70–80% of a device's lifetime CO₂e is manufacturing, before first use. Smartphone ≈ 70 kg CO₂e. Wafer ≈ 2,200 L water.

The informal sector line

Formalise it (training, equipment, certification) — do NOT propose eliminating it. It provides ~100,000 livelihoods.

Engineer's liability framing

Under EPR, specifying toxic materials or a glued enclosure creates a future FINANCIAL liability for the employer.
