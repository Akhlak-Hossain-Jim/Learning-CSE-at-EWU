How to use this guide

Class 8 — Sustainable Hardware. Velete Ch.8 (all). Course outcomes CO1 and CO2.

Class 7 asked what happens to hardware at the end. Class 8 goes back to the beginning: how you choose, specify and design it. The faculty framed the whole lecture around one claim, and everything else in it is downstream of that claim, so make sure you can state it cold.

The one-sentence version of Class 8

70–80% of a device's lifetime carbon is emitted during manufacturing, before it is ever switched on. So the biggest environmental lever an engineer has is procurement and design — what you buy and how long you keep it — not how efficiently the thing runs.

This guide covers the five certifications, lifecycle assessment, the seven sins of greenwashing, the procurement scorecard, and the four engineering roles. The practice sections are heavy on total cost of ownership arithmetic, because that is where this topic's numerical questions live, and on claim-analysis scenarios, because the greenwashing detector was the most interactive part of the class and is very likely to appear as a written question.

Topic 01

Embodied carbon vs operational carbon

Get this distinction right and half of Class 8 follows automatically.

Think about a laptop's whole life as two separate bills. The first bill is paid before you ever open the box: mining the metals, refining them, fabricating chips, assembling, shipping, and eventually disposing of it. The second bill is paid a little at a time, every day you use it, as electricity. The lecture gives these two bills names.

The two definitions — Velete Ch.8

Embodied carbon — the CO₂e emitted to manufacture, transport and eventually dispose of a device. It is "locked in" before the device is used at all.

Operational carbon — the CO₂e emitted while using the device, from electricity consumption.

Which of the two dominates is not a fixed fact about electronics — it depends entirely on the device, and that dependency is the examinable point.

Consumer device (phone / laptop)Enterprise server (24/7)
Manufacturing share70–80% of lifetime footprint20–40% — operational catches up
Most important actionExtend device lifespanMaximise utilisation (virtualisation)
Second priorityChoose repairability (Fairphone scores highest)Choose energy-efficient models (Energy Star, EPEAT)
Third priority—Power from renewables (Scope 2 reduction)

The supporting figures the faculty put on screen: manufacturing one laptop takes about 1,500 kWh — equivalent to roughly three years of operating it. Buying a new phone every year rather than every four produces about 3× more carbon per year of use. And a modern smartphone contains 60+ elements from the periodic table.

Watch out — the biggest trap in this topic

"Sustainable hardware" instinctively means "low power consumption". For a server running around the clock for years, that instinct is roughly right. For a phone or a laptop it is wrong, and the exam will probe exactly that. If a question compares a slightly less efficient device kept twice as long against a more efficient one replaced on schedule, the longer-lived device usually wins, because you are comparing a small operational saving against an entire second manufacturing event.

Topic 02

The five certifications

The faculty built a five-tab explorer around these. Know what each covers — and, just as important, what each does not.

Walk into an electronics shop and every box has a green claim on it. Most are marketing. Five represent real third-party verification with technical requirements behind them, and the faculty was explicit that knowing which is which is a core professional competency. Crucially, the five do not overlap much — each covers a different dimension, which is why a complete procurement specification names several of them.

Energy Star — US EPA · voluntary

What it means: the product falls in the top 25% of energy efficiency for its category. Covers computers, monitors, servers, storage, imaging equipment and data centres.

  • Covers: operational energy efficiency only — electricity used in active and idle states.
  • Typical saving: Energy Star computers use 30–65% less energy than standard models. For a 200-computer office that is $10,000–$30,000/year in electricity.
  • Procurement weight: mandatory for US federal procurement; referenced in the Bangladesh ICT Policy 2018 for government purchases.
  • Limitation (examinable): covers only operational energy. A product can be Energy Star certified while being built with toxic materials, having no recycling programme, and carrying large manufacturing emissions.

EPEAT — Green Electronics Council · Bronze / Silver / Gold

What it means: a comprehensive lifecycle environmental rating, going well beyond Energy Star. Products are scored on required and optional criteria.

  • Covers: materials selection (hazardous substance restrictions), product longevity and upgradability, end-of-life management (take-back programmes), energy conservation, and corporate performance.
  • The tiers — learn the exact thresholds: Bronze = meets all required criteria. Silver = Bronze + 50% of optional criteria. Gold = Bronze + 75% of optional criteria. Gold is the benchmark for best-in-class.
  • Procurement weight: required by US federal agencies; referenced in EU Green Public Procurement criteria.
  • Bangladesh: not formally required in government procurement, but used as a reference standard by foreign-funded projects (World Bank, ADB) and by Bangladeshi firms serving EU/US markets.

TCO Certified — TCO Development, Sweden

What it means: widely considered the most rigorous and comprehensive sustainable electronics certification available. Its distinguishing feature is examinable in one word: social.

  • Environmental scope: hazardous substances, energy efficiency, lifespan design, recycling programmes, packaging, corporate environmental reporting — with annual factory audits by third-party verifiers.
  • Social scope (unique to TCO): working conditions in factories — minimum wage, working hours, prohibition of child labour and forced labour, health and safety. Supply-chain traceability required.
  • Conflict minerals: requires documented responsible sourcing of the 3TG — tin, tantalum, tungsten, gold — per OECD Due Diligence Guidance, going beyond US Dodd-Frank requirements.
  • Best for: organisations with ESG reporting obligations or European institutional clients.

80 PLUS — Plug Load Solutions · PSU efficiency

What it means: the power supply unit converts AC mains to DC at at least 80% efficiency at 20%, 50% and 100% load. Higher tiers demand more. Every watt lost in that conversion becomes waste heat.

80 PLUS tiers — efficiency at 50% load

Basic 80% · Bronze 85% · Silver 88% · Gold 92% · Platinum 94% · Titanium 96%

  • The lecture's worked example: a 500 W server with an 80%-efficient PSU wastes 100 W as heat. The same server with a 93% Gold PSU wastes only 38 W — saving 62 W continuously, or about 543 kWh/year per server.
  • The compounding effect: PSU losses compound with PUE. A watt wasted in the PSU becomes roughly 1.5 W wasted in total, because the cooling system must then remove the extra heat. Gold is the minimum recommended for data centres.
  • Bangladesh note: many locally assembled PCs use budget PSUs at 70–75% efficiency. Specifying 80 PLUS Gold is a low-cost, high-impact green procurement requirement with fast payback.

RoHS — European Union · legally binding for the EU market

What it means: restricts hazardous substances in electrical and electronic equipment sold in the EU. Originally six substances (2003), expanded to ten in RoHS 3 (2019).

  • The substances: lead (Pb), mercury (Hg), cadmium (Cd), hexavalent chromium (Cr VI), polybrominated biphenyls (PBB), polybrominated diphenyl ethers (PBDE), plus four phthalates added in RoHS 3.
  • Maximum concentration values: 0.1% by weight for most substances; 0.01% for cadmium. These are the numbers to quote.
  • Why it went global: compliance is mandatory to sell in the EU, and manufacturers rarely make EU-only product variants — so a European rule reshaped worldwide supply chains. The lecture calls it "the most globally influential materials standard in electronics history".
  • Link back to Class 7: RoHS-compliant devices contain far less toxic material, which makes informal recycling less hazardous and formal recycling more economically viable, since less hazardous-waste treatment is required.
  • Bangladesh: not domestically mandated, but any Bangladeshi exporter to the EU must supply RoHS-compliant products, and the E-Waste Rules 2021 reference RoHS thresholds as guidance for domestic safety standards.
The faculty's own quick-reference — reproduce this in a procurement question

Energy efficiency only: Energy Star + 80 PLUS Gold (PSU). Full lifecycle sustainability: EPEAT Gold or TCO Certified. Hazardous substance compliance: RoHS. A complete green procurement spec: require all four — they address different dimensions and do not overlap significantly.

Watch out — the three most-confused pairs

Energy Star vs EPEAT: Energy Star is operational energy only; EPEAT is full lifecycle including materials and end-of-life. A device can be Energy Star and still be environmentally poor overall.
EPEAT vs TCO Certified: both are lifecycle standards, but only TCO includes social responsibility — labour conditions in the supply chain. That is the discriminator.
80 PLUS vs Energy Star: 80 PLUS rates only the power supply unit's conversion efficiency; Energy Star rates the whole product's energy consumption.

Topic 03

Lifecycle Assessment (LCA)

The method, and the one conclusion the faculty wanted you to draw from it.

A Lifecycle Assessment quantifies the total environmental impact of a product from raw material extraction through manufacturing, use and disposal — the standard phrase is "cradle to grave". It is a method, not a certification; EPEAT and TCO both draw on LCA thinking, but LCA itself is just the accounting exercise.

The reason the faculty spent twelve minutes on it is a single counter-intuitive finding: the phase engineers spend most of their time optimising — operational energy — is often not the largest contributor to total impact. For a smartphone the total lifetime figure given was around 70 kg CO₂e, dominated by manufacturing.

Key idea — the LCA insight that changes priorities

For embodied-carbon-dominant devices, buying a slightly less energy-efficient model and keeping it twice as long is usually better for the environment than buying the most energy-efficient model and replacing it on schedule. Longevity beats efficiency. Say this sentence in any question that pits efficiency against lifespan.

What the LCA implies for design

  • Prioritise repairability — a user-replaceable battery is the number-one longevity feature.
  • Modular design — upgrade CPU/RAM without replacing the whole unit.
  • Software support commitment — minimum 5 years of OS updates.
  • Durable materials — scratch and drop resistance reduces cosmetic-damage-driven replacement.
  • Spare parts guaranteed for 7–10 years (the EU Right to Repair requirement).

What it implies for procurement

  • Specify a minimum 4-year refresh cycle, not 2–3 years.
  • Require a manufacturer sustainability report with per-product LCA data.
  • Evaluate total cost of ownership including energy over 4 years, not sticker price.
  • Prefer EPEAT Gold / TCO Certified as the baseline.
  • Consider certified refurbished for non-critical workloads.
Topic 04

Greenwashing and the seven sins

The faculty's most interactive section. Learn the seven labels — then learn to apply them to a claim you have not seen before.

Greenwashing is the practice of making misleading or exaggerated environmental claims about a product or company. The lecture's point was that it is rarely outright lying; far more often it is a statement that is technically true and deeply misleading. Engineers specifying hardware must be able to identify and reject it.

The seven sins of greenwashing (TerraChoice)

1 · Hidden trade-off — one green attribute conceals other harms.
2 · No proof — the claim is unverifiable.
3 · Vagueness — "eco-friendly", with no definition.
4 · Irrelevance — true, but meaningless.
5 · Lesser of two evils — greener within an inherently harmful category.
6 · Fibbing — simply false.
7 · False labels — fake certification logos.

The five claims the faculty analysed

These are worth studying as patterns of reasoning, because the exam will hand you a claim you have not seen and ask you to do the same analysis.

1. "Our laptop uses 40% less energy than the previous model." Verdict: mixed — potentially misleading. It could be genuine, but only if the previous model was the market standard; if the predecessor was unusually power-hungry, the new one might still be below average. The questions to ask: compared to what baseline? Is there a third-party certification (Energy Star) confirming it? What happened to manufacturing emissions?

2. "Our servers are built with eco-friendly materials." Verdict: greenwashing — the vagueness sin. "Eco-friendly" is meaningless without specification. RoHS compliant? Recycled content? Sustainably sourced? Recyclable? Any company can print the phrase with zero verification. The rule the faculty gave: if a company cannot tell you exactly what "eco-friendly" means in numbers or certifications, assume it means nothing.

3. "This monitor is Energy Star certified and uses 35% less energy — saving you $28/year in electricity." Verdict: genuine. This is the model of what a good claim looks like: a real third-party certification, a specific measurable metric, and a calculated financial benefit the buyer can verify against their own tariff. Specific standard + specific metric + specific benefit in user-relevant terms. Still fair to ask about manufacturing and end-of-life, but the operational claim itself is credible.

4. "Our company is committed to being carbon neutral by 2040." Verdict: insufficient without details. A commitment 15+ years out with no interim milestones and no third-party accountability is cheap to make. The four questions: Does "carbon neutral" include Scope 3? What are the 2025 and 2030 interim targets? Is it verified by the Science Based Targets initiative (SBTi)? Is offsetting doing most of the work instead of actual emission reduction?

5. "This printer is CFC-free!" Verdict: greenwashing — the irrelevance sin. CFCs have been banned under the Montreal Protocol since 1987. Every printer is CFC-free. The claim creates an impression of virtue while saying nothing about the product. The faculty's phrase: "lesser compliance masquerading as achievement."

Watch out — the test to apply to any claim

Three questions, in order. (1) Is it specific? Numbers, not adjectives. (2) Is it verifiable? Named third-party standard, not self-declaration. (3) Is it in context? Compared against a meaningful baseline, and does it address the dominant impact — for a consumer device that means manufacturing, not just operation. A claim that passes all three is credible. A claim that fails any one of them needs the specific sin named.

Topic 05

The green procurement scorecard and the engineer's four roles

The applied end of the lecture — and where the scenario questions will come from.

The faculty's scorecard rates a product across six sustainability dimensions, each scored 1–3 stars on evidence, not marketing claims. Maximum 18. The bands given were: 14–18 excellent · 9–13 acceptable · below 9 avoid.

The six scorecard dimensions

1 · Energy certification  ·  2 · Lifecycle certification  ·  3 · Hazardous substance compliance  ·  4 · Repairability  ·  5 · Recycled content  ·  6 · Take-back programme
1★ = no evidence / poor · 2★ = partial / unverified · 3★ = strong evidence / certified

The four roles

The lecture closed by assigning specific levers to four different jobs. A scenario question may put you in one of these roles, so know which levers belong to which.

  • Product engineers (hardware designers) — choose RoHS-compliant materials, design for disassembly, make the battery user-replaceable, minimise the number of distinct material types, commit to spare parts availability, use recycled content where structurally valid. Their decisions at design stage lock in most of the product's lifetime impact.
  • Systems / infrastructure engineers — specify Energy Star, EPEAT and 80 PLUS Gold in procurement requirements; maximise virtualisation before adding hardware; choose servers with high utilisation capability; design for the longest possible refresh cycle with upgrade paths; document asset lifecycle for Scope 3 reporting.
  • Procurement officers / IT managers — write sustainability requirements into tender documents rather than judging on price alone; evaluate TCO over 4–5 years; maintain asset registers with acquisition dates to trigger certified disposal; prefer certified refurbished for non-critical use.
  • Software engineers — efficient code means less hardware, which means fewer devices and less manufacturing carbon. The lecture's example: a software team cutting CPU usage by 30% may defer a hardware upgrade cycle by two years across a fleet. Sustainability is a software quality metric, not just a hardware one.
Key idea — the procurement multiplier (Velete Ch.8)

A single procurement officer signing one hardware tender determines the environmental performance of thousands of devices over 4–5 years. Writing "EPEAT Gold required" into a 500-laptop tender has more environmental impact than any individual action any of those 500 users will ever take — including recycling them at end of life. Specification is the highest-leverage moment in the whole lifecycle. This is the quotable line of Class 8.

Practice A

Numerical practice — worked line by line

TCO comparisons, PSU efficiency, and energy savings. Attempt each on paper first.

Question 1 · easy · warm-up

A 500 W server uses a PSU that is 80% efficient. (a) How much power is wasted as heat? (b) Repeat for a 93% efficient 80 PLUS Gold PSU. (c) How many kWh per year does upgrading save, running continuously?

Show worked solution

  1. \( P_{\text{out}} = 500\ \text{W},\quad \eta_1 = 0.80 \)

    The 500 W is the DC power the server actually needs; efficiency is the fraction of drawn AC power that reaches it. Write efficiency as a decimal so it can divide.

  2. \( P_{\text{in}} = \dfrac{P_{\text{out}}}{\eta} \)

    Definition of efficiency: \(\eta = P_{\text{out}}/P_{\text{in}}\). Rearranging for the input power is the step that lets you find the waste.

  3. \( P_{\text{in},1} = \dfrac{500}{0.80} = 625\ \text{W} \)

    Substitute. The wall must supply 625 W for the server to receive 500 W.

  4. \( P_{\text{waste},1} = 625 - 500 = 125\ \text{W} \)

    Conservation of energy: whatever goes in and does not come out as usable DC leaves as heat.

  5. \( P_{\text{in},2} = \dfrac{500}{0.93} = 537.6\ \text{W} \)

    Same efficiency definition, new efficiency value for the Gold PSU.

  6. \( P_{\text{waste},2} = 537.6 - 500 = 37.6\ \text{W} \)

    Same conservation step. Compare with the lecture's stated "only 38 W" — it matches, confirming the method.

  7. \( \Delta P = 125 - 37.6 = 87.4\ \text{W} = 0.0874\ \text{kW} \)

    Difference of the two waste figures is the continuous saving. Convert W to kW (÷1,000) because annual energy is conventionally in kWh.

  8. \( E = P \times t = 0.0874 \times 8{,}760 = 765.6\ \text{kWh/yr} \)

    Energy = power × time. 8,760 h is one full year (365 × 24) — the figure to use whenever a problem says "continuously" or "24/7".

Answer

(a) 125 W wasted · (b) ≈ 37.6 W wasted · (c) ≈ 766 kWh/year saved.

Note on reading the lecture: the slide computes the saving as 62 W (100 W → 38 W) because it treats the 500 W as the drawn power rather than the delivered power. Both framings appear in textbooks. State your assumption in one line — "taking 500 W as the server's DC load" — and either is defensible. Sanity check: the answer must be a few hundred kWh/year, since ~90 W continuous over a year is roughly 0.09 × 8,760 ≈ 790.

Question 2 · easy · warm-up

Following the lecture's PUE-compounding rule, a watt wasted in the PSU costs about 1.5 W in total facility power. A data centre has 200 servers, each wasting an extra 62 W because budget PSUs were specified instead of 80 PLUS Gold. At $0.12/kWh, what is the annual cost of that specification decision?

Show worked solution

  1. \( P_{\text{IT waste}} = 200 \times 62 = 12{,}400\ \text{W} \)

    Per-unit × count. This is the waste at the IT layer only — before cooling is accounted for.

  2. \( P_{\text{facility}} = 12{,}400 \times 1.5 = 18{,}600\ \text{W} \)

    Apply the compounding multiplier. The extra heat must be removed by the cooling plant, which itself draws power — so the true facility cost is 1.5× the IT-layer figure.

  3. \( = 18.6\ \text{kW} \)

    Convert W to kW (÷1,000) before combining with a per-kWh tariff.

  4. \( E = 18.6 \times 8{,}760 = 162{,}936\ \text{kWh/yr} \)

    Energy = power × time, with 8,760 hours for continuous annual operation.

  5. \( C = 162{,}936 \times \$0.12 = \$19{,}552 \)

    Energy × tariff = cost. Units: kWh × $/kWh = $, so kWh cancels.

Answer

≈ $19,552 per year, every year, from one line in a tender document.

This is the numerical form of the procurement multiplier point. Sanity check: had you forgotten the 1.5× PUE factor you would have got ≈ $13,035 — so if your answer is exactly two-thirds of the expected value, that is the step you dropped.

Question 3 · medium · core

Product A costs $400, has no certifications, and a 3-year lifespan. Product B costs $520, is EPEAT Gold and Energy Star certified with an 80 PLUS Gold PSU, and has a 5-year lifespan. Energy Star saves about $30/year. Compare the two over a 5-year horizon and recommend one.

Show worked solution

  1. \( T = 5\ \text{years} \)

    Fix a common time horizon first. You cannot compare a 3-year product with a 5-year product until both are expressed over the same period — this is the whole trick of the question.

  2. \( n_A = \dfrac{5}{3} \approx 1.67\ \text{purchases} \)

    Lifespan ÷ into horizon: a 3-year product must be bought 5/3 times to cover 5 years. Keep the fraction rather than rounding up to 2, since the lecture's own worked answer uses the ratio.

  3. \( C_A = \$400 \times \dfrac{5}{3} = \$667 \)

    Unit price × number of purchases gives the 5-year acquisition cost of Product A.

  4. \( n_B = \dfrac{5}{5} = 1 \Rightarrow C_B = \$520 \)

    Product B's lifespan already covers the horizon, so it is bought once — no multiplier.

  5. \( S_{\text{energy}} = \$30 \times 5 = \$150 \)

    Annual saving × horizon. Only Product B earns this, because only Product B carries the Energy Star certification.

  6. \( C_B^{\text{net}} = \$520 - \$150 = \$370 \)

    Subtract operational savings from acquisition cost to get effective total cost of ownership. This is the step that makes it a TCO comparison rather than a price comparison.

  7. \( \$667 - \$370 = \$297 \)

    Difference of the two TCO figures — the actual saving from choosing B.

Answer

Product B. Over 5 years it effectively costs $370 against Product A's $667 — roughly half — while being significantly greener.

The point the faculty wanted: green procurement is usually better value, not worse. The $120 sticker premium is an illusion created by ignoring lifespan and energy. Sanity check: B is cheaper on both counts once normalised, so the conclusion should not be close — and it isn't. Worth adding in an exam: this ignores disposal cost and the embodied carbon of the extra 0.67 units Product A requires, both of which would widen the gap further.

Question 4 · medium · core

A 200-computer office replaces standard machines with Energy Star models using 45% less energy. Each standard machine consumed 320 kWh/year. At $0.12/kWh: (a) annual kWh saved; (b) annual money saved; (c) if each new machine cost $40 more, what is the simple payback period?

Show worked solution

  1. \( E_{\text{old,unit}} = 320\ \text{kWh/yr},\quad r = 45\% = 0.45 \)

    Per-machine baseline consumption and the fractional reduction, converted to a decimal so it can multiply.

  2. \( \Delta E_{\text{unit}} = 0.45 \times 320 = 144\ \text{kWh/yr} \)

    Percentage saving × baseline gives the saving per machine. Note it is 45% of the old figure, not of the new one.

  3. \( \Delta E_{\text{total}} = 144 \times 200 = 28{,}800\ \text{kWh/yr} \)

    Scale to the fleet by multiplying by the machine count.

  4. \( S = 28{,}800 \times \$0.12 = \$3{,}456/\text{yr} \)

    Energy × tariff = money. kWh × $/kWh leaves dollars.

  5. \( \text{CapEx} = 200 \times \$40 = \$8{,}000 \)

    The extra upfront cost of the whole fleet — the numerator of a payback calculation.

  6. \( \text{Payback} = \dfrac{\text{CapEx}}{\text{Annual saving}} = \dfrac{8{,}000}{3{,}456} \)

    Definition of simple payback period: upfront cost divided by annual saving. "Simple" means no discounting of future cash flows.

  7. \( \approx 2.3\ \text{years} \)

    Divide. Units: $ ÷ ($/yr) = yr, which confirms the answer is a time.

Answer

(a) 28,800 kWh/year · (b) $3,456/year · (c) ≈ 2.3 years payback.

Interpretation to add for marks: with a 4–5 year refresh cycle, a 2.3-year payback means the premium is recovered and roughly two further years of pure saving follow — so the Energy Star specification is financially justified on its own, before any environmental argument. Sanity check: the lecture states a 200-computer office saves $10,000–$30,000/year from Energy Star; this scenario's smaller figure reflects a lower assumed baseline consumption, so the order of magnitude is consistent.

Question 5 · hard · stretch

Laptop X has embodied carbon of 300 kg CO₂e and consumes 50 kWh/year. Laptop Y is more efficient at 30 kWh/year but has the same 300 kg embodied carbon. Grid carbon intensity is 0.6 kg CO₂e/kWh. Policy A: buy Y and replace every 2 years. Policy B: buy X and keep it 4 years. Compare total emissions over 8 years and state which policy wins and why.

Show worked solution

  1. \( T = 8\ \text{yr},\quad e_{\text{emb}} = 300\ \text{kg},\quad I = 0.6\ \text{kg/kWh} \)

    Fix the common horizon and list the shared constants. Both policies must be measured over the same 8 years or the comparison is meaningless.

  2. \( n_A = \dfrac{8}{2} = 4,\qquad n_B = \dfrac{8}{4} = 2 \)

    Horizon ÷ replacement interval gives how many devices each policy consumes. This is where the two policies actually differ.

  3. \( E^{\text{emb}}_A = 4 \times 300 = 1{,}200\ \text{kg} \)

    Device count × embodied carbon per device. Every replacement triggers a fresh manufacturing event, so embodied carbon is paid again each time.

  4. \( E^{\text{emb}}_B = 2 \times 300 = 600\ \text{kg} \)

    Same step for Policy B, which buys half as many machines.

  5. \( E^{\text{op}}_A = 30\ \tfrac{\text{kWh}}{\text{yr}} \times 8\ \text{yr} \times 0.6\ \tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{kWh}} = 144\ \text{kg} \)

    Annual consumption × years × carbon intensity. Units chain: kWh/yr × yr × kg/kWh = kg. A machine is running throughout the horizon regardless of how often it is swapped, so use the full 8 years.

  6. \( E^{\text{op}}_B = 50 \times 8 \times 0.6 = 240\ \text{kg} \)

    Same calculation for the less efficient Laptop X used under Policy B.

  7. \( E_A = 1{,}200 + 144 = 1{,}344\ \text{kg CO}_2\text{e} \)

    Total = embodied + operational. The two components are independent and simply add.

  8. \( E_B = 600 + 240 = 840\ \text{kg CO}_2\text{e} \)

    Same summation for Policy B.

  9. \( E_A - E_B = 504\ \text{kg},\quad \dfrac{504}{1344} = 37.5\% \)

    Difference, then difference as a share of the larger figure, to express the result as a percentage reduction.

Answer

Policy B wins: 840 kg against 1,344 kg — a 37.5% reduction, despite Policy B using the less energy-efficient laptop.

Why, stated for the marks: Policy A's efficiency advantage saves 96 kg of operational carbon over the eight years, but its shorter refresh cycle costs an extra 600 kg of embodied carbon. The embodied penalty is more than six times the operational gain. This is the numerical proof of the lecture's headline claim — longevity beats efficiency for embodied-carbon-dominant devices — and it works precisely because manufacturing is 70–80% of a laptop's lifetime footprint. Sanity check: operational carbon is small relative to embodied in both policies (144/1344 ≈ 11%, 240/840 ≈ 29%), which is consistent with the 20–30% use-phase share the lecture gives for consumer devices.

Practice B

Analytical questions

Question 6 · short answer

A vendor states their monitor is Energy Star certified and argues this makes it "the sustainable choice". Critique that argument.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from This joins Topic 02 (what Energy Star does and does not cover) to Topic 01 (embodied vs operational carbon). The examiner is testing whether you know the limitation of a certification, not just its name. The word "sustainable" doing the work in the vendor's claim is the thing to attack.

Model answer. The certification itself is genuine — Energy Star is a real US EPA programme and it means the product sits in the top 25% of energy efficiency for its category. So the factual claim is not greenwashing in the fibbing sense.

The argument, however, overreaches, because Energy Star covers operational energy efficiency only: electricity used in active and idle states. It says nothing about materials, manufacturing emissions, or end-of-life management. The lecture is explicit that a product can be Energy Star certified while being made with toxic materials, having no recycling programme, and carrying substantial manufacturing emissions.

That matters here specifically because of the embodied-carbon split (Topic 01): for a monitor — a consumer-class device — the dominant share of lifetime carbon is manufacturing, not operation. Energy Star addresses the smaller share of the total impact. The vendor is therefore committing something close to the hidden trade-off sin: one genuine green attribute is being used to imply overall environmental performance that has not been demonstrated.

What would substantiate the claim: an EPEAT Gold or TCO Certified rating, since both cover the full lifecycle including materials and end-of-life, plus RoHS compliance for hazardous substances and evidence of a take-back programme. The lecture's own guidance is that the four certifications address different dimensions and do not overlap significantly, so a complete claim requires more than one.

Question 7 · short answer

Compare EPEAT and TCO Certified. Both are described as lifecycle standards — what distinguishes them, and when would you specify each?

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from Topic 02. These two are the hardest pair to separate because both genuinely cover the full lifecycle. The examiner wants one specific discriminator — the social dimension — and then a sensible statement of when each is the right instrument.

Common ground. Both go well beyond Energy Star. Both cover materials selection, energy, product lifespan, and end-of-life management across the whole product lifecycle. Either is an acceptable baseline for a "full lifecycle sustainability" procurement requirement.

The distinguishing feature: social responsibility. TCO Certified uniquely includes criteria on working conditions in the manufacturing supply chain — minimum wage, working hours, prohibition of child and forced labour, health and safety — and requires supply-chain traceability. It further requires documented responsible sourcing of the 3TG conflict minerals (tin, tantalum, tungsten, gold) under OECD Due Diligence Guidance, going beyond US Dodd-Frank requirements. It also mandates annual third-party factory audits. EPEAT is environmental and corporate-performance focused; it does not carry an equivalent labour-conditions regime.

Structural difference. EPEAT is tiered — Bronze (all required criteria), Silver (Bronze + 50% of optional), Gold (Bronze + 75% of optional) — so it lets a buyer specify a level of ambition. TCO Certified is pass/fail against a single comprehensive standard.

When to specify which. Specify EPEAT Gold where the requirement is strong environmental lifecycle performance with a recognised tier to write into a tender — it is required by US federal agencies and referenced in EU Green Public Procurement, so vendor availability is broad. Specify TCO Certified where the organisation carries ESG reporting obligations or serves European institutional clients, or where supply-chain labour conditions are a stated concern — in the Bangladeshi context that applies particularly to firms exporting to EU/US markets and to World Bank or ADB-funded projects.

Question 8 · short answer

Analyse the claim: "Our new server range is built with eco-friendly materials and is carbon neutral by 2035." Identify the greenwashing sins present and state what evidence you would demand.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from Topic 04. This is a composite of the faculty's claim 2 and claim 4, so it tests whether you can apply the seven sins to a claim you have not seen rather than recite the five they analysed. Deal with the two halves separately — they fail for different reasons.

First half — "eco-friendly materials". This is the vagueness sin. "Eco-friendly" has no defined meaning and no verification behind it. Does it mean RoHS compliant? Recycled content? Sustainably sourced? Recyclable at end of life? Any company can print the phrase at zero cost. The lecture's rule applies directly: if a company cannot state exactly what "eco-friendly" means in numbers or certifications, assume it means nothing. There is also an element of the no proof sin, since no third-party standard is cited.

Second half — "carbon neutral by 2035". Insufficient without details, and potentially a hidden trade-off. A commitment a decade out, with no interim milestones and no independent accountability, is cheap to make and hard to hold anyone to.

Evidence I would demand. On materials: a specific RoHS compliance declaration with the 0.1%-by-weight (0.01% cadmium) thresholds met; a stated percentage of recycled content; and an EPEAT Gold or TCO Certified registration, which would substantiate the whole-lifecycle claim rather than a vague adjective. On the neutrality target, the lecture's four questions: does it include Scope 3 (manufacturing and supply chain — for hardware this is most of the footprint); what are the 2025 and 2030 interim targets; is it verified by the Science Based Targets initiative (SBTi); and how much of the work is offsetting rather than actual emission reduction?

What a good version would look like, using the faculty's model claim as the template: specific standard + specific metric + specific benefit. For example — "EPEAT Gold registered, RoHS compliant, 30% post-consumer recycled aluminium chassis, 80 PLUS Titanium PSU, SBTi-verified 42% absolute Scope 1–3 reduction by 2030." Every element there is checkable.

Question 9 · long answer

You are writing the IT hardware specification for a new 300-seat EWU computer lab. List and justify the sustainability requirements you would include in the tender document, and explain why the specification stage matters more than any later intervention.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from The faculty's own specification scenario from Section 6, combined with the procurement multiplier point from Section 7. It draws on Topic 02 (certifications), Topic 03 (LCA implications for procurement) and Topic 05 (the scorecard). The second half of the question — why specification matters most — is the examiner asking for the Velete Ch.8 quotation, so do not leave it out.

Core requirements. The lecture's own model answer gives four, and I would add three more from the scorecard dimensions:

  1. Energy Star certified (or demonstrated equivalent, top 25% efficiency for category). Rationale: this is the operational-energy dimension, and across 300 machines it is the difference between roughly $10,000 and $30,000 per year in electricity for an office of this size.
  2. EPEAT Silver or Gold registered. Rationale: Energy Star alone covers only operational energy; EPEAT adds materials selection, longevity and upgradability, end-of-life take-back and corporate performance — the dimensions that actually govern the 70–80% of lifetime carbon that is embodied.
  3. PSU must be 80 PLUS Gold or higher (92% at 50% load). Rationale: the lecture flags that locally assembled PCs frequently ship 70–75% efficient supplies, and PSU losses compound with PUE at roughly 1.5×. This is a low-cost clause with a fast payback.
  4. Minimum 5-year warranty with guaranteed spare parts availability. Rationale: longevity is the dominant environmental lever for embodied-carbon-dominant devices, and a spare parts guarantee is what makes a 4–5 year refresh cycle actually achievable rather than aspirational.
  5. RoHS compliance — hazardous substance thresholds of 0.1% by weight (0.01% cadmium). Rationale: reduces disposal liability and makes end-of-life processing cheaper and safer, which matters given how few DoE-certified recyclers operate in Bangladesh.
  6. A vendor take-back programme at end of life, contractually committed. Rationale: this pushes the EPR obligation onto the manufacturer where it belongs, and gives the university a documented disposal route for Scope 3 reporting.
  7. Recycled content ≥10% in the chassis, as a scorecard dimension with evidence required rather than a claim accepted.

How I would evaluate bids. Score each on the six scorecard dimensions — energy certification, lifecycle certification, hazardous substance compliance, repairability, recycled content, take-back — at 1–3 stars on evidence, not marketing claims, for a maximum of 18. Reject anything below 9. And evaluate on total cost of ownership over 4–5 years including energy, not on sticker price, since as the TCO arithmetic shows a higher-specification machine with a longer life is routinely cheaper in real terms.

Why the specification stage matters most. This is the procurement multiplier from Velete Ch.8. A single procurement officer signing one tender determines the environmental performance of thousands of devices over four to five years, because the specification multiplies across the entire fleet. Writing "EPEAT Gold required" into a 500-laptop tender has more environmental impact than any individual action any of those 500 users will ever take — including recycling them at end of life. Every other lever in this course acts on a device after its impact is largely locked in; the specification acts before. For a 300-seat lab the same logic holds: one paragraph in a tender document outweighs three years of switching monitors off at night.

Question 10 · long answer

A local PC assembler offers laptops with no certifications at 30% below market price for a new university lab. The finance office is enthusiastic. Advise the IT department.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from The faculty's Bangladesh scenario from Section 6. It tests whether you can argue the TCO case (Topic 03, Topic 05) against a sticker-price case, using the certifications (Topic 02) as the evidence framework. Note the phrasing — you are asked to advise, so a flat refusal is weaker than a conditional one.

Recommendation: do not accept the offer as presented — but the right response is to demand documentation rather than to reject the vendor outright.

Why the 30% saving is likely illusory. Work through what each missing certification implies:

  • No Energy Star — the machines are not demonstrably in the top 25% of efficiency, so electricity costs over a 4–5 year life will be higher. The lecture puts Energy Star savings at 30–65% of consumption; across a lab-sized fleet that offsets a substantial part of the upfront discount.
  • No 80 PLUS rating — and the lecture specifically warns that locally assembled PCs frequently use budget PSUs at 70–75% efficiency, against 92% for Gold. That waste is continuous, and it compounds at roughly 1.5× once cooling is included.
  • No RoHS declaration — unrestricted toxic materials create a disposal liability at end of life, which is a real cost in Bangladesh where certified recycling capacity is scarce.
  • No warranty or spare parts guarantee — a higher failure and replacement rate. Since 70–80% of lifetime carbon is embodied, a shorter effective lifespan is both the largest environmental cost and, per the TCO arithmetic, the largest financial one.
  • No EPEAT or TCO Certified — no accountability for the supply chain, and nothing the university can cite in its own sustainability reporting.

The financial argument in the finance office's own language. A machine bought at 30% less but lasting 3 years instead of 5 costs more per year of service: on a $400 versus $520 comparison, the cheap option works out at roughly $667 over five years against $370 for the certified one once lifespan and energy are included. The discount is not a saving; it is a deferred cost. This is exactly the TCO-beats-sticker-price point from the scorecard section.

What I would actually do. Rather than rejecting the vendor, issue the sustainability requirements as tender conditions and invite them to meet them: request RoHS compliance documentation, PSU efficiency data, warranty and spare-parts terms, and per-product energy consumption figures. If the assembler can supply that evidence, the low price may be genuine and a local supplier has advantages in support and lead time. If they cannot, the missing documentation is the answer. The principle is the one from the greenwashing section — demand specific, measurable, verifiable evidence, and treat an absence of evidence as an absence of the attribute.

Question 11 · long answer · stretch

"Sustainability is a hardware problem, so software engineers have no role in it." Evaluate this statement with reference to Class 8, and explain how the four engineering roles differ in the levers available to them.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from Topic 05, the engineer's-role section. The statement is a deliberate provocation — the lecture explicitly contradicts it. The examiner wants you to refute it using the specific mechanism the faculty gave, then demonstrate you know all four roles rather than just the one being challenged.

The statement is false, and Class 8 refutes it directly. The faculty's position is that sustainability is a software quality metric, not just a hardware one, and the mechanism is concrete: efficient code means less hardware is needed, which means fewer devices are manufactured, which means less manufacturing carbon. The worked example given was that a software team cutting CPU usage by 30% may defer a hardware upgrade cycle by two years across a fleet — avoiding the embodied carbon of an entire replacement round.

That mechanism matters precisely because of the embodied-carbon finding from Topic 01. If 70–80% of a device's lifetime footprint is locked in at manufacture, then anything that avoids a manufacturing event is worth far more than anything that trims operational draw. Software that lets existing hardware stay adequate for longer does exactly that. Software also drives the reverse effect: when vendors stop shipping OS updates, functional hardware becomes insecure and gets discarded — software obsolescence is a hardware waste driver, which is why the EU now mandates 5–10 year support windows.

The four roles and their distinct levers. The lecture's framing is that each role controls different levers, not that some roles matter and others don't:

  • Product engineers hold the largest single lever, because material choice, design for disassembly, user-replaceable batteries, minimising distinct material types and spare parts commitments all happen at design stage — and those decisions lock in most of the product's lifetime impact before it exists.
  • Systems / infrastructure engineers specify certifications in requirements, maximise virtualisation before adding hardware, choose high-utilisation servers, design for long refresh cycles with upgrade paths, and document asset lifecycles for Scope 3.
  • Procurement officers / IT managers hold the procurement multiplier — one tender determines the performance of thousands of devices for years. They evaluate on TCO over 4–5 years rather than price, and maintain asset registers that trigger certified disposal rather than casual binning.
  • Software engineers extend the useful life of hardware already in service, which is the cheapest possible way to avoid embodied carbon since no procurement decision is required at all.

Conclusion. The statement mistakes where the carbon is emitted for who influences it. The carbon is emitted in hardware manufacturing, but the decisions that determine how much hardware gets manufactured are distributed across all four roles — and the software role is the only one that can act on devices already deployed.

Last-minute revision — Class 8

The headline claim

70–80% of a consumer device's lifetime carbon = manufacturing, before first use. Servers: only 20–40%.

Embodied vs operational

Embodied = manufacture + transport + disposal, locked in before use. Operational = electricity during use.

Energy Star

US EPA. Top 25% efficiency in category. Saves 30–65%. Covers OPERATIONAL ENERGY ONLY — that limitation is examinable.

EPEAT tiers

Bronze = all required. Silver = Bronze + 50% optional. Gold = Bronze + 75% optional. Full lifecycle.

TCO Certified

Most comprehensive. UNIQUE feature = SOCIAL criteria (labour conditions). 3TG conflict minerals, OECD guidance, annual factory audits.

80 PLUS at 50% load

Basic 80 · Bronze 85 · Silver 88 · Gold 92 · Platinum 94 · Titanium 96. Gold = data centre minimum.

PSU compounding

1 W wasted in the PSU ≈ 1.5 W total facility cost, because cooling must remove the heat too.

RoHS numbers

6 substances (2003) → 10 in RoHS 3 (2019). Limits: 0.1% by weight; 0.01% for cadmium.

RoHS substances

Pb, Hg, Cd, Cr VI, PBB, PBDE + 4 phthalates (RoHS 3).

The LCA insight

Keeping a device 2× longer usually beats buying the most efficient model and replacing on schedule. Longevity > efficiency.
