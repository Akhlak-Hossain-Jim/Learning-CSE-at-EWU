The 2–3 day plan

Six topics, one exam, limited time. Here is where to put the hours.

This is the combining guide. It does not re-teach the six topics — the individual guides do that. What it does is give you the master formula sheet, the cross-topic threads the examiner is likely to test, the traps collected in one place, a mixed mock exam, and a schedule for the time you have left.

The single thread running through all six classes

Most of a device's environmental cost is locked in before it is switched on and determined by decisions made upstream of the person using it. Class 8 says it about manufacturing (70–80% embodied carbon), Class 7 says it about design for disassembly, Class 11 says it about site and structure, Class 12 says it about idle power you can only remove by switching machines off, Class 13 says it about governance that outlives the project, and Class 16 says it about a sensor's duty cycle. If a long-answer question asks you to connect topics, this is the connection.

Day 1 — the numerical spine

The exam is maths, analytical and scenario. Maths is the most reliably scorable, so secure it first. Work through Practice A in guides 3 (Data Center), 4 (Virtualization), 5 (Transformation) and 6 (Green IoT), in that order — they build on each other. Do them on paper with the solutions hidden. Then come back to this guide's master formula sheet and check you can write every formula from memory without looking.

Day 2 — definitions, distinctions and scenarios

Read the concept sections of guides 1 (E-Waste) and 2 (Sustainable Hardware) — these are the two most definition-heavy topics and the least numerical. Then work the cross-topic comparison tables below, which cover the distinctions that get confused: Tier II vs III, Type 1 vs Type 2, EPEAT vs TCO, RoHS vs WEEE vs Basel, LoRaWAN vs NB-IoT, payback vs ROI, greening of vs by. Finish by attempting three Practice C scenarios from different guides, writing full answers by hand.

Day 3 (or the final evening) — mock and cheat sheets

Sit the mixed mock exam in this guide under timed conditions. Then read every topic's cheat sheet block — the dark panels at the end of each guide — and the trap list below. Do not learn anything new on the last evening; consolidate what is already there.

If you only have one evening

Master formula sheet → trap list → the six cheat-sheet panels → mock exam section A. In that order. The formulas are the highest return per minute because they are unambiguous and they recur across four of the six topics.

Section 01

Master formula sheet

Everything numerical across all six classes, in one place, with the constants.

Constants you must know cold

Memorise these four

8,760 — hours in a year (365 × 24). Used in every "continuous" or "24/7" problem.
525,600 — minutes in a year. Used for downtime conversions.
0.6 kg CO₂e/kWh — Bangladesh grid carbon intensity (the faculty's stated figure).
1,000 — the conversion that keeps appearing: W↔kW, kg↔tonnes, µA↔mA, m↔km.

Data centre efficiency (Class 11)

PUE, DCiE, downtime, capacity
\[ \text{PUE} = \frac{\text{Total facility power}}{\text{IT equipment power}} \qquad (\text{always} \ge 1) \] \[ \text{DCiE} = \frac{1}{\text{PUE}} \times 100\% \] \[ \text{Downtime} = (1 - \text{uptime fraction}) \times \text{period} \] \[ \text{Racks} = \frac{\text{Hall area}}{\text{Footprint per rack}} \qquad \text{IT capacity} = \text{Racks} \times \text{kW per rack} \] \[ P_{\text{facility}} = \text{PUE} \times P_{\text{IT}} \]

Virtualisation and power (Class 12)

Power curve, consolidation, energy
\[ P = P_{\text{idle}} + \text{Load}\% \times \left( P_{\max} - P_{\text{idle}} \right) \] \[ \text{Hosts} = \frac{\text{Total VMs}}{\text{Consolidation ratio}} \quad (\text{round up}) \qquad \text{Decommissioned} = \text{Original} - \text{Hosts} \] \[ \Delta P_{\text{facility}} = \Delta P_{\text{IT}} \times \text{PUE} \] \[ E_{\text{kWh}} = P_{\text{kW}} \times t_{\text{hours}} \]

Business case (Class 13)

The faculty's own four-formula quick reference
\[ \text{① Annual \$} = \text{kWh saved} \times \text{Tariff} \] \[ \text{② Simple payback (yrs)} = \frac{\text{CapEx}}{\text{Annual \$ saved}} \] \[ \text{③ Tonnes CO}_2\text{e} = \frac{\text{kWh} \times \text{Emission factor}}{1{,}000} \] \[ \text{④ ROI \%} = \frac{\text{Total savings} - \text{CapEx}}{\text{CapEx}} \times 100\% \]

IoT power budgets (Class 16)

Duty cycle, average current, battery life, coverage
\[ \text{Duty cycle } d = \frac{t_{\text{active}}}{T_{\text{period}}} \times 100\% \] \[ I_{\text{avg}} = d \cdot I_{\text{active}} + (1-d) \cdot I_{\text{sleep}} \] \[ \text{Battery life (h)} = \frac{C\ (\text{mAh})}{I\ (\text{mA})} \qquad \text{then} \div 8{,}760 \text{ for years} \] \[ A_{\text{coverage}} = \pi r^2 \qquad E_{\text{harvested}} = P \times t \qquad E_{\text{cell}} = C_{\text{mAh}} \times V \]

Hardware TCO (Class 8)

Lifespan-normalised comparison and PSU efficiency
\[ \text{TCO over } T = \text{Price} \times \frac{T}{\text{Lifespan}} - (\text{Annual saving} \times T) \] \[ \eta_{\text{PSU}} = \frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{in}}} \qquad P_{\text{waste}} = P_{\text{in}} - P_{\text{out}} \]

And the compounding rule: 1 W wasted in the PSU ≈ 1.5 W total facility cost, once cooling is included.

The four unit errors that cost the most marks

1 · µA vs mA (Class 16) — a factor of 1,000. Convert before dividing into mAh.
2 · Seconds vs minutes in duty cycle — convert the period to seconds first.
3 · Forgetting × 8,760 when converting continuous power to annual energy.
4 · mAh is a charge, not an energy — multiply by voltage before comparing with mWh.

Section 02

The distinctions the examiner will test

Seven pairs that get confused. Each row is a likely short-answer question.

PairThe discriminator — the one thing that separates them
Tier II vs Tier III
Class 11		 Both are N+1. The difference is the distribution path, not the components. Tier III is concurrently maintainable — every path can be taken down for planned maintenance with zero impact. Tier II has one path, so servicing it drops the load. (22.0 hrs/yr → 1.6 hrs/yr.)
Tier III vs Tier IV
Class 11		 III protects against planned maintenance. IV protects against unplanned single failure anywhere — which needs 2N, two fully independent paths, not just a spare unit.
Type 1 vs Type 2 hypervisor
Class 12		 Type 1 runs on bare metal (1–3% overhead); Type 2 runs on a host OS (10–20%). The green-specific reason: Type 2 cannot control CPU C-states/P-states, so cores can't reach their efficient states.
EPEAT vs TCO Certified
Class 8		 Both are full-lifecycle. Only TCO includes social responsibility — labour conditions, child/forced labour prohibition, supply-chain traceability, 3TG conflict minerals, annual factory audits. EPEAT is tiered (Bronze / Silver +50% / Gold +75%); TCO is pass/fail.
RoHS vs WEEE vs Basel
Classes 7 & 8		 RoHS = what goes IN (substances, 0.1% by weight / 0.01% Cd). WEEE = who PAYS at end of life (EPR; 65% collection target). Basel = where it may TRAVEL (Ban Amendment 2019).
LoRaWAN vs NB-IoT
Class 16		 Both long-range and low-power. LoRaWAN = unlicensed, operator-independent, you run gateways, longest range (15+ km rural), lowest power. NB-IoT = licensed cellular, uses existing towers, no gateways to maintain, higher data rate, carrier subscription.
Payback vs ROI
Class 13		 Payback = how long until break-even, in years — a risk metric. ROI = how much gained over a stated period, as a percentage — a magnitude metric. Neither discounts future cash flows.
Greening OF vs BY IoT
Class 16		 OF = making the devices/networks themselves efficient. BY = using sensing to cut energy elsewhere. The paradox: IoT is simultaneously a sustainability tool and a potential energy/e-waste source.
Embodied vs operational carbon
Class 8		 Embodied = manufacture + transport + disposal, locked in before use. Operational = electricity during use. Consumer devices 70–80% embodied; 24/7 servers only 20–40%.
Section 03

Cross-topic threads

Where the six classes connect. Long-answer questions live here.

Thread 1 — The PUE multiplier chain (Classes 11 → 12 → 13)

This is the most important cross-topic chain in the course, and it determines where investment should go. A watt eliminated at the IT layer is multiplied by the PUE into a facility saving; that facility saving becomes kWh via 8,760; those kWh become dollars via the tariff and tonnes via the emission factor; and those figures become the payback and ROI that get the project approved.

The full chain, end to end
\[ \Delta P_{\text{IT}} \;\xrightarrow{\times\,\text{PUE}}\; \Delta P_{\text{facility}} \;\xrightarrow{\times\,8760}\; \Delta E_{\text{kWh}} \;\xrightarrow{\times\,\text{tariff}}\; \$ \;\xrightarrow{\div\,\text{CapEx}}\; \text{Payback} \] \[ \Delta E_{\text{kWh}} \;\xrightarrow{\times\, 0.6 \div 1000}\; \text{tonnes CO}_2\text{e} \;\rightarrow\; \text{Scope 2 report} \]

The strategic conclusion: facility improvements (better PUE) reduce only the multiplier and face diminishing returns. IT improvements (consolidation) reduce the base and get the multiplier applied on top. Reduce the IT load first, then size the facility around it — the reverse order over-sizes the plant and wastes part of the PUE gain to partial-load inefficiency.

Thread 2 — Embodied carbon governs everything upstream (Classes 8 → 7 → 16)

Because 70–80% of a consumer device's lifetime carbon is manufacturing, three separate conclusions in three different classes all follow from the same fact: longevity beats efficiency (Class 8), recycling is the last resort behind reuse and repair (Class 7), and a 2–5 year IoT replacement cycle is a major e-waste driver (Class 16). If you can state the embodied-carbon fact and then derive all three, you have the spine of a strong essay answer.

Thread 3 — The GHG scopes as an organising frame (Class 13 across all)

  • Scope 1 — diesel generators, which in Bangladesh are near-mandatory because of grid unreliability (Class 11). Note the tension: the same N+1 redundancy that is carbon-neutral on a reliable grid is carbon-positive here.
  • Scope 2 — purchased electricity. This is what PUE improvement (Class 11), consolidation (Class 12) and PC power management (Class 13) all reduce, and what Bangladesh Bank requires commercial banks to report.
  • Scope 3 — embodied carbon of procured hardware (Class 8) and e-waste at end of life (Class 7). Usually the largest for an IT organisation, and usually the least reported — which is exactly why the Class 8 greenwashing test asks whether a neutrality pledge includes Scope 3.

Thread 4 — Technical gains need governance to survive (Class 13 across all)

Every technical intervention in this course decays without an organisational mechanism. Virtualisation ratios degrade into virtual sprawl; procurement standards get waived under deadline pressure; sleep policies accumulate exemptions; e-waste routes revert to casual binning. Phase 4 (Institutionalize) and Kotter step 8 (anchor in culture) exist to prevent exactly this. The named erosion mechanisms — staff turn over, legacy hardware creeps back, ratios degrade — are worth quoting.

Thread 5 — Bangladesh reorders the priorities

The faculty's consistent local point across five of six classes is that the criteria are universal but their ranking is local:

  • Class 7 — the informal sector should be formalised, not eliminated; ~100,000 livelihoods.
  • Class 8 — locally assembled PCs often ship 70–75% efficient PSUs; specifying 80 PLUS Gold is high-impact and low-cost.
  • Class 11 — flood elevation is the deciding site constraint, above cost and cooling. N+1 gensets near-mandatory.
  • Class 12 — consolidation's headline local benefit is UPS runtime during load-shedding, not the bill.
  • Class 13 — Bangladesh Bank green guidelines and SREDA make Green IT a compliance obligation for banks.
  • Class 16 — off-grid operation is the default design constraint, not an edge case.
Section 04

Every trap, in one list

The mistakes that cost marks, collected from all six guides.

Conceptual traps

1. "Send it to a certified recycler immediately" is the wrong first answer for an end-of-life device — assess Reuse, Repair, Refurbish first.
2. "Energy Star means sustainable" — it covers operational energy only, which for a consumer device is the smaller share of impact.
3. Adding more UPS units and chillers does not get you from Tier II to Tier III — you need redundant distribution paths (dual-corded PDUs, isolatable cooling loops).
4. Running servers "more gently" saves almost nothing — an idle server draws 50–70% of peak. You must switch machines off.
5. A good PUE does not mean an efficient data centre — PUE cannot see utilisation.
6. Optimising CPU code to save IoT battery is usually the wrong target — the radio dominates.
7. Energy harvesting is not a substitute for duty cycling; it complements a power budget that is already minimised.
8. RoHS ≠ EPR. Substances rule vs financial-responsibility rule.
9. Proposing to ban informal e-waste recycling in Bangladesh misses the lecture's position — formalise it.
10. Stopping a transformation after the easy wins doesn't plateau, it regresses.

Arithmetic traps

11. N+1 with N=3 is 4 units, not 6. 2N is 6 (two paths of 3), and 2N+1 is 8 (two paths of N+1), not 7.
12. Don't round 99.982% to 99.98% — it changes the downtime answer by over 10%.
13. In a TCO comparison, normalise both options to a common horizon before comparing prices.
14. After consolidation the surviving hosts draw more per machine — the saving comes from having fewer of them.
15. µA → mA is ÷1,000 before dividing into mAh. Getting this wrong turns 22 years into 8 days.
16. Convert the duty-cycle period to seconds before dividing.
17. mAh is a charge. Multiply by voltage to compare with mWh.
18. Gateway coverage uses radius, not diameter, and the gateway count rounds up.
19. Include every capital component in a payback (hardware + infrastructure), not just the obvious one.
20. When a policy changes operating hours as well as power, both factors apply — don't use 8,760 for a fleet that now runs 10 hours a day.

Mixed mock exam

Section A: numerical. Section B: analytical. Section C: scenario. Attempt under timed conditions — roughly 90 minutes.

Section A — Numerical (attempt all)

A1 · Class 11 · 8 marks

A facility has an IT load of 750 kW and a total facility draw of 1,125 kW. (a) Calculate PUE and DCiE. (b) The hall is 600 m² with racks at 2.5 m² each (aisles included) — how many racks, and at 5 kW/rack what is the IT capacity? (c) Does the existing 750 kW load fit within that capacity? (d) The facility is Tier III — how many minutes of downtime per year does that allow?

Show worked solution

  1. \( \text{PUE} = \dfrac{1125}{750} = 1.5 \)

    Definition of PUE: total facility ÷ IT power. Result > 1 as required.

  2. \( \text{DCiE} = \dfrac{1}{1.5}\times 100\% = 66.7\% \)

    DCiE is the reciprocal of PUE as a percentage. Cross-check: 750/1125 = 0.667, same value.

  3. \( n = \dfrac{600}{2.5} = 240\ \text{racks} \)

    Area ÷ footprint per rack. The footprint already includes aisle space, so no further deduction.

  4. \( P_{\text{cap}} = 240 \times 5 = 1{,}200\ \text{kW} \)

    Rack count × density per rack gives design IT capacity.

  5. \( 750 < 1{,}200 \Rightarrow \text{yes, with } 450\ \text{kW headroom} \)

    Compare actual load with design capacity. The margin is the answer to (c).

  6. \( D = (1-0.99982)\times 525{,}600 = 94.6\ \text{min} \)

    Downtime = (1 − uptime) × period, using 525,600 minutes per year and the full Tier III figure of 99.982%.

Answer

(a) PUE 1.5, DCiE 66.7% — industry average. (b) 240 racks, 1,200 kW capacity. (c) Yes, running at 62.5% of design capacity with 450 kW headroom. (d) ≈ 94.6 minutes (1.6 hrs).

Analytical addition worth marks: running at 62.5% of design capacity means the power and cooling plant is operating at partial load, which is less efficient than at design load — a plausible contributor to the mediocre 1.5 PUE, and the argument for modular phased installation rather than building full capacity on day one.

A2 · Classes 12 + 13 · 12 marks

An organisation runs 90 legacy servers at 160 W each. It virtualises at 9:1 onto hosts with \(P_{\max}\) = 420 W, \(P_{\text{idle}}\) = 250 W, running at 75% utilisation. PUE is 1.5, electricity $0.15/kWh, grid factor 0.6 kg CO₂e/kWh, project CapEx $78,000.

(a) IT power before and after. (b) Facility power saving. (c) Annual kWh and dollars. (d) Simple payback. (e) 5-year ROI. (f) Annual tonnes CO₂e avoided.

Show worked solution

  1. \( P_{\text{before}} = 90 \times 160 = 14{,}400\ \text{W} = 14.4\ \text{kW} \)

    Count × per-unit power, converted to kW immediately so later energy steps stay consistent.

  2. \( H = \dfrac{90}{9} = 10\ \text{hosts} \)

    Workloads ÷ consolidation ratio. Divides cleanly, so no rounding decision.

  3. \( P_{\text{host}} = 250 + 0.75\,(420-250) = 250 + 127.5 = 377.5\ \text{W} \)

    Linear interpolation formula. Dynamic range is 170 W; 75% of it is 127.5 W above the idle baseline.

  4. \( P_{\text{after}} = 10 \times 377.5 = 3{,}775\ \text{W} = 3.775\ \text{kW} \)

    Per-host draw × host count gives the new IT load.

  5. \( \Delta P_{\text{IT}} = 14.4 - 3.775 = 10.625\ \text{kW} \)

    Difference at the IT layer.

  6. \( \Delta P_{\text{fac}} = 10.625 \times 1.5 = 15.94\ \text{kW} \)

    Apply the PUE multiplier — each IT watt removed also removes its cooling and distribution overhead.

  7. \( E = 15.94 \times 8{,}760 = 139{,}613\ \text{kWh/yr} \)

    Energy = power × time over a continuous year.

  8. \( S = 139{,}613 \times 0.15 = \$20{,}942/\text{yr} \)

    Formula ①: kWh × tariff.

  9. \( \text{Payback} = \dfrac{78{,}000}{20{,}942} \approx 3.72\ \text{yrs} \)

    Formula ②: CapEx ÷ annual saving.

  10. \( \text{ROI} = \dfrac{20{,}942\times 5 - 78{,}000}{78{,}000}\times 100\% \approx +34.2\% \)

    Formula ④: total 5-year savings minus CapEx, over CapEx.

  11. \( m = \dfrac{139{,}613 \times 0.6}{1{,}000} = 83.8\ \text{t} \)

    Formula ③: energy × emission factor ÷ 1,000 for metric tonnes.

Answer

(a) 14.4 kW → 3.775 kW · (b) ≈ 15.94 kW · (c) ≈ 139,613 kWh ≈ $20,942/yr · (d) ≈ 3.7 years · (e) ≈ +34% over 5 years · (f) ≈ 83.8 tonnes CO₂e/yr.

Interpretation for the analytical marks: a 3.7-year payback falls in the faculty's acceptable but not excellent band (excellent is under 2, poor is over 4), and a +34% five-year ROI is modest. The reason is that this is a hardware project — CapEx buys equipment. Software-driven projects like DPM or PC power management pay back far faster. A stronger case would add the deferred hardware replacement, floor space, switch ports and maintenance for the 80 decommissioned machines, none of which appear in the electricity figure.

A3 · Class 16 · 8 marks

A flood sensor wakes for 3 seconds every 20 minutes, drawing 25 mA active and 4 µA in deep sleep, on a 2000 mAh battery. (a) Duty cycle. (b) Average current. (c) Battery life in years. (d) A solar panel yields 40 mW over 5 usable hours daily — compare the daily harvest with the node's daily consumption at 3.6 V and state whether the panel can sustain it.

Show worked solution

  1. \( T = 20 \times 60 = 1{,}200\ \text{s} \)

    Convert the period to seconds so it matches the active time's unit.

  2. \( d = \dfrac{3}{1200} = 0.0025 = 0.25\% \)

    Duty cycle = active ÷ period. Under 1%, satisfying the 1% rule.

  3. \( I_{\text{sleep}} = 4\ \mu\text{A} = 0.004\ \text{mA} \)

    Convert µA to mA (÷1,000) to match the mAh capacity unit.

  4. \( I_{\text{avg}} = 0.0025(25) + 0.9975(0.004) \)

    Time-weighted average current — the fraction of time in each state multiplied by that state's draw.

  5. \( = 0.0625 + 0.00399 = 0.0665\ \text{mA} \)

    Evaluate both terms separately — keeping them apart shows active dominates at 94%.

  6. \( t = \dfrac{2000}{0.0665} = 30{,}075\ \text{h} \)

    Capacity ÷ average current. Units: mAh ÷ mA = h.

  7. \( t = \dfrac{30{,}075}{8{,}760} \approx 3.43\ \text{years} \)

    Convert hours to years (÷8,760).

  8. \( E_{\text{harvest}} = 40 \times 5 = 200\ \text{mWh/day} \)

    Energy = power × time for the harvest.

  9. \( E_{\text{use}} = 0.0665\ \text{mA} \times 24\ \text{h} \times 3.6\ \text{V} \)

    Convert current draw to energy: average current × hours gives mAh/day, then × voltage gives mWh/day — the same unit as the harvest.

  10. \( = 1.596\ \text{mAh/day} \times 3.6 = 5.75\ \text{mWh/day} \)

    Evaluate. Now both figures are in mWh/day and can be compared directly.

  11. \( \dfrac{200}{5.75} \approx 35\times \)

    Ratio of harvest to consumption answers whether the panel sustains the node, and by what margin.

Answer

(a) 0.25% · (b) ≈ 0.0665 mA · (c) ≈ 3.4 years on battery alone · (d) harvest 200 mWh/day versus consumption ≈ 5.75 mWh/day — the panel supplies roughly 35× what the node needs, so it comfortably sustains indefinite operation.

The design conclusion, which is where the analytical marks are: 3.4 years on battery alone fails the 5+ year unattended requirement for a flood sensor, so the panel is not optional. With a 35× margin, the design goal shifts from generation to storage — the supercapacitor or rechargeable buffer must be sized to carry the node through an extended monsoon overcast period, which is the binding constraint in Bangladesh. Note also that active current is 94% of the budget here, so if further savings were needed, the lever is shortening the wake/transmit window, not a lower-sleep-current MCU.

A4 · Classes 7 + 8 · 6 marks

A university retires 250 laptops, each with 320 kg CO₂e embodied carbon. Policy X: recycle all. Policy Y: 30% reused, 35% repaired and kept, 35% recycled — each retained unit displacing one new purchase at $500. (a) Embodied CO₂e avoided under Y. (b) Procurement cost avoided. (c) Which stage of the circular hierarchy does the recycled 35% represent, and why is it last?

Show worked solution

  1. \( f_{\text{kept}} = 30\% + 35\% = 65\% \)

    Add the retained categories. Both Reuse and Repair keep a device in service and therefore displace a new build; the recycled share does not.

  2. \( N_{\text{kept}} = 0.65 \times 250 = 162.5 \approx 162\ \text{units} \)

    Share × total. Round down to a whole device — you cannot retain half a laptop, and rounding down is the conservative choice.

  3. \( E_{\text{avoided}} = 162 \times 320 = 51{,}840\ \text{kg} \)

    Retained count × embodied carbon per device, since each retained unit avoids one manufacturing event.

  4. \( = 51.84\ \text{t CO}_2\text{e} \)

    Convert kg to tonnes (÷1,000) for reporting.

  5. \( C = 162 \times \$500 = \$81{,}000 \)

    Retained count × unit price gives the avoided procurement spend.

Answer

(a) ≈ 51.8 tonnes CO₂e avoided · (b) $81,000 procurement avoided.

(c) The recycled 35% represents Recycle, the last resort in the circular hierarchy (Reuse → Repair → Refurbish → Remanufacture → Recycle). It is last because recycling recovers materials only — the design, fabrication, assembly and functional value are all discarded. Since 70–80% of a laptop's lifetime carbon is embodied in manufacturing, every earlier stage in the hierarchy retains far more value. Recycling is not worthless — it recovers the urban mine — but it is the failure state, applied only to what cannot be reused, repaired or refurbished.

Note for full marks: the avoided emissions are Scope 3 (embodied carbon of purchased equipment), and the disposal route for the recycled 35% must be documented as part of the university's Scope 3 e-waste reporting, with certified chain of custody.

Section B — Analytical (answer any three)

B1 · short answer

"A facility with a PUE of 1.15 is a green data centre." Evaluate this statement.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from Class 11 (PUE) against Class 12 (utilisation) — the bridge the faculty built deliberately. The examiner is testing whether you know what PUE structurally cannot measure.

The claim is incomplete, and relying on it leads to the wrong investment decision.

A PUE of 1.15 is genuinely excellent — DCiE of 87%, well above the industry-average 1.5 (66.7%). Only 13% of incoming electricity is lost to cooling, power conversion and lighting. The facility layer is efficient.

But PUE is a ratio of total facility power to IT power, and it says nothing about whether the IT power does useful work. The faculty's own framing: "even the greenest Tier IV data centre with a 1.15 PUE is inherently wasteful if its underlying servers sit idle at 10% utilisation." At legacy utilisation of 10–15%, an idle server still draws 50–70% of peak power, so almost all of that efficiently delivered electricity powers idle silicon.

Worse, the metric can be gamed in the wrong direction: if the servers drew more power for the same work, the IT figure would rise and PUE would fall. A metric that improves when the base gets less efficient cannot be a complete measure of efficiency.

What a complete assessment needs: PUE alongside a utilisation or work-delivered metric, plus the facility's carbon intensity (a PUE 1.15 facility on coal may emit more than a PUE 1.5 facility on renewables) and its embodied footprint. And the correct sequencing is reduce the IT load first, then optimise the facility — since every watt removed at the IT layer is multiplied by the PUE, whereas facility improvements only shrink the multiplier and face diminishing returns.

B2 · short answer

Explain why Extended Producer Responsibility is described as changing engineering rather than merely reallocating cost.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from Class 7, Topic 03. The phrasing "rather than merely reallocating cost" tells you the examiner wants the feedback loop, not the definition. Define briefly, then spend the answer on the mechanism.

Definition first, in one sentence. EPR makes the manufacturer financially and operationally responsible for the product across its whole lifecycle, including collection and recycling at end of life, shifting that burden from governments and consumers back to the company that created the product.

Why it is more than reallocation. Once the manufacturer pays the recycling bill, end-of-life cost appears on its own balance sheet — and a cost you bear is a cost you have an incentive to design out. That produces four specific engineering changes, all named in the lecture: reducing material complexity (fewer material types means cheaper sorting), eliminating toxic substances (less costly hazardous-waste handling), designing for disassembly (lower labour cost to process), and extending product life (fewer units entering the waste stream at all).

The mechanism is therefore a market feedback loop running from end-of-life economics back into design decisions. Without EPR that loop is broken: the designer's choices determine the disposal cost, but someone else pays it, so the signal never reaches the decision-maker.

The evidence the lecture cites is that WEEE (implementing EPR) plus RoHS (restricting substances) together transformed European electronics design — neither alone would have done it. And the consequence for the individual engineer is the framing worth quoting: under EPR, specifying a toxic material or a glued-shut enclosure creates a future financial liability for the employer, not merely environmental harm. The concrete illustration is the glued battery — it forces a device from Repair straight to Recycle, discarding ~70 kg CO₂e of embodied carbon to avoid replacing a $20 part, and raises the recycler's manual pre-processing cost, which under EPR the manufacturer eventually pays.

B3 · short answer

An engineer must select a wireless protocol for 400 water-quality sensors distributed along 30 km of river in rural Bangladesh, reporting a few bytes every 30 minutes. Recommend a protocol and justify the rejection of the alternatives.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from Class 16, Topic 03 applied to a case not in the lecture. The examiner wants the selection logic, and explicit rejection of the alternatives — that is what distinguishes understanding from table-memorisation.

Recommendation: LoRaWAN.

Why it fits. The deployment is kilometre-scale and rural, which is exactly LoRaWAN's design point — 15+ km rural line-of-sight range. At a 15 km radius one gateway covers roughly 700 km², so a 30 km river stretch can be served by one or two gateways. The payload is a few bytes every 30 minutes, so the modest 0.3–50 kbps data rate is irrelevant, and such an extreme low duty cycle plays directly to LoRaWAN's very low power profile and multi-year battery life. Because it runs on unlicensed spectrum it is operator-independent — no carrier subscription for 400 devices, and no dependence on cellular coverage reaching every riverside point.

Rejecting Zigbee. Range is only 10–100 m per hop. Covering 30 km would require an enormous chain of relay hops, and the lecture notes that router nodes draw more power than end devices — so a mesh spanning a river would need hundreds of mains-powered or frequently-serviced relays. Zigbee is designed for dense indoor deployments, not linear rural ones.

Rejecting BLE. Range of 10–50 m makes it a personal-area technology. Its strength — up to 2 Mbps and smartphone compatibility — is worthless here, since the payload is a few bytes and there is no phone in range. It would require a collector every few tens of metres.

Rejecting NB-IoT — the genuine contender. Its range of 1–10 km on existing towers is adequate, and the lecture notes that Bangladesh's dense mobile coverage makes NB-IoT attractive precisely because there are no gateways to maintain. It is a defensible alternative and should be named as such. It loses here on three counts: coverage along a rural river cannot be assumed continuous; it carries a per-device carrier subscription across 400 nodes; and its power draw, while very low, is higher than LoRaWAN's, which matters when the design target is years of unattended operation off-grid.

Accompanying power strategy, since protocol alone does not answer the deployment: aggressive duty cycling (already implied by 30-minute reporting), deep-sleep hardware between wakes, and solar with a supercapacitor buffer — because in Bangladesh off-grid operation is the default design constraint, and with 400 nodes the dominant lifetime cost is site visits, not hardware.

B4 · short answer

Analyse the following procurement claim and state what evidence you would require: "Our new server line is eco-friendly, Energy Star certified, and will be carbon neutral by 2040."

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from Class 8, Topic 04 (the seven sins) plus Topic 02 (certification limits). Three clauses, three different verdicts — treat them separately, which is the structure the faculty's own claim analyses use.

Clause 1 — "eco-friendly": greenwashing, the vagueness sin. The term has no defined meaning and no verification behind it. RoHS compliant? Recycled content? Sustainably sourced? Recyclable? Any company can print it at zero cost. The lecture's rule: if a company cannot state exactly what it means in numbers or certifications, assume it means nothing. There is also a no proof element, since no third-party standard is cited.

Clause 2 — "Energy Star certified": genuine but limited. Energy Star is a real US EPA certification meaning the product is in the top 25% of energy efficiency for its category. That is a real, verifiable claim. But it covers operational energy only — not materials, manufacturing, or end-of-life. Using it to support an overall "eco-friendly" claim edges into the hidden trade-off sin. For a server the limitation is less severe than for a phone, since 24/7 operation means operational carbon reaches 60–80% of the lifetime total, so the certification is more relevant here than it would be for a consumer device — a nuance worth stating.

Clause 3 — "carbon neutral by 2040": insufficient without details. A commitment 15+ years out with no interim milestones and no independent accountability is cheap to make. The lecture's four questions: does it include Scope 3; what are the 2025 and 2030 interim targets; is it SBTi-verified; and is offsetting doing most of the work rather than actual emission reduction?

Evidence I would require before accepting the bid: an EPEAT Gold or TCO Certified registration to substantiate the lifecycle claim; a RoHS declaration against the 0.1% by weight / 0.01% cadmium thresholds; a stated percentage of recycled content; the 80 PLUS tier of the PSU (Gold minimum for data centre use); per-product LCA data in the sustainability report; and a contractual take-back programme at end of life. On the neutrality pledge, SBTi verification and published interim targets.

The template for what a good claim looks like, per the faculty's model: specific standard + specific metric + specific benefit. Their example — "Energy Star certified and uses 35% less energy, saving $28/year in electricity" — is credible because every element is checkable. The claim under analysis has none of that structure in two of its three clauses.

Section C — Scenario (answer one)

C1 · long answer · 20 marks

A Dhaka commercial bank asks you to prepare a five-year Green IT roadmap covering its data centre, its 180 branches, and its hardware procurement. Set out your recommendations, justify them technically and financially, and address the Bangladeshi regulatory context.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from The full-course synthesis question, drawing on all six classes. The structure that scores best is the Class 13 four-phase lifecycle as the skeleton, with the technical content from Classes 7, 8, 11, 12 and 16 slotted into the phases where it belongs. Do not present six disconnected topic summaries — the marks are for integration.

Framing. This is a lifecycle, not a project, and enterprise-wide Green IT maturity typically takes 3–5 years — so a five-year roadmap is the right horizon. For a bank it is also not discretionary: Bangladesh Bank green guidelines require commercial banks to allocate funds to green finance, implement in-house green office policies, and report annual Scope 1 and 2 footprints. The roadmap should be presented as a compliance deliverable that also reduces cost, which is a far stronger position than the reverse.

Year 1 — Phase 1: Assess & Plan

Establish Scope 1, 2 and 3 baselines: generator fuel (Scope 1 — significant here, since grid unreliability makes N+1 diesel capacity near-mandatory), purchased electricity across the data centre and 180 branches (Scope 2), and the embodied footprint of procured hardware plus e-waste (Scope 3). Run a Maturity Model assessment and gap analysis. Measure the data centre's PUE and, critically, its server utilisation — because PUE alone cannot see idle capacity.

Kotter steps 1–2: build urgency on rising commercial electricity tariffs, regulatory exposure and reputational risk; form a Green Steering Committee spanning IT, Facilities, Finance and HR. An IT-only committee cannot change procurement standards or branch manager performance criteria and will stall.

Years 1–2 — Phase 2: Architect & Implement

Branches — the largest and fastest win. Deploy centralised automated PC power management plus thin clients, cutting per-terminal draw from 150 W to 15 W and enforcing off-hours shutdown. The power management policy alone has effectively zero CapEx — a Group Policy change — making it the ideal Kotter quick win (steps 5–6), delivering visible savings before any capital request. Kotter step 5 matters here literally: the barrier is usually a policy requiring always-on hardware, not a technical one.

Data centre — attack the IT load before the facility. Virtualise and consolidate onto Type 1 (bare-metal) hypervisors — ESXi, KVM or Hyper-V — targeting 65–80% utilisation at a realistic 3:1 to 15:1 ratio, then physically decommission the surplus hosts. Type 1 is required rather than preferred: Type 2 imposes 10–20% overhead and cannot control CPU C-states/P-states. Enable DRS and DPM with threshold hysteresis so off-peak load consolidates automatically and idle hosts sleep, while avoiding migration-induced energy net-loss.

Two locally decisive benefits to highlight: halving IT power roughly doubles UPS runtime during load-shedding, giving generators time to stabilise — a resilience gain that never appears in a payback calculation but matters enormously in Dhaka. And hypervisor HA clusters deliver the isolation and high availability Bangladesh Bank circulars require for core banking software without duplicate hardware sprawl.

Procurement — the highest-leverage single act. Write sustainability into every tender: Energy Star, EPEAT Gold (or TCO Certified where supply-chain assurance matters), 80 PLUS Gold PSUs, RoHS compliance, a minimum 5-year warranty with guaranteed spare parts, and a contractual take-back programme. The 80 PLUS clause is especially valuable locally, since the lecture notes many locally assembled PCs ship 70–75% efficient supplies against 92% for Gold — and PSU losses compound at roughly 1.5× once cooling is counted. Evaluate on TCO over 4–5 years, not sticker price. This is the procurement multiplier: one tender determines the environmental performance of thousands of devices for years.

Years 2–4 — Phase 3: Measure & Optimize

Deploy automated DCIM and IoT monitoring across the data centre and branch network, tracking PUE, utilisation ratios and kWh per unit of service. Green IoT design principles apply if branch sensors are battery-powered: duty cycle under the 1% rule, deep-sleep hardware, and NB-IoT for branch metering — attractive here because Bangladesh's dense mobile coverage means no private gateway infrastructure to maintain.

Address the data centre facility after the IT load has shrunk, not before — otherwise the cooling and power plant is sized for a load that is about to fall, and will run at inefficient partial load. Then consider containment, chiller upgrades and SREDA's rooftop solar guidance, which attacks Scope 2 at source. If the facility must reach Tier III for core banking under central bank guidance, note that this requires concurrent maintainability — dual-corded PDUs and isolatable cooling loops — not simply more spare units, and that most existing Dhaka facilities need phased brownfield retrofits (3–9 months) rather than a rebuild.

Years 4–5 — Phase 4: Institutionalize

This is what determines whether any of the above survives. Link Green IT KPIs to executive and branch manager performance reviews (Kotter step 8). Establish certified chain-of-custody e-waste disposal with vendor audits — critical given only a handful of DoE-certified recyclers operate nationally, and that the E-Waste Rules 2021 place take-back obligations on manufacturers the bank should push into its contracts. Apply the circular hierarchy to retired branch hardware: assess Reuse, Repair and Refurbish before any device reaches a recycler. Adopt ISO 14001 as the EMS framework and publish annual ESG reporting on GHG Protocol scopes. Reinvest the energy savings into the next phase (step 7) so the programme becomes self-funding.

Guard explicitly against the "set and forget" risk — staff turn over, legacy hardware creeps back, virtualisation ratios degrade into virtual sprawl. Counter with VM lifecycle governance (named owners, expiry dates requiring active renewal), scheduled re-audits, and KPI ownership.

The financial case

Every proposal goes to the board with CapEx, annual OpEx savings, simple payback, 5-year net ROI and tonnes CO₂e avoided — because CFOs do not approve projects on environmental goodwill. Sequence by payback: the branch power management policy pays back immediately at near-zero CapEx; thin-client rollout typically inside two years; virtualisation around three to four; facility retrofit last and phased. Present the carbon figure (kWh × 0.6 ÷ 1,000) as the Scope 2 line for Bangladesh Bank reporting.

One caution to include. Replacing serviceable branch desktops carries substantial embodied carbon — 70–80% of a device's lifetime footprint. Phase the thin-client rollout to coincide with the natural refresh cycle, and apply the circular hierarchy to what is displaced. The free policy change delivers most of the benefit in the meantime, so there is no reason to force an early manufacturing event.

The one-page final sweep

Constants

8,760 hrs/yr · 525,600 min/yr · 0.6 kg CO₂e/kWh (BD grid) · ÷1000 for W→kW, kg→t, µA→mA.

PUE / DCiE

PUE = Total ÷ IT (≥1). DCiE = 1/PUE × 100%. 1.5 ≈ average (66.7%). Facility kW = PUE × IT kW.

Downtime

(1 − uptime) × 525,600. Tier I 28.8h · II 22.0h · III 1.6h (94.6 min) · IV 0.4h (26 min).

Redundancy (N=3)

N=3 · N+1=4 · 2N=6 · 2N+1=8. 2N overbuild = 100%.

Tier II vs III

Both N+1. III = concurrently maintainable — redundant PATHS (dual-corded PDUs), not more spares.

Server power

Idle = 50–70% of peak. \( P = P_{idle} + L\%(P_{max}-P_{idle}) \). Only switching OFF saves real energy.

Consolidation

Hosts = VMs ÷ ratio (round up). Legacy 10–15% → target 65–80%. Footprint falls 50–70%.

Hypervisors

Type 1 bare-metal, 1–3% overhead, ESXi/KVM/Hyper-V/Xen. Type 2 hosted, 10–20%, VirtualBox. T2 can't control C/P-states.

Four business formulas

$ = kWh × tariff · Payback = CapEx ÷ $ · t CO₂e = kWh×f ÷1000 · ROI% = (Savings−CapEx)÷CapEx ×100.

Payback bands

<2 yrs excellent · 2–4 acceptable · >4 poor. Software projects beat hardware projects.

4 phases

Assess & Plan → Architect & Implement → Measure & Optimize → Institutionalize. 3–5 yrs. Skip 4 and it regresses.

Kotter pairs

1–2 urgency + coalition · 3–4 vision + communicate · 5–6 empower + quick wins · 7–8 sustain + anchor.

80/20

Technology = 20% of failure. Humans = 80%. 70% of transformations fail on change management.

GHG scopes

1 = gensets (direct) · 2 = purchased electricity · 3 = embodied carbon + e-waste + supply chain.

Circular hierarchy

Reuse → Repair → Refurbish → Remanufacture → Recycle. Recycling is the LAST resort.

Embodied carbon

70–80% of a consumer device's lifetime CO₂e = manufacturing. Servers (24/7) only 20–40%.

5 certifications

Energy Star (top 25%, operational only) · EPEAT (Bronze/Silver+50%/Gold+75%) · TCO (+SOCIAL) · 80 PLUS Gold 92% · RoHS (0.1%/0.01% Cd).

7 greenwashing sins

Hidden trade-off · No proof · Vagueness · Irrelevance · Lesser of two evils · Fibbing · False labels.

E-waste numbers

62 Mt · 17.4% recycled · $57 bn lost · 2.6 kg/person. BD: 200–400 kt, ~100k informal workers.

IoT power budget

d = t_active/T. \( I_{avg}=d I_a+(1-d)I_s \). Life = mAh ÷ mA ÷ 8760. Radio dominates, not CPU.

LPWAN

LoRaWAN 2–15+ km unlicensed · NB-IoT 1–10 km licensed cellular no gateways · Zigbee 10–100 m mesh · BLE 10–50 m fastest.

Bangladesh one-liners

Formalise the informal sector · Flood elevation decides the site · Consolidation doubles UPS runtime · BB requires Scope 1&2 · Off-grid is the default.

The multiplier chain

ΔP_IT ×PUE → ×8760 → ×tariff → ÷CapEx = payback. Cut IT load FIRST, then the facility.

If you forget everything else

Impact is locked in upstream and early — in the design, the specification, the site, and the duty cycle. Not at the point of use.
