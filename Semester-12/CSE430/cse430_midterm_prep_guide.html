How this exam actually works

Read this before anything else — it changes how you should study.

If this is all brand new to you

Don't worry — this guide assumes you know nothing about software testing yet. Here's the one-paragraph picture to hold in your head: software is written by people, people make mistakes, and a mistake in software is called a defect (or bug). Software testing is the work of finding those defects before real users hit them, and software quality assurance is the bigger job of setting up good habits so fewer defects happen in the first place. Everything in this course is either (a) a way to measure or manage quality, or (b) a clever technique for choosing which tests to run so you find the most bugs with the least effort. Each topic below starts with a plain-English "start from zero" box, so you can read them in order and build up.

The faculty notice is unusually specific, and it tells you the exam is not a definitions quiz. You'll be handed a short scenario — a login system, an ATM, an online form, a project with some numbers — and asked to analyse it, choose the right testing or quality concept, apply it, and justify why. That last word matters: naming the technique earns you little; showing why it fits this scenario earns the marks.

The mindset that scores

For every scenario, silently ask three things: (1) What is the scenario really testing — a single input range, a combination of rules, or a pile of independent settings? (2) Which technique matches that shape? (3) Can I produce the concrete artefact (the test values, the table, the number) and one sentence justifying the choice?

Because of that, this guide is built around scenario questions with fully worked solutions, not flashcards. Each solution shows every step and why it follows the one before — so on exam day you can reproduce the reasoning, not just recognise the answer. The final section is a technique-picker: given any scenario, how to decide which tool to reach for. That decision is the single most examinable skill here.

Watch out

The trap in every scenario question is answering with a definition instead of an application. If the question gives you an ATM with four conditions and you write "Decision Table Testing is a black-box technique for combinations…" without building the table for that ATM, you've answered a different question than the one asked.

Topic 01

Quality attributes, factors, criteria, metrics

The four-level ladder that turns a vague word like "reliable" into a number.

Start from zero

Imagine you tell a builder "I want a nice, comfortable house." That's useless to them — "nice" isn't something you can measure or check off. A good builder would push back: "OK, comfortable how? Warm? Then let's say the heating keeps every room at 21°C. Quiet? Then let's say outside noise stays under 30 decibels." They're turning your fuzzy wish into concrete numbers they can actually build toward and verify.

Software quality works exactly the same way. "The app should be reliable" is the fuzzy wish. This topic is the four-step ladder that walks that wish down into a checkable number: the broad quality (a factor) → splits into specific things to check (criteria) → each of which gets an actual number (metric). Learn the ladder once and you can apply it to any quality word the exam throws at you.

Start with the problem this solves. A stakeholder says "the system should be reliable and easy to use." That sentence is useless to a tester — you can't test a wish. Software quality work is the discipline of walking that fuzzy wish down a ladder until it becomes something you can measure and check. Your faculty draws that ladder as four levels.

A quality attribute is an observable characteristic of the software — reliability, usability, security, portability. It answers "what kind of good are we talking about?" A quality factor is a higher-level category that groups related attributes together for specification and evaluation (your notes give the McCall groupings: operation, revision, transition). A criterion is a specific, evaluable condition under a factor — for reliability, that's things like "failure frequency" and "recoverability." A metric is the quantitative measure of a criterion — the actual number, like mean time between failures or failures per 100 hours.

The ladder (memorise the direction of travel)
\[ \textbf{Factor} \;\longrightarrow\; \textbf{Criteria} \;\longrightarrow\; \textbf{Metrics} \]

Management-oriented view (the broad quality goal) on the left → quantitative measures on the right. Reading right-to-left, numbers roll up into criteria, criteria roll up into a factor.

The worked example your faculty repeated

Factor: Reliability → Criteria: failure frequency, recoverability → Metrics: mean time between failures (MTBF), number of failures per 100 hours. If you can reproduce exactly this chain, you can reproduce it for usability, security, or any other factor they throw at you.

The other idea from this lecture is what a comprehensive quality requirement looks like. A weak requirement states only what the software does. A strong one also states how well: it is specific, verifiable, and measurable. Your notes contrast these directly.

Weak vs improved (this is exam gold)

Weak: "The system should be reliable and easy to use."
Improved: "The system shall be available 99.5% of the time during office hours, recover from failure within 5 minutes, and let a new user complete registration within 3 minutes." Notice the improvement is just the ladder in action — vague factor turned into measurable criteria.

Question 1 · scenario · short answer

A hospital's appointment-booking system has this requirement in its SRS: "The system should be fast and dependable." Rewrite it as a comprehensive quality requirement, and for the "dependable" part, trace it down the factor → criteria → metric ladder.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from This tests whether you can turn a vague requirement into a measurable, verifiable one (Lecture 2, "Comprehensive Quality Requirements") and walk the factor → criteria → metric ladder (Lecture 2, Slides 6–7). The examiner wants the vague word decomposed into numbers, not a restatement.

  1. "Fast" and "dependable" are factors, not testable statements.

    Diagnose: both are top-of-ladder categories — no number to check against, so they fail the "verifiable" test from Slide 8.

  2. "Fast" → efficiency → response time → respond within 2 seconds under normal load.

    Descend the ladder: pick the criterion (response time) and attach a metric (2 s). Mirrors the faculty's "respond within 2 seconds" example.

  3. "Dependable" → Reliability (factor) → failure frequency + recoverability (criteria).

    Apply the exact chain the faculty repeated: reliability splits into how often it fails and how fast it recovers.

  4. Criteria → metrics: available 99.5% during clinic hours; MTBF ≥ 200 h; recover within 5 min.

    Attach numbers: availability % and MTBF measure failure frequency; the 5-minute target measures recoverability.

Model rewrite

"The system shall respond within 2 seconds under normal load, be available 99.5% of the time during clinic hours, and recover from any failure within 5 minutes."
Ladder for "dependable": Reliability → {failure frequency, recoverability} → {99.5% availability / MTBF, 5-min recovery}. The justification is what earns marks: each number is the measurable form of a named criterion.

Topic 02

McCall's quality model

Three questions that sort every quality factor into a box.

McCall's model exists because "software quality" is not one property — it's several, and they pull in different directions. The model's insight is that every quality factor answers one of three questions about the software's life: how it runs now, how easily you can change it, and how easily you can move it somewhere new. Those three questions are the three categories, and your faculty uses these exact names.

McCall's three categories — learn the question, then the factors follow
CategoryIts questionFactors
Product Operation How well does it run now? Correctness, Reliability, Efficiency, Integrity, Usability
Product Revision How easily can it be changed / fixed / tested? Maintainability, Flexibility, Testability
Product Transition How easily can it move to new environments / systems? Portability, Reusability, Interoperability
Memory hook

Operation = run, Revision = change, Transition = move. Five factors in Operation, three in Revision, three in Transition. If you can place a factor in the right box and say which question it answers, you've got the McCall question.

Watch out

Two factors are easy to misfile. Testability lives in Revision (it's about how easily you can change-and-check the code), not Operation. Interoperability (works with other systems) and Portability (moves to other environments) both sit in Transition — don't confuse either with Integrity/Usability, which are about running now (Operation).

Question 2 · scenario · medium

An online banking system has these concerns. Assign each to a McCall category and name the factor, then justify:

  1. Login, balance check and fund transfer must produce correct, secure results.
  2. A new transaction-limit rule must be easy to modify and re-test.
  3. The system must integrate with mobile apps and third-party payment gateways.
Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from This is the exact online-banking example from Lecture 2 (Slides 10–11). It tests whether you map a real concern to the right McCall category by the question it answers, not by rote.

  1. (1) → Product Operation — Correctness & Integrity.

    Ask "how well does it run now?" Producing correct, secure results at runtime is exactly Operation; "secure" points to Integrity, "correct results" to Correctness.

  2. (2) → Product Revision — Maintainability & Testability.

    Ask "how easily can it be changed?" "Easy to modify" = Maintainability; "easy to re-test" = Testability. Both are Revision factors.

  3. (3) → Product Transition — Interoperability (& Portability).

    Ask "how easily can it move / connect?" Integrating with other systems is Interoperability, a Transition factor.

Answer

Operation (Correctness, Integrity) · Revision (Maintainability, Testability) · Transition (Interoperability). The justification — naming which of the three questions each concern answers — is the part that scores.

Topic 03

Cost of Quality (CoQ)

Four buckets — and the golden rule that prevention is the cheapest.

Here's the motivating idea: quality isn't free, but poor quality is far more expensive than good quality — you just pay for it later, and to different people. Cost of Quality is the accounting that makes this visible. It splits every quality-related dollar into four buckets, and the whole point is to show that spending early (left side) saves far more later (right side).

The four buckets split along two lines. First, conformance costs (money spent to achieve quality) versus non-conformance costs (money lost because quality failed). Then each side splits again:

The four CoQ buckets — the "when did we catch it?" ladder
BucketSpent to…Examples from your notes
Prevention
conformance		 stop defects ever happening SQA planning, training, coding standards, checklists/templates, requirement & design reviews, process improvement, config management
Appraisal
conformance		 detect defects (inspection) formal design reviews, peer reviews/inspections & walkthroughs, all testing levels (unit, integration, system, acceptance)
Internal failure
non-conformance		 fix defects found before delivery re-design, re-programming, regression re-testing, rework, delays, downtime — caught by our reviews/tests
External failure
non-conformance		 fix failures found after delivery warranty repairs, customer complaints, bug fixes in operation, customers' bad will, lost sales
The one distinction the exam will probe

Internal vs External failure = who found the defect. If you caught it (design review, your own testing, before the customer had it) → internal. If the customer or maintenance team caught it after installation → external. Same bug, wildly different cost — external failure adds bad will and lost sales on top of the fix.

Watch out — the classic mix-up

Appraisal is detecting; Internal failure is fixing. Running a test is appraisal. Correcting the bug that test found is internal failure. A code review is appraisal (prevention-flavoured detection); the re-programming after it is internal failure. If the activity finds a problem it's appraisal; if it repairs one it's failure cost.

Question 3 · scenario · medium

A team building an e-commerce checkout logs these five activities. Classify each into one of the four CoQ buckets and justify in a phrase:

  1. Running a two-day workshop teaching developers the secure-coding standard.
  2. A peer inspection of the payment module before it's merged.
  3. Re-coding the discount engine after that inspection found a rounding bug.
  4. A customer reports being double-charged in production; support issues a refund and hotfix.
  5. Regression-testing the whole checkout after the discount engine was re-coded.
Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from Tests whether you can classify real activities into Prevention / Appraisal / Internal / External failure (Lecture 2, Slides 12–19) using the "when/who caught it" logic — not just recite the four names.

  1. (a) Training workshop → Prevention.

    Purpose test: it reduces the number of defects before any exist. Training is a named prevention example in the notes.

  2. (b) Peer inspection → Appraisal.

    It detects, doesn't fix. Peer reviews/inspections are listed under appraisal (Slide 17).

  3. (c) Re-coding after the inspection → Internal failure.

    Fixing a defect we caught ourselves before delivery. Re-programming in response to review findings is the textbook internal-failure example.

  4. (d) Double-charge in production + refund + hotfix → External failure.

    Customer found it after installation. Complaint handling + operational bug fix = external failure (and note the "bad will" it carries).

  5. (e) Regression testing after the re-code → Internal failure.

    Trap: testing is usually appraisal, but the notes explicitly list "repeated design review and re-testing (regression tests)" under internal failure because it's rework triggered by an already-found defect. Tie it to the fix, not to fresh detection.

Answer

a = Prevention · b = Appraisal · c = Internal failure · d = External failure · e = Internal failure. Item (e) is the discriminator — most people reflexively say "appraisal" for anything with the word test in it. The justification (rework from a known defect) is what wins it.

Topic 04

Software quality across the SDLC — Verification vs Validation

Why testing at the end is not enough, and the two questions that split all quality work.

The core message of this lecture is a warning: testing alone cannot guarantee quality. Defects are born early — in requirements, in design, in process decisions — but testing only catches them late, after the flawed work is already built on top of. The later you catch a defect, the more it costs to fix (this connects straight back to Cost of Quality). So quality must be built into every phase, not inspected only at the end. That's what SQA is: planned, preventive, quality activities across the whole life cycle.

The registration-system illustration your faculty used

Suppose the requirement forgets that students must clear prerequisites before advanced courses. Caught at requirements review → fix one document. At design review → add validation logic before coding. At system testing → change code and re-test. After release → students register wrongly, records corrupt, emergency fixes. Same defect, exploding cost. That escalation is the argument for early SQA.

The most examinable idea here is the split between Verification and Validation. They sound alike but ask opposite questions.

V & V — learn the two questions first, everything else follows
VerificationValidation
Question Are we building the product right? Are we building the right product?
Checks against specs, standards, design user needs, business goals, real use
When throughout SDLC, before execution once executable software exists
Activities reviews, inspections, walkthroughs, static analysis system testing, acceptance testing, UAT, beta
Evidence review report, checklist, traceability matrix test results, user feedback, acceptance sign-off
Watch out

The one-line memory aid: Verification = "right" is an adverb (built correctly); Validation = "right" is an adjective (the correct thing). Anything you do by reading/reviewing artefacts is verification; anything you do by running the software against real user needs is validation. A design review is verification even though it happens "during development"; UAT is validation even if the requirement was written perfectly.

Question 4 · scenario · medium

For the course-registration system, label each activity as Verification or Validation, and justify with the question it answers:

  1. Reviewing the SRS to confirm the prerequisite rules are written clearly and completely.
  2. A real student attempts to register for a course without the prerequisite, and the system blocks it.
  3. Inspecting whether test cases exist for valid registration, prerequisite failure, seat-full, and clash.
  4. A department officer confirms the workflow matches real advising and approval practice.
Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from Straight from Lecture 3/4, Slides 18–22 (registration-system V&V examples). It tests whether you sort activities by which question they answer, not by when they happen.

  1. (a) Reviewing the SRS → Verification.

    Reading an artefact against its spec ("are we building it right?"). A review is the archetypal verification activity.

  2. (b) Real student blocked from invalid registration → Validation.

    Running the system against a real user need ("is this the right product?"). Executable + real use = validation.

  3. (c) Inspecting test-case coverage → Verification.

    Trap: it involves the word "test," but you're inspecting whether cases exist, not running them. Reviewing test cases against requirements is verification (listed explicitly in the notes).

  4. (d) Officer confirms workflow fits real advising → Validation.

    Checking against business/real-world need, done by a user/stakeholder. That's validation's home turf.

Answer

a = V · b = Val · c = V · d = Val. Item (c) is the discriminator — "test cases" tempts you toward validation, but reviewing them is verification.

Topic 05

Software quality metrics

Turning "is it good?" into numbers — and the formulas the exam will make you compute.

The premise: you cannot manage what you cannot measure. Metrics convert quality from opinion into evidence. Your faculty sorts them into three families by what they measure — the product, the process, or the project — and this classification is itself examinable.

Three metric families
FamilyMeasuresExamples
Productthe software artefact itselfLOC, defect density, cyclomatic complexity, failure rate, MTBF
Processhow well the dev/test process worksDefect Removal Efficiency, defect leakage, review effectiveness, test pass rate
Projectplanning & management healthschedule variance, effort variance, productivity, requirement volatility

Also know the three-word vocabulary the faculty distinguishes: a measure is a raw value (45 defects found); a metric is an interpreted measurement (4.5 defects/KLOC); an indicator is a decision (Module A is high-risk). Now the formulas — these are the ones you will be asked to compute.

The formulas to memorise
\[ \text{Defect Density} = \frac{\text{Number of defects}}{\text{Size (KLOC)}} \qquad \text{KLOC} = \frac{\text{LOC}}{1000} \] \[ \text{DRE} = \frac{\text{Defects found before release}}{\text{Defects found before release} + \text{Defects found after release}} \times 100\% \] \[ \text{Defect Leakage} = \frac{\text{Defects escaped to later phase / production}}{\text{Total defects}} \times 100\% \] \[ \text{Schedule Variance} = \text{Actual duration} - \text{Planned duration} \qquad \text{Effort Variance} = \text{Actual effort} - \text{Planned effort} \] \[ \text{MTBF} = \frac{\text{Total operating time}}{\text{Number of failures}} \qquad \text{Requirement Volatility} = \frac{\text{Changed requirements}}{\text{Total requirements}}\times 100\% \]
Why defect density beats raw defect count

Module A: 5,000 LOC, 20 defects → 4 defects/KLOC. Module B: 20,000 LOC, 40 defects → 2 defects/KLOC. B has more defects but A is more defect-prone per unit size. Raw counts punish big modules unfairly; density normalises by size so you compare fairly. This exact example is in the notes — expect to defend it.

Watch out — DRE denominator

DRE's denominator is total defects = before-release + after-release, not just before-release. A high DRE means your process removed most defects before shipping. Its mirror image is defect leakage (what escaped) — if DRE = 82%, leakage ≈ 18%.

Question 5 · scenario · hard · full computation

This is the faculty's own classroom problem. A system has:

ModuleLOCPre-release defectsPost-release defects
A5,000205
B10,000255
C20,0003010

Project: planned duration 8 weeks, actual 10 weeks; planned effort 60 person-days, actual 75. Find: (1) defect density per module, (2) overall DRE, (3) schedule variance, (4) effort variance, (5) the major quality risk.

Show worked solution

Try it first, then check each line — the right column says why the line follows.

  1. \( \text{DD}_A = \dfrac{20}{5000/1000} = \dfrac{20}{5} = 4 \) defects/KLOC

    Apply defect density: defects ÷ KLOC. 5,000 LOC = 5 KLOC. Use pre-release defects — density describes the artefact as built/tested.

  2. \( \text{DD}_B = \dfrac{25}{10} = 2.5,\quad \text{DD}_C = \dfrac{30}{20} = 1.5 \) defects/KLOC

    Same formula, each module. 10,000 LOC = 10 KLOC; 20,000 LOC = 20 KLOC.

  3. Pre-release total \(= 20+25+30 = 75\); post-release total \(= 5+5+10 = 20\).

    Sum each column to get project-wide figures — DRE is computed over the whole system, not per module.

  4. \( \text{DRE} = \dfrac{75}{75 + 20}\times 100 = \dfrac{75}{95}\times 100 \)

    Apply DRE: before ÷ (before + after). Denominator is all defects = 95, not 75.

  5. \( \text{DRE} \approx 78.9\% \)

    Divide: 75/95 = 0.789. Interpretation: ~21% of defects leaked to production — moderate removal, not great.

  6. \( \text{SV} = 10 - 8 = +2 \) weeks

    Schedule variance = actual − planned. Positive = delay.

  7. \( \text{EV} = 75 - 60 = +15 \) person-days

    Effort variance = actual − planned. Positive = overrun (25% more effort than planned).

Answer & the "major risk" justification

DD: A = 4, B = 2.5, C = 1.5 defects/KLOC · DRE ≈ 78.9% · SV = +2 weeks · EV = +15 person-days.
Major quality risk = Module A. Justify it: A has the highest defect density (4/KLOC — most defect-prone per unit size), the project is both late (+2 wk) and over-effort (+15 pd), and DRE is only ~79% so defects are already leaking. Module A concentrates the product risk while the schedule/effort overrun raises the chance testing gets rushed. Sanity check: C is largest but lowest density — size alone doesn't equal risk, which is exactly the density lesson.

Question 6 · scenario · short answer

A manager boasts: "Our test pass rate is 98% and our module has only 3 reported defects — quality is excellent." Give two reasons, from the limitations of metrics, why this conclusion may be wrong.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from Lecture 5, "Misuse and Limitations of Metrics" (Slide 23). The examiner wants you to show a metric can be gamed or misread, not to compute anything.

Reason 1 — a low defect count may mean weak testing, not high quality. If the test cases are shallow, few defects get found; the defects are still there. "3 reported defects" measures detection, not absence.

Reason 2 — a high pass rate can hide weak test cases. If the suite mostly checks easy, happy-path inputs, nearly everything passes while the risky boundary and combination cases go untested. The notes state plainly that one metric alone should never decide release readiness.

Answer

Both numbers describe the testing effort's behaviour, not the product's real quality. Read together with coverage and defect density they might mean something; alone they're an opinion dressed as evidence.

Topic 06

STLC & Test Strategy vs Test Plan

Testing has its own life cycle — and "strategy" and "plan" are not the same word.

People imagine testing as a single burst at the end. The Software Testing Life Cycle (STLC) reframes it as its own structured sequence that runs in parallel with development, so testing is planned, measurable, and documented rather than random button-clicking. Your faculty lists these phases:

STLC phases (in order)

Requirement Analysis → Test Planning → Test Case Design → Test Environment Setup → Test Execution → Defect Reporting & Tracking → Test Closure

Strategy vs Plan — the distinction the exam loves

Test Strategy = the general approach (high-level, organisation- or product-wide, relatively stable): how testing will generally be done. Test Plan = that approach applied to one specific project/module/release (what to test, when, by whom, with what data; changes per project). Strategy is the policy; plan is the project-specific document that follows the policy.

The assignment-submission example makes it concrete. Strategy: "use requirement-based testing for major functions, boundary value analysis for file-size limits, negative testing for invalid types/late submissions, regression after fixes." Plan: "test login, course selection, upload, deadline validation, confirmation on Chrome and Firefox with valid and invalid accounts; finish functional testing before release." The strategy names techniques; the plan names the exact features, tools, and schedule.

Watch out

Don't confuse a test plan (scope, approach, schedule, resources, entry/exit criteria, risks, deliverables for a project) with a test case (one documented set of steps, data, and expected result). Also know entry criteria (conditions needed before testing starts, e.g. requirements finalised, module available) vs exit criteria (conditions to stop, e.g. all critical cases passed, no open high-severity defect).

Question 7 · scenario · short answer

A team is testing an online food-delivery app. For each statement below, say whether it belongs in the Test Strategy or the Test Plan, and justify:

  1. "For all products, use risk-based testing and automate regression."
  2. "Test the cart, checkout and payment modules in Sprint 4 using Selenium on Chrome and Safari, with two test accounts."
  3. "Apply boundary value analysis wherever an input has a numeric range."
Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from Lecture 9/10, Slides 9–10. Tests whether you sort statements by scope and stability: general+stable = strategy, project-specific = plan.

  1. (a) → Test Strategy.

    "For all products" = organisation-wide, stable policy. That's the definition of strategy.

  2. (b) → Test Plan.

    Names specific modules, a sprint, tools, browsers, accounts — the concrete, project-specific detail that defines a plan.

  3. (c) → Test Strategy.

    A general technique rule ("wherever a range exists") independent of any one project = strategy. The plan would say which field, in which module.

Answer

a = Strategy · b = Plan · c = Strategy. Rule of thumb: if you could copy the sentence to a different project unchanged, it's strategy; if it only makes sense for this project, it's the plan.

Topic 07

Requirements Traceability Matrix (RTM)

One table that answers "has every requirement been tested?"

Imagine a system with 40 requirements and 200 test cases. How do you prove nothing slipped through — that every requirement has at least one test, and you know its result? You build an RTM: a table that maps each requirement to the test case(s) that check it, plus each case's status. It's a coverage ledger.

RTM shape (from the assignment-submission example)
Req IDRequirementTest Case IDStatus
REQ-AS-01Submit valid assignment before deadlineTC-AS-001Passed
REQ-AS-02Reject submission after deadlineTC-AS-002Failed
REQ-AS-03Accept only allowed file typesTC-AS-003, 004Not Run
What RTM buys you (say these when asked "why use it")

Prevents missing requirements, shows test coverage, exposes untested features, supports change-impact analysis (change a requirement → the matrix shows which test cases to revisit), and provides audit/review evidence. In short: it connects requirements, test cases, and results in one traceable line.

Watch out

An RTM's power is in the links and statuses, not the table itself. A requirement with no test case is the whole point of building one — the empty cell is the finding. And a "Failed" or "Not Run" status flags exactly where release risk sits.

Question 8 · scenario · short answer

Late in a project, a stakeholder asks a tester to prove that every requirement in the SRS has been tested and to identify anything that hasn't. Which document answers this, and how does it do so? Then explain how the same document helps when REQ-05 is changed after a client request.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from Lecture 9/10, Slides 26–28. Two skills: naming the RTM as the coverage tool, and applying it to change-impact analysis — a favourite second-half-of-question twist.

Document: the Requirements Traceability Matrix. It maps every requirement ID to the test case(s) verifying it and each case's status. To prove full coverage, you scan the matrix: any requirement row with a test case ID and a Passed status is covered; any row with a blank test case column, or a Not-Run/Failed status, is exactly the untested or at-risk item you surface.

When REQ-05 changes: you look up REQ-05's row, read off the test cases linked to it, and know precisely which cases must be updated and re-run — that's change-impact analysis. Without the RTM you'd guess; with it, the affected tests are one lookup away.

Answer

RTM — because it traces requirement → test case → result, coverage gaps become visible empty cells and change impact becomes a single-row lookup.

Topic 08

Equivalence Partitioning & Boundary Value Analysis

You can't test every input — so test one from each group, and both edges of every group.

Start from zero

Imagine a nightclub with a "must be 18–25" entry rule and a bouncer at the door. To check the bouncer is doing the job, you don't need to send a person of every single age from 1 to 100 through the door. That would take all night. Instead you think: "everyone under 18 should be turned away — they're all treated the same, so one under-age person is enough to test that group. Everyone 18–25 should get in — again, one is enough. Everyone over 25 turned away — one is enough."

That "group people who get treated the same, then test one from each group" idea is Equivalence Partitioning. Now, where does a lazy bouncer most likely slip up? Right at the edges — letting in a 17-year-old, or wrongly turning away someone who just turned 18. So you also send people right at the boundary: 17, 18, 25, 26. That edge-checking is Boundary Value Analysis. Two simple ideas, and together they shrink a hundred tests down to about six without losing coverage.

Now the same idea in testing language. The problem both techniques attack: a field that accepts, say, 1 to 100 has a hundred-plus possible inputs. You can't test all of them (exhaustive testing is impossible under real time and budget), so you need a smart way to pick a small set of inputs that still covers all the behaviours. Two techniques do this, and they work together.

▶ Boundary Value Analysis & Equivalence Partitioning: Software Testing Tutorial — Guru99. Why it helps: a slow, beginner-paced walk through the exact pizza-style range example your faculty uses, showing how the partitions and boundary values are picked step by step.

Equivalence Partitioning (EP) divides the input into groups — equivalence classes — where the system is expected to behave the same for every value in the group. The hypothesis (your faculty states it exactly like this): if one value in a partition passes, all others in it will pass; if one fails, all fail. So you pick just one representative from each partition. That collapses a hundred inputs into a handful.

Boundary Value Analysis (BVA) adds the observation that defects cluster at the edges of partitions — off-by-one errors, wrong < vs ≤. So instead of one value per partition, BVA tests the values right at the boundaries between partitions. Your faculty's checklist of points to pick: minimum, just above minimum, a nominal value, just below maximum, maximum — and crucially, test both the valid and the invalid side of each boundary (also called range checking).

The pizza example your faculty worked

Order Pizza accepts 1–10 (valid, success message); 11–99 is invalid ("Only 10 Pizza can be ordered"); 0 or below is invalid. EP gives three partitions: {≤0 invalid}, {1–10 valid}, {≥11 invalid} — one value each, e.g. −5, 5, 50. BVA tests the edges instead: 0, 1, 10, 11 (and often 2 and 9 for "just inside"). Notice BVA deliberately straddles each boundary — 1 is the first valid, 0 the last invalid; 10 the last valid, 11 the first invalid.

Watch out

The single most common mistake: forgetting the invalid-side boundary. For a 1–10 range, testing 1 and 10 is not boundary testing — you must also test 0 and 11. The boundary is the line between two partitions, so both sides of the line get a value. Also: EP and BVA don't replace each other — BVA comes after EP (you need the partitions before you can find their boundaries).

What this tests & where it comes from Lecture 11 (BVA & EP). Directly mirrors the pizza example and the "Age: 17, 18, 19, 59, 60, 61" pattern the faculty shows in the technique comparison. Tests whether you can generate the values, not define the terms.

    Divide by expected behaviour: the rule creates one valid band and two invalid bands — three equivalence classes.

Topic 09

Decision Table Testing

When the output depends on a combination of conditions, not one input.

Start from zero

Think about an ATM. It only gives you cash if a whole chain of things is true at once: your card is valid, AND your PIN is right, AND you have enough money, AND you're under your daily limit. If any one of those is wrong, you get a different message. So the machine's behaviour isn't about one input — it's about the combination.

A decision table is just a tidy grid for writing out every such combination and what the system should do for each. You list the yes/no questions down the side (card valid? PIN correct?), and each column across the top is one scenario: "card valid = yes, PIN = yes, balance = yes, limit = yes → give cash." The clever bit is a "don't care" mark (a dash, –): once the card is invalid, it doesn't matter what the PIN is — the machine rejects it either way, so you don't write out every PIN possibility. That's how a scary-looking 16 combinations shrinks to just 5 rows. Each row then becomes one test case.

Now in testing language. EP and BVA handle one input at a time. But real systems decide based on combinations: a login depends on username AND password AND account status AND OTP; an ATM on card AND PIN AND balance AND limit. Testing each input alone misses defects that only appear when conditions interact. Decision Table Testing lays every relevant combination out as a table so no rule is forgotten.

▶ What is Decision Table Testing? Software Testing Tutorial — Guru99. Why it helps: builds a decision table from scratch for a simple login/upload example, so you can watch conditions, rules, and the "don't care" dash come together before you tackle the ATM problem below.

A decision table has four parts: conditions (the yes/no questions that drive the decision), condition values (Y = true, N = false, and "–" = don't care, meaning that condition is irrelevant for this rule), actions (the system responses), and rules (each column = one specific combination of condition values and its resulting action). One rule usually becomes one test case.

The "don't care" (–) is the whole trick

If the card is invalid, the ATM rejects it regardless of PIN, balance, or limit — so those conditions are "–" for that rule. The "–" collapses many combinations into one rule. This is how the faculty reduces a table from \(2^4 = 16\) raw combinations down to just 5 rules: once an early condition fails, the later ones don't matter.

The ATM reduced decision table (memorise this shape)

C3 Sufficient balance?Y––NY

Watch out

Common mistakes the faculty flags: missing a condition, mixing a condition with an action (conditions are yes/no questions; actions are responses), writing test cases before the table is finished, ignoring "don't care" values, and forgetting negative rules. The "checked in sequence" assumption (card → PIN → balance → limit) is what lets you use "–" — state it explicitly in your answer.

Topic 10

Pairwise (All-Pairs) Testing

When there are too many settings to combine — test every pair, not every combination.

Start from zero

Suppose you're testing a website that has to work on 4 browsers, 3 operating systems, and 2 languages. If you tried every single combination it's 4 × 3 × 2 = 24 tests — and real apps have far more knobs, so the number quickly becomes thousands. Nobody has time for that.

Here's the shortcut, and it's based on a real observation: most bugs that only show up in combinations are caused by just two settings clashing — say, "Chrome + Spanish" breaks, no matter the operating system. So instead of testing every full combination, you build a smaller set of tests where every pair of settings (every browser paired with every OS, every browser with every language, and so on) shows up at least once. You still catch the two-setting bugs, but with a fraction of the tests. That's pairwise (all-pairs) testing — cover all the pairs, skip the redundant full combinations.

Now in testing language. Decision tables work when you have a few conditions. But some systems have many independent parameters, each with several values — browsers, payment methods, coupon types, locations — and the full combination count explodes. The bookstore example in your notes reaches 96 combinations from six variables; a real config space can hit thousands. Exhaustive testing is impossible. Pairwise testing is the smart sample.

▶ Software Testing Tutorial — What is pairwise testing? (with example). Why it helps: walks through why the combinations explode and how picking all-pairs shrinks the test set, with a worked example much like the bookstore one in your notes.

Its justification is an empirical fact your faculty quotes: most defects (experimentally 60–95%) come from the interaction of just two parameters, not from three or four at once. So instead of covering every full combination, you design a much smaller set of test cases in which every possible pair of values (across any two parameters) appears at least once. You catch the two-way interaction faults while discarding the redundant higher-order combinations.

Interaction fault — the thing pairwise catches

An interaction fault is when each value works fine alone but a specific pair fails together. The faculty's example: Chrome works, Credit Card works, Discount Coupon works — but Chrome + Credit Card + Discount Coupon → payment failure. Pairwise guarantees the Chrome+CreditCard, Chrome+Coupon, and CreditCard+Coupon pairs are each tested, so such a fault surfaces.

Watch out — what pairwise does NOT do

Pairwise only guarantees 2-way coverage. A fault that needs three specific values together can slip through. Other limits your notes list: it can produce unrealistic combinations users never hit, it may miss faults that only show in logs (not the UI), it doesn't find back-end bugs, and it's useless if you pick the wrong variables. So it reduces effort dramatically but isn't a guarantee of correctness.

Topic 11

Which technique? — the decision the exam is really testing

The faculty said "identify the appropriate technique." This is how you decide, fast.

Nearly every scenario question reduces to one choice: given this system, which test-design technique fits? The trick is to read the shape of the input in the scenario. Three shapes, three tools.

The picker — match the scenario's shape to the technique
If the scenario is about…UseTell-tale words
a single input with a range or valid/invalid classesEP (one per class), then BVA (the edges)"between X and Y", "age 18–60", "1 to 100", "max file size"
a decision driven by a combination of conditions / business rulesDecision Table Testing"only if all of…", "depends on card AND PIN AND…", "if/then rules"
many independent parameters, each with several values, combinations explodingPairwise / All-Pairs"browsers × payment × coupon × OS", "too many configurations"
One-line justifications to have ready

EP/BVA: "The input is a numeric range, so defects cluster at class boundaries — I partition then test edges." Decision table: "The output depends on a combination of conditions, so I enumerate the rules to avoid missing an interaction." Pairwise: "There are too many independent configurations to test exhaustively, and most faults are two-way, so I cover all value-pairs."

The distinction that trips people

Decision table vs pairwise both handle "combinations," so which? Decision table when conditions are logically linked business rules (card valid AND PIN correct → dispense) and the number is small enough to enumerate. Pairwise when parameters are independent settings (browser, OS, payment) and the full product is too large to enumerate. Rules → table; configurations → pairs.
