Topic 01

What is acceptance testing, and why is it the last gate?

Every other testing level in the course asks "did we build it right?" This one asks "did we build the right thing?"

Picture a tailor who has just finished sewing a suit. Before the customer ever wears it out the door, the tailor has already checked the seams don't fray, the buttons don't fall off, and the fabric hasn't shrunk — that is quality control inside the workshop, and it is what unit testing, integration testing, and system testing do for software. But none of that tells you whether the customer can actually move their arms in the jacket, whether it fits their shoulders, or whether it's even the style they asked for. Only the customer trying it on can answer that. In software, that final "does the customer actually accept this" step is acceptance testing.

Formally: acceptance testing is the phase of the software development lifecycle (SDLC) that verifies whether the software meets the specified requirements and is genuinely ready for deployment — checked from the point of view of the people who will use or operate it, not the people who built it. It happens after system testing is complete and before the software goes live, and it is normally carried out by end users or clients, working in a realistic operating environment, rather than by the development team.

Key idea

Acceptance testing is the final checkpoint before production release. Everything before it asks "does the code work correctly?" Acceptance testing alone asks "does this actually satisfy the business need and the user, in the real world?"

The lecture frames this with two statistics worth memorising exactly, because they are the kind of number an exam loves to quote back at you: 88% of professionals consider UAT (User Acceptance Testing) critical for achieving software quality — yet 29% report that poor-quality software still reaches the UAT phase in the first place. Read those together and the point is: almost everyone agrees this gate matters, but a large minority of teams are still letting software arrive at the gate broken, which is exactly why a well-defined, disciplined UAT process is needed rather than an informal "let the client click around for a bit."

Acceptance testing specifically validates three things about the software:

  • Functional correctness — does it do what it's supposed to do?
  • Usability — can a real user actually get it done without a manual?
  • Reliability — does it keep working the way it should under normal, realistic use?
Watch out

Don't confuse acceptance testing with "extra QA testing done by testers at the end." The defining feature is who is testing and what question they're answering — end users/clients/operations people, asking "is this fit for real-world use and ready to go live?" — not another round of the developers' own bug-hunting.

Topic 02

UAT vs OAT — the first pair students mix up

Same family, completely different question being asked.

"Acceptance testing" is an umbrella term with six named types underneath it (Topic 04 walks through all six). But two of them come up so often that the exam is very likely to test whether you can tell them apart cleanly: UAT and OAT.

User Acceptance Testing (UAT)

UAT is conducted by the end users to verify that the software meets their needs and requirements. It focuses on functionality and usability in real-world scenarios. Ask yourself: "if I handed this to the person who actually asked for this feature, would they say yes, this does what I needed?" That question is UAT's whole job.

Operational Acceptance Testing (OAT)

OAT — also called production acceptance testing — verifies that the software is ready to be deployed and operated in the production environment. It is not about whether a feature is useful to a user; it's about whether the system as a whole can survive being run in production. It includes checks such as:

  • backup and restore procedures
  • disaster recovery
  • routine maintenance tasks
  • performance under production-like conditions
Key idea

UAT asks "does this software do what the business/user needed?" OAT asks "can operations keep this thing alive and running once it's live?" One is about the feature; the other is about the machine room.

UAT vs OAT at a glance
User Acceptance Testing (UAT)Operational Acceptance Testing (OAT)
Who testsEnd users / clientsIT operations / support team
Question askedDoes it meet my needs and work the way I expect?Can this be safely run and maintained in production?

Watch out

A very common exam trap: a scenario about simulating a crash, testing backups, or checking failover sounds like "testing," but because it's about keeping the system alive in production rather than about a user's feature, it's OAT, not UAT — even though both happen in the same phase of the SDLC.

Topic 03

The acceptance testing process — a five-step pyramid

Acceptance testing happens after system testing and before deployment, following five structured phases.

Once system testing is done, a QA team writes and sets up acceptance tests to see how the software behaves in a simulated production environment. This confirms the software's stability and surfaces any remaining flaws. The lecture presents this as a pyramid built from the ground up — you can't skip a lower layer and jump to a higher one.

The five phases, bottom to top
  1. Planning — develop a detailed plan outlining the acceptance criteria and test cases, based on the business requirements.
  2. Testing — execute the test cases, letting end users interact with the software to assess its usability and functionality.
  3. Recording — document the outcomes of the tests, noting any defects or issues encountered.
  4. Comparing — compare the test results against the acceptance criteria to determine whether the software meets the required standards.
  5. Result evaluation — evaluate the overall results to decide if the software is ready for deployment.

Notice the shape of the logic: you can't compare results against criteria you never wrote down in the planning step, and you can't reach a deployment decision in result evaluation without first recording what actually happened during testing. Each layer depends on the one below it — that's why it's drawn as a pyramid rather than a plain list.

Key idea

Memorise the order as one phrase: Plan → Test → Record → Compare → Evaluate. Through this structured approach, production-ready software ends up matching actual business expectations, rather than just "seeming to work."

Watch out

Students often merge "Recording" and "Comparing" into one step because they feel similar. They're distinct: Recording is neutral documentation (what happened, defect or not), while Comparing is a judgment step (does what happened satisfy the criteria we planned?).

Topic 04

The six types of acceptance testing, one at a time

This is the part that's easy to blur together — so build each type from scratch, with its own tiny example, before comparing them.

"Acceptance testing" isn't one single activity — it's a category with six named members, each answering a different acceptance question, for a different audience, at a different point before release. Go through them one at a time; don't try to hold all six in your head at once yet.

1. User Acceptance Testing (UAT)

Who: the end users themselves. Checks: that the software meets their needs and requirements — functionality and usability, in real-world scenarios. Tiny example: before a hospital adopts a new patient-scheduling system, the receptionists who will use it daily walk through booking, rescheduling, and cancelling an appointment to confirm it actually supports how they work.

2. Operational Acceptance Testing (OAT)

Who: the operations/IT support team. Checks: that the software can be operated in the production environment — backup/restore, disaster recovery, maintenance tasks, and performance. Tiny example: before that same scheduling system goes live, the IT team confirms the nightly database backup can actually be restored, and that the system handles the clinic's peak Monday-morning login surge without slowing to a crawl.

3. Contract Acceptance Testing

Who: typically the client/procurement side of a contracted build. Checks: that the software meets the specifications and requirements written into a signed contract — every clause, before final acceptance. Tiny example: a logistics company hired a vendor to build a custom shipment-tracking platform under a contract listing 40 specific features; before releasing the final payment, the logistics company checks the delivered system against every one of those 40 clauses.

4. Regulation Acceptance Testing

Who: compliance/regulatory reviewers. Checks: that the software complies with the necessary regulations and standards — particularly important in tightly regulated industries such as healthcare, finance, and aviation. Tiny example: a new hospital records system is checked against patient-data-privacy regulations before it's allowed to store real patient information.

5. Alpha Testing

Who: internal staff, at the developer's own site. Checks: it's an early form of acceptance testing meant to catch bugs before the software ever reaches external users. Tiny example: the developers and in-house QA team at a game studio spend a week playing an unfinished build in the office and filing bugs straight into the internal tracker.

6. Beta Testing

Who: a select group of external users, in a real-world environment. Checks: gathers feedback from actual users and surfaces issues that internal testing never found. Tiny example: two weeks after the alpha round above, that same studio invites 500 volunteer players to try a near-final build at home on their own devices and submit feedback through an in-app survey.

Key idea

Each type of acceptance testing plays a vital role in verifying that the software is reliable, meets user expectations, and is ready for deployment — but each one verifies a different promise, to a different audience.

Untangling all six at once

Now that each type has its own footing, here is the comparison the exam is actually testing when it asks "which type is this?" — notice the three questions that separate them: who does the testing, where it happens, and what it's checking against.

Watch out

Alpha and Beta are often confused with UAT because all three involve "users." The difference is who the users are and where testing happens: Alpha = internal staff, at the developer's site, early. Beta = external volunteers, in their own environment, later. UAT = the actual end users/clients the software was built for, checking it against their specific business requirements — not a general bug hunt.

Topic 05

Entry and exit criteria

Clear rules for when acceptance testing is allowed to start, and clear rules for when it's allowed to be declared finished.

You wouldn't ask a customer to test-drive a car whose brakes haven't been bolted on yet, and you wouldn't hand over the keys just because the customer drove it around the block once without incident. Entry and exit criteria are the guardrails on both ends of acceptance testing: they say precisely what must be true before testing can begin, and precisely what must be true before the software can be declared done and ready to ship.

Entry criteria vs exit criteria
Entry criteria (testing may begin when...)Exit criteria (testing is complete when...)
Requirements are fully documented and analyzed.All test cases have been completed, with results recorded.
A detailed test plan has been formally approved.All discovered defects have been documented and reported.
The testing environment has been properly prepared.All critical defects have been fixed.
All test cases have been finalized and approved.The software meets all set acceptance criteria.
The required test data is ready for use.The software is confirmed ready for deployment by stakeholders.
Key idea

Under these criteria, testing can only begin once the groundwork (requirements, plan, environment, test cases, data) is in place — and it can only be considered complete once every test case is finished, defects are handled, and stakeholders sign off. Both ends have to hold, not just one.

Watch out

"All defects fixed" is not an exit criterion — that would make almost no software ever ship. The actual rule is narrower and worth quoting precisely: all critical defects need to be fixed, and non-critical ones simply need to be documented and reported. Mixing these two up is a classic exam-trap phrasing.

Topic 06

How to write an acceptance test — five steps

Writing an acceptance test is a systematic approach to ensure the software meets the specified requirements and is ready for deployment.

The five-step staircase
  1. Requirement Analysis — understand the business objectives and user expectations; review requirement documents, use cases, and flow diagrams; identify the critical functionalities that need validation.
  2. Write the Test Plan — set the overall acceptance testing strategy; define specific objectives, scope, and resources required; set testing schedules and deadlines.
  3. Design the Test Case — give each test case a unique identifier (Test Case ID); a brief description (Test Title); the specific steps to execute (Test Procedure); and the expected outcome based on the requirements.
  4. Execute the Test — run each test case according to its predefined steps; record results meticulously, noting any discrepancies between expected and actual outcomes; identify and document any defects or issues encountered.
  5. Confirm Desired Results — check that all acceptance criteria have been met, that any identified defects have been addressed, and that the software is free of critical bugs and ready for deployment.

Notice this staircase is really the same logic as the process pyramid in Topic 03, just zoomed in to the level of a single test: you can't design a sensible test case (step 3) without first knowing the requirement it's meant to validate (step 1) and having a plan that scopes it (step 2); and you can't confirm results (step 5) without having actually executed and recorded them (step 4).

Key idea

Every well-formed acceptance test case needs four things: an ID, a title, a procedure (steps), and an expected result. If a test case is missing any of these four, it isn't finished yet.

Topic 07

Worked example: UAT for a password reset feature

The faculty's own end-to-end example — trace it from user story to executed test cases.

Scenario: imagine a web application that lets users manage their accounts. One essential feature is the ability to reset a forgotten password. The acceptance test will verify that this feature meets user expectations and functions correctly.

User story

As a registered user, I want to be able to reset my password so that I can regain access to my account.

Acceptance criteria

These are the conditions the finished feature must satisfy — notice each one is small, testable, and observable:

  1. The "Forgot Password" link is clearly visible on the login page.
  2. Clicking the link takes the user to a password reset form.
  3. The password reset form requires the user to enter their registered email address.
  4. Upon submission, the user receives a confirmation email with instructions on how to reset their password.
  5. The user can successfully set a new password and log in with it.

Test cases derived from those criteria

Notice how each row below traces back to one (or more) of the five acceptance criteria above — this is exactly the "Design the Test Case" step from Topic 06, applied for real.

Password reset — UAT test cases
Test CaseStepsExpected Result
Verify "Forgot Password" Link Visibility Navigate to the login page and check the visibility of the "Forgot Password" link. The link should be visible and clickable.
Navigate to the Password Reset Form Click on the "Forgot Password" link. The user should be redirected to the password reset form.
Submit Password Reset Request Enter a registered email address in the password reset form and submit the form. A confirmation message appears, and an email is sent to the provided address.
Reset Password Functionality Click on the link in the confirmation email, enter a new password, and submit it. The user can log in with the new password.

Execution of the tests

During UAT, testers execute these test cases step by step, documenting any differences between expected and actual results. If issues arise — for example, links that don't work or emails that never arrive — they get reported for resolution, exactly as the "Recording" phase of the process pyramid describes.

Watch out

Notice this whole example is explicitly labelled UAT — it's end users confirming the feature matches their needs, not operations confirming the server survives a crash. If the exam swapped this scenario to "IT checks that the password-reset emails still get sent after a failover to the backup mail server," that would be OAT instead, even though it's the same feature.

Topic 08

Benefits, challenges, and best practices

Why bother, what tends to go wrong, and how experienced teams avoid it.

Benefits of acceptance testing

  • Quality Assurance — confirms software quality and reliability.
  • Risk Mitigation — reduces the risk of critical issues surfacing after deployment.
  • User Satisfaction — ensures the software is user-friendly and meets real needs.
  • Compliance and Standards — meets regulatory and industry standards.
  • Stakeholder Confidence — builds trust that the software is genuinely ready.

Challenges with acceptance testing

  • Changing Requirements — evolving needs require constant test updates.
  • Uncertain Requirements — ambiguous needs hinder accurate test creation.
  • User Involvement — limited user engagement affects feedback quality.
  • Environment Setup — inconsistent environments lead to inaccurate results.
  • Resource Constraints — limited resources delay testing and the project timeline.
  • Test Coverage — incomplete validation leaves issues undetected.
  • Performance Under Load — it's difficult to simulate peak loads, risking performance surprises.
  • Defect Management — cumbersome defect resolution delays deployment.

Acceptance testing best practices

  • Clear Requirements — ensures the software meets stakeholder needs and forms the basis for accurate tests.
  • Comprehensive Test Plan — outlines scope, objectives, and resources for accurate test results.
  • Effective Communication — maintains open dialogue between teams and stakeholders for prompt issue resolution.
  • User Training & Support — enables end users to participate effectively and provide valuable feedback.
  • Iterative Testing — allows for continuous feedback and improvement throughout the testing process.
Key idea

Final takeaway from the lecture: acceptance testing is a key SDLC phase that ensures the system meets requirements and real-world needs, helps detect issues early (reducing deployment risk), involves end users directly (improving satisfaction and usability), and builds stakeholder confidence in the product's market readiness.

Question 3 · medium · compare/contrast

Compare and contrast User Acceptance Testing (UAT) and Operational Acceptance Testing (OAT). Use a concrete example for each in your answer.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from This is the headline compare/contrast pair from Topic 02. The examiner wants you to connect who performs the test to what question it answers — the two ideas that actually distinguish UAT from OAT, rather than a vague "they're both acceptance testing."

Similarities: both are forms of acceptance testing, both happen after system testing and before full production release, and both exist to build confidence that the software is ready to go live.

Differences: UAT is performed by the end users or clients and checks whether the software meets their functional and usability needs in real-world scenarios — for example, a branch manager confirming the new loan-approval screen matches how their team actually approves loans. In short: UAT protects the user's experience of the feature; OAT protects the system's survival in production.

Question 9 · medium · write the test cases

A web application has this user story: "As a registered user, I want to log into my account with my email and password so that I can access my dashboard." Its acceptance criteria are:

  1. The login form (email + password fields) is visible on the homepage.
  2. Entering a valid, registered email and password and clicking "Log In" redirects the user to their dashboard.
  3. Entering an incorrect password shows a generic error message, without revealing whether the email itself exists.
  4. After 5 consecutive failed attempts, the account is temporarily locked and the user is notified.

Following the same method as the password-reset example, design a set of acceptance test cases for this feature (Test Case / Steps / Expected Result).

Show worked solution

Following "Design the Test Case" (Topic 06): each acceptance criterion above becomes one test case, with a clear procedure and an expected result that mirrors the criterion's wording.

  1. Verify Login Form Visibility

    Steps: navigate to the homepage and check that the email and password fields, plus a "Log In" button, are visible. Expected result: the login form displays correctly and is usable. (Traces to criterion 1.)

  2. Successful Login With Valid Credentials

    Steps: enter a valid, registered email and its correct password, then click "Log In." Expected result: the user is redirected to their dashboard. (Traces to criterion 2.)

  3. Login Attempt With Incorrect Password

    Steps: enter a registered email with a deliberately wrong password, then click "Log In." Expected result: a generic error message appears (e.g. "Invalid email or password") — it must not reveal whether the email is registered. (Traces to criterion 3.)

  4. Account Lockout After Repeated Failures

    Steps: attempt to log in with the correct email and an incorrect password five times in a row. Expected result: after the 5th failed attempt, the account is temporarily locked and the user receives a notification about the lockout. (Traces to criterion 4.)

Answer

Four test cases, one per acceptance criterion — exactly the pattern the faculty's Password Reset example uses. Sanity check: every criterion in the story is covered by exactly one test case, and no test case tests two criteria at once.

Question 10 · hard · write the test cases

User story: "As a shopper, I want to complete checkout using my saved card so that I can finish my purchase." Acceptance criteria:

  1. The cart summary correctly shows items, quantities, and total price before checkout begins.
  2. Clicking "Proceed to Checkout" lets the user select a saved payment method or add a new one.
  3. Submitting a valid payment method processes the order and displays an order confirmation number.
  4. If the payment is declined, the user sees a clear error message and can retry without losing the items in their cart.

Write the acceptance test cases for this feature.

Show worked solution

  1. Verify Cart Summary Accuracy

    Steps: add several items with different quantities to the cart, then open the cart summary before checkout. Expected result: the items, quantities, and total price displayed match what was actually added. (Traces to criterion 1.)

  2. Select a Payment Method at Checkout

    Steps: click "Proceed to Checkout" from a populated cart. Expected result: the user is shown their saved payment methods and given the option to add a new one. (Traces to criterion 2.)

  3. Successful Order Submission

    Steps: select a valid saved card and submit the order. Expected result: the order is processed and an order confirmation number is displayed to the user. (Traces to criterion 3.)

  4. Handle a Declined Payment

    Steps: submit the order using a card known to be declined (e.g. a test card with insufficient funds). Expected result: a clear error message is shown, and the cart contents remain intact so the user can select a different payment method and retry. (Traces to criterion 4.)

Answer

Four test cases, each tracing to one criterion. Sanity check: the "declined payment" case is the one students most often forget — acceptance criteria describing an error path (criterion 4) need their own test case just as much as the happy path does.

Question 12 · stretch · long answer

A fintech company is rolling out a new online payment gateway that must integrate with several partner banks. Design an acceptance-testing strategy for this rollout: identify which of the six types of acceptance testing would apply, in what order you'd run them, and map the overall effort onto the five-step process pyramid from Topic 03.

Show model answer
What this tests & where it comes from This stretch question deliberately combines two ideas from different parts of the lecture — the six types (Topic 04) and the five-step process pyramid (Topic 03) — to check you can use them together on a scenario, rather than only recognise each in isolation.

Which types, and in what order:

  1. Alpha Testing first — the payment company's own developers and QA staff exercise the gateway in-house to catch obvious bugs before anyone outside the company sees it.
  2. Regulation Acceptance Testing next — because this is a financial product, compliance reviewers must confirm it meets financial-data and payment-processing regulations before real transactions are ever risked, even in a limited trial.
  3. Contract Acceptance Testing in parallel — since the gateway integrates with partner banks under formal agreements, each integration is checked against the specifications in that partner's contract.
  4. Beta Testing — a small group of real external merchants processes real (small-value) transactions in production-like conditions, surfacing issues internal testing missed.
  5. UAT — the actual end users (merchants' finance teams) confirm the gateway's dashboard, reconciliation reports, and refund flow meet their day-to-day needs.
  6. OAT last, right before full launch — operations confirms the gateway can be run in production: failover if a partner bank's API goes down, backup of transaction logs, and performance under peak transaction volume.

Mapped onto the five-step pyramid: all six rounds above sit inside the middle three layers. Planning happens once, up front, defining acceptance criteria and test cases for every type listed. Each round of testing above is one pass through Testing → Recording → Comparing — execute that type's test cases, log the outcomes, and check them against that type's specific criteria (contract clauses, regulations, user needs, or production readiness, depending on the type). Only once every type has cleared its own comparison does the process reach the top of the pyramid, Result Evaluation, where the overall go/no-go decision for launching the payment gateway is made.
